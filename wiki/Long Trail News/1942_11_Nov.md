FOR FAMILY FUN
By CORNELIA M. WILBUR

SPEAKING from a personal angle, I'm not entirely sorry about this business of having to let the family car settle back on its haunches for a good long rest. It's as good a time as any for folks to learn what a lot of fun there is in walking. I've been trying to tell people for a long time that hiking the Long Trail is one form of outdoor recreation that the whole family can do together, old and young, and up to now the ones who have listened have been those fortunate enough to have made the same discovery.

In the first place, there's nothing competitive about it—you can set a pace that suits everybody in the family, choose your own

point of the Trail nearest our home, by horse and buggy. Oh, yes,
there were cars! But we weren't in any hurry, and we got there
and back all right. The whole family took that kind of vacation
every summer, and loved it.
   The neighbors used to look wonderingly and a little pityingly
at the strange creatures who liked to get into old clothes, pack
things into knapsacks to carry on their backs, and go out into the
mountains for a week or ten days of camping and hiking, instead of
going where they could be "comfortable." Poor things! The neigh-
bors, I mean.
   They never could imagine the thrills there were in padding
single file along the Trail, Dad in the lead to point out things we

mustn't miss seeing baby partridges, the different flowers that grow in hidden spots, songbirds that stay away from crowded places. They never knew the joy of leaving clocks behind, of forgetting that time means anything but time to eat, when you're hungry, or to sleep, when you're sleepy; of setting out to climb to the top of a mountain in order to marvel at what lies below, instead of rushing somewhere just to start for somewhere else; of sitting in front of camp as it grows darker, in the sleepy, fragrant quiet of the woods, waiting for the flash of white that would mean a deer had come to investigate; of making jokes over makeshift dishpans and nocturnal prickly visitors; of sitting out under the stars and singing, all of you together—all pleasures that anyone could have.
   Try it, as a family unit, and see if you don't have more fun than you ever had in your lives, and if you don't like yourselves and each other more than you ever did before, because you're doing something together. You'll get tired, of course, and you'll get soaking wet, lots of times. You'll get over it, and I'll bet you'll never catch cold—we never did. Even if you have to make yourself be a good sport the first time, you'll find it's no effort the second time. The particular appeal of the Trail is as strong for old as for young, and if you haven't already discovered it, it's easy to find out.

MY MACHETE
By WALDO C. HOLDEN

AFTER an all-too-short vacation in Vermont I have put away my machete, freshly sharpened for another day, to return to my duties with one of the national war agencies.

My machete is a wonderful little instrument. On the Trail it is fully competent to deal adequately with anything from a Sensitive Fern to a stout limb, while at a shelter it serves varied purposes from cutting firewood to peeling and slicing tomatoes. The machete must be sharp, and as such it is a dangerous weapon. Its usefulness on the Trail depends very much on how it is handled. The three important points to know are the portions of the blade which are to be used for different work, the angle of the blow, and the strength applied. Although one of the chief claims to fame for machetes generally has been in harvesting sugar cane, they have been used increasingly by surveyors for cutting out brush. The fact that they now have become established in Vermont was clearly shown when two young men recently purchased them at the local hardware store to take back to New York as souvenirs of the state.

My vacation has given me some opportunity to see some of the effects of the war upon the Trail and Trail activities. Hiking has fallen off in our section and to some extent the demands of today have interfered with Trail maintenance work. Certainly those who can get to the Trail are doing so. Labor Day week end found Fay Fuller packed, Glastenbury well filled, and Willis Ross filled to overflowing. (It must be those Worcesterites.)

As my own machete is put away lovingly for another day I note with much interest that the machete has been adopted as an instrument of war. The unwary sentinel caught in a clandestine raid must soon regret his unguarded moment if a well-directed machete intervenes.

The usefulness of the machete on the Trail and at war brings to mind the other respects in which the GMC is tying in with the War program. Last winter some of our members on skis and snowshoes were scouting the local terrain, cooperating informally with the program of the National Ski Patrol. More recently it was voted by the GMC to cooperate with the war program of fire prevention. A schedule of common carrier approaches to the Trail was published in the May NEWS so that many might be able to reach the Trail despite gasoline and tire rationing.

As the war continues and the demands on all of us increase, the Trail and its activities will suffer further impacts, but it will still go on from Massachusetts to the Canadian Border. Having been on the Trail will mean much to all of us and the knowledge that it is still there is bound to help. With decreased hiking and decreased maintenance the bushes and briars may do their damage. But when our war is ended my machete and I plan to be back on the Trail. Don't you?

AUTUMN HIKE

OUR Green Mountain Club sections were represented in the State club's Third Intersectional Autumn Hike, which was held this year on Mt. Mansfield. Present on September 13 were Burlington, 23 members; Killington section (Rutland), 14; St. Albans section, 4; members-at-large (Morrisville), 4. Ronald Busse, former vice-president and now member of the Army Medical Corps, represented the New York section.

The program, bettered by near perfect hiking weather, was directed by Prof. L. B. Puffer, GMC trustee and former president. The party hiked from Stevensville to the Club's scenically located Mable Taylor Butler lodge, and followed the Long Trail over the ridge of Mt. Mansfield to the Chin, highest point in the Green Mountain Range—elevation 4393 ft. The return trip to Butler Lodge was made by way of Taft Lodge and the Cliff Trail. A side trip ended at the Cave of Winds.

Pride of the Trail, little five-year-old Freddy Mould of Morrisville, rollicked over Mansfield with the "big fellers" and gave everybody the big hand.

Anticipating the get-together, Prof. R. O. Buchanan, trail chairman, checked the trail in advance and the group found trail and lodges in fine condition.

A group of Burlingtonians and a group from the Rutland section spent Saturday night at Butler Lodge. Members coming by train were over-night guests at the homes of the Burlington clubsters.

Outing Chairman Lee Brown was pleased with the turnout, considering the gas and tire situation, and avers that many are again finding it fun to use the trains.

A SOLDIER'S DREAM COMES TRUE

Having been away from the Green Mountains of Vermont and unable to do any mountain climbing for a number of months now, I have often thought and perhaps "dreamed" of mountain climbing.

My dream came true recently when I was granted a furlough and again visited my old stamping ground, Mt. Mansfield. My first impression was that there are not many hikers on the trails these days, judging from the overgrown conditions of some of the trails. My most lasting impression was that mountain climbing is an activity much different from army drill and training hikes.

Perhaps I overdid a little in taking Cowles cut-off trail, via Profanity, to Taft Lodge and in repeating the trip the very next day with a light overnight pack. Labor Day week-end weather was so attractive that a lover of the trail could not stay in town. Our party found Caretaker "Sandy Austin" on duty (although it is rumored that he spent considerable time in Underhill) and Taft Lodge in fine condition.

When I am out on my next weekly conditioning hike I shall think of our Green Mountain Club trails and wish I was back in Vermont hiking with the GMC.

To those who are fortunate enough to be able to remain at their regular jobs and get out on the trail once in a while, in spite of all restrictions—let's keep the spirit of the GMC alive and make up for all our club shortcomings after the duration.

KENNETH B. FISHER, President of the Burlington Section. Currently Staff Sergeant, Finance Office, Dow Field, Bangor, Maine.

ALL TOGETHER
By LARRY DEAN

EVERYBODY has or is soon to feel the pinch of this global war, and our Club and its Trail are no exceptions. Many are leaving the Trail to combat the foes to their way of life. Memberships will undoubtedly fall off as money and energies are diverted to the support of the war. Are we who remain behind going to consider the Trail and the Club part of the past?

Are bushes and nettles to choke the Trail for the lack of restraint? Are the shelters to fall into disrepair? Or will we at home keep the spirit, keep the faith, do all we can to prevent the entire blotting out of the Trail and the total disrepair of the shelters?

<!-- page: 12 -->
As Club and Section members, all of us have an opportunity to
show our loyalty and worth, even though we have little time or
money, by doing what we can to maintain the Club and its Trail.
   In the past our trail and lodge work has been done by a handful
of workers or by the Patrol. Now many of those are gone.
   The leader of the Long Trail Patrol, Prof. Roy Buchanan, kept
faith with the former Patrol members now in the service (his son
among them) by carrying on alone during his limited vacation time
this past summer. If Roy can carry on with little or no help and a
cut in funds, so can we.
   Here I am reminded of a construction boss that I once worked
for. When we had a hard lift to make he, mindful of the ways of a
group of men, would get down with us and shout at the top of his
lungs: "Lift, all together—one after another." Let us who remain
at home get down and lift, but lift all together, so that when the
boys return they can again find the Trail and lodges inviting.
                                                         KEN FISHER

DANGER Until recently the Burlington hikers had only one First Aider; Larry Dean, to look out for. Now that he has instructed a couple of First Aid classes among the section membership there is a great demand for real victims to practice on. When a hiker gets hurt up Burlington-way, he wonders whether he tripped or was pushed.

POEM A hiker submits the following lines as expressive of the thrills which lure men and women over the rocky trails. He doesn't know the name of the poem or its author but judges that "he must have been a 'rich' man of the trail, rich in the experiences and love of the outdoors .."

Transfused through you, oh Mountain Friends,
With you my solemn spirit blends,
And life no more has separate ends.

I read each misty mountain sign,
I know the voice of wave and pine.
I am yours and you are mine.

Life's burdens fall, its discords cease,
I am at rest in the release
Of Nature's own exceeding peace.
