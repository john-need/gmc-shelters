   long time that hiking the Long Trail is one form of outdoor recrea-
   tion that the whole family can do together, old and young , and up to
   now the ones who have listened have been those fortunate enough
   to have made the same discovery.

you ever had in your lives, and if you don't like yourselves and each
other more than you ever did before, because you' re doing some~
thing together. You'll get tired, of course, and you'll get soaking
wet, lots of times. You'll get over it, and I'll bet you'll never catch
cold- we never did. Even if you have to make yourself be a good
sport the first time , you'll find it's no effort the second time. The
particular appeal of the Trail is as strong for old as for young, and
if you haven't already discovered it, it's easy to find out.

MY MACHETE
                       By w ALDO c. HOLDEN
           an all-too-short vacation in Vermont I have put away my
A   FT.ER
      machete, freshly sharpened for another day, to return to my
duties with one of the national war agencies .
   My machete is a wonderful little instrument. On the Trail it is
fully competent to deal adequately with anything from a Sensitive
Fern to a stout limb, while at a shelter it serves varied purposes
from cutting firewood to peeling and slicing tomatoes. The machete
must be sharp , and as such it is a dangerous weapon. Its usefulness
on the Trail depends very much on how it is handled. The three
important points to know are the portions of the blade which are
to be used for different work, the angle of the blow, and the strength
applied. Although one of the chief claims to fame for machetes
generally has been in harvesting sugar cane, they have been used
increasingly by surveyors for cutting out brush. The fact that they
now have become established in Vermont was clearly shown when
two young men recently purchased them at the local hardware
store to take back to New York as sourvenirs of the state.
    My vacation has given me some opportunity to see some of the
effects of the war upon the Trail and Trail activities. Hiking has
fallen off in our section and to some extent the demands of today
have interfered with Trail maintenance work. Certainly those
who can get to the Trail are doing so. Labor Day week end found
Fay Fuller packed , Glastenbury well filled, and Willis Ross filled
 to overflowing. (It must be those Worcesterites.)
    As my own machete is put away lovingly for another day I note
with much interest that the machete has been adopted as an instru-
ment of war. The unwary sentinel caught in a clandestine raid
 must soon regret his unguarded moment if a well-directed machete
 intervenes .

   The usefulness of the machete on the Trail and at war brings to
mind the other respects in which the GMC is tying in with the
War program. Last winter some of our members on skis and snow~
shoes were scouting the local terrain, cooperating informally with
the program of the National Ski Patrol. More recently it was voted
by the GMC to cooperate with the war program of fire prevention.
A schedule of common carrier approaches to the Trail was pub~
lished in the May NEws so that many might be able to reach the
Trail despite gasoline and tire rationing.
   As the war continues and the demands on all of us increase, the
Trail and its activities will suffer further impacts , but it will still
go on from Massachusetts to the Canadian Border. Having been on
the Trail will mean much to all of us and the knowledge that it is
still there is bound to help. With decreased hiking and decreased
maintenance the bushes and .briars may do their damage. But when
our war is ended my machete and I plan to be back on the Trail.
Don't you?

AUTUMN HIKE
                               OUR   Green Mountain Club sections
                           F  were represented in the State club's
                           Third lntersectional Autumn Hike,
                             which was held this year on Mt. Mans-
                             field . Present on September i 3 were
                             Burlington, 23 members; Killington·
                             section (Rutland), i4; St. Albans sec-
                             tion, 4; members-at-large (Morrisville),
                             + Ronald Busse, former vice-president
                             and now member of the Army Medical
Corps , represented the New York section.
   The program, bettered by near perfect hiking weather , was
directed by Prof. L. B. Puffer, GMC trustee and former president.
The party hiked from Stevensville to the Club's scenically located
Mable Taylor Butler lodge, and followed the Long Trail over the
ridge of Mt. Mansfield to the Chin, highest point in the Green
Mountain Range- elevation 4393 ft. The return trip to Butler
Lodge was made by way of Taft Lodge and the Cliff Trail. A side         ~
trip ended at the Cave of Winds .
   Pride of the Trail, little five-year-old Freddy Mould of Morris-
ville, rollicked over Mansfield with the "big fellers" and gave
everybody the big hand.
   Anticipating the get-together, Prof. R . 0 . Buchanan, trail
chairman, checked the trail in advance and the group found trail        (11
and lodges in fine condition.
   A group of Burlingtonions and a group from the Rutland section
spent Saturday night at Butler Lodge. Members coming by train
were over-night guests at the homes of the Burlington clubsters.
   Outing Chairman Lee Brown was pleased with the turnout ,
considering the gas and tire situation, and avers that many are
again finding it fun to use the trains .

A SOLDIER'S DREAM COMES
          TRUE
    AVING   been away from the Green Mountains of Vermont and
H   unable to do any mountain climbing for a number of months
now, I have often thought and perhaps "dreamed" of mountain
climbing.
   My dream came true recently when I was granted a furlough
and again visited my old stamping ground, Mt. Mansfield . My first
impression was that there are not many hikers on the trails these
days , judging from the overgrown conditions of some of the trails.
My most lasting impression was that mountain climbing is an
activity much different from army drill and training hikes .
   Perhaps I overdid a little in taking Cowles cut-off trail, via
Profanity, to Taft Lodge and in repeating the trip the very next
day with a light overnight pack. Labor Day week-end weather
was so attractive that a lover of the trail could not stay in town. Our
party found Caretaker " Sandy Austin" on duty (although it is
rumored that he spent considerable time in Underhill) and Taft
Lodge in fine condition.
   When I am out on my next weekly conditioning hike I shall
think of our Green Mountain Club trails and wish I was back in
Vermont hiking with the GMC .
   To those who are fortunate enough to be able to remain at their
regular jobs and get out on the trail once in a while, in spite of all
restrictions- let's keep the spirit of the GMC alive and make up
for all our club shortcomings after the duration.
                                K ENNETH B. F1sHER , President of the
                                  Burlington Section . Currently- Staff
                                  Sergeant , Finance Office , Dow Field ,
                                  Bangor, Maine.

ALL TOGETHER
                           By LARRY D EAN
   VERYBODY has or is soon to feel the pinch of this global war , and
E   our Club and its Trail are no exceptions . Many are leaving the
Trail to combat the foes to their way of life. Memberships will
undoubtedly fall off as money and energies are diverted to the sup~
port of the war. Are we who remain behind going to consider the
Trail and the Club part of the past?
  Are bushes and nettles to choke the Trail for the lack of restraint?
Are the shelters to fall into disrepair? Orwill we at home keep the
spirit , keep the faith , do all we can to prevent the entire blotting
out of the Trail and the total disrepair of the shelters?

<!-- page: 12 -->
As Club and Section members, all of us have an opportunity to
show our loyalty and worth, even though we have little time or
money, by doing what we can to maintain the Club and its Trail.
   In the past our trail and lodge work has been done by a handful
of workers or by the Patrol. Now many of those are gone.
   The leader of the Long Trail Patrol, Prof. Roy Buchanan, kept
faith with the former Patrol members now in the service (his son
among them) by carrying on alone during his limited vacation time
this past summer. If Roy can carry on with little or no help and a
cut in funds, so can we.
   Here I am reminded of a construction boss that I once worked
for. When we had a hard lift to make he, mindful of the ways of a
group of men, would get down with us and shout at the top of his
lungs : "Lift, all together- one after another." Let us who remain
at home get down and lift, but lift all together, so that when the
boys return they can again find the Trail and lodges inviting.

POEM A hiker submits the following lines as expressive of the thrills which
lure men and women over the rocky trails. He doesn't know the name of the
poem or its author but judges that "he must have been a 'rich' man of the trail,
rich in the experiences and love of the outdoors .."

                Transfused through you, oh Mountain Friends,
                  With you my solemn spirit blends,
                  And life no more has separate ends .

                I read each misty mountain sign,
                   I know the voice of wave and pine.
                   I am yours and you are mine.

                Life's burdens fall, its discords cease,
                   I am at rest in the release
                   Of Nature's own exceeding peace.
