The Certificate was signed by the Chief, U.S. Forest Service. President James took this opportunity to observe that the excellent relationship of the GMC with the Forest Service, making this award possible, is largely due to Joe Sposta. Members concurred, with applause.

Once again, though, Joe had bad news to go with the good news of the award. Programs in his area, affecting the Long Trail in the National Forest, have been subjected to severe funding cuts. Agencies are being combined and realigned, with the result being uncertainty about what resources the Forest Service will have to carry out programs that directly or indirectly support the Club's objectives in maintaining the Long Trail.

Sposta also noted that the Green Mountain National Forest will celebrate its fiftieth anniversary next year. A history of the GMNF is to be published, and other activities are being considered. He is in hopes that the GMC will help and be part of the National Forest's celebration.

Executive Director Reports

Executive Director Harry Peet stated that his major objective was to carry out the Club's mandate within the constraint of the reduced budget. A start on this was the reduction of the Club's full time staff from three persons to two. A part-time secretary has been added; she is Maureen Doyle, and began working in June. The Field Supervisor job will not be filled.

Peet also reported that two grants have been received from the Appalachian Trail Conference, and will be put to good use. One was used for the purchase of tools, which will be made available to the sections and the LTP for their trail maintenance work. The other grant will be used to produce a slide show and synchronized audio tape on the subject of proper trail maintenance procedures. This show is designed for the GMC sections, as well as ATC and other interested users. The ATC was quite impressed with the whole idea and supported the production enthusiastically.

An ATC representative attending the Annual Meeting, Roger Stemburgh, confirmed this and emphasized some other matters of interest to GMC'ers. He stated that even though it looks like the federal government's support of wilderness programs will be reduced, spending is still possible within budgetary guidelines and from funds not derived from taxes, such as offshore oil lease proceeds. It is important to get what monies are available spent on projects of benefit to the GMC and the Long Trail. The most effective way of doing this is by communicating your desires to your elected representatives in Congress.

Reinforcing some of Stemburgh's comments, At-Large Director Ellen Reiss delivered some comment on the current mood in Washington, from her vantage point as an aide to Interior Secretary James Watt. The message was one of caution, as the extent and direction of the impact on GMC-related activities that will result from Administration actions are not yet fully discernible.

Taken altogether, the officers reports and the ensuing discussion all contained a good deal of this caution and uncertainty about the impact of government actions on Long Trail-related activities and Club income and expense.

Only the Burlington Section grew enough to gain a new director; a report distributed at the meeting shows it to be the largest section, with 422 members.

New LTP Truck Succeeds Two Long-Term Veterans

By Robert L. Hagerman

"The new truck of the patrol service, the gift of President [Mortimer R.] Proctor, was in evidence at the trustees' meeting. It is adorned with painted arrows, is labelled 'Long Trail Patrol No. 1', and is equipped with a mattress, so that it can be used as a bedroom at night."

So read an item in the June, 1931 Long Trail News about the first truck to serve the GMC's Long Trail Patrol. This year the Club purchased a new truck, a 1980 General Motors pick-up (see the August LTN). It is only the third truck in nearly 50 years of Long Trail Patrol operations. A little history about the first two trucks thus seems appropriate at this time.

That first one was a new 1931 Chevrolet pick-up. That was the year, of course, that the Long Trail Patrol was established under what became the long-term sprightly leadership of the late Professor Roy O. Buchanan. Ted Goddard of Springfield, who worked on the LTP from 1955 to 1960, provided the Long Trail News with these reminiscences about both the first and second trucks:

"The first year [1955] we were still using the 1931 Chevy. That was some truck. It would go just about anywhere. However, getting there could be interesting. It had mechanical brakes that required much foot pressure and praying to bring it to a stop. Luckily, it did not go over 35 downhill with a tail wind. Neither side window existed and the windshield, which was on hinges to be opened, would not clamp shut. It had tires that were removed from the rim to change and by the end of the season we had no more casing. In fact, we drove back to Rutland at the end of the season with a truck boot wrapped around the tube and stuffed in the casing to cover a split. I think that was the reason we finally gave up on the truck. . . . Another interesting note was the roof. It was a wooden roof that [extended over] the cab and body [and was] covered with asphalt shingles [put on] by Roy Buchanan.*

"For the next year my father [Theodore N. (Ted) Goddard] loaned his 1948 International truck to the Patrol with the understanding that GMC pay the registration and insurance costs. This is the way it was handled for the next several years. I am not sure if he ever formally donated it to the club or not. All I know is the club took over operation and maintenance of the truck [and see below]. . . ."

Serving the same rugged duty as the first truck over back- and woods-roads, the 1948 International KB-2 pick-up has been kept in operating condition largely through the dedicated and extended (since about 1967) volunteer effort of Roderick Rice of Burlington. He recalls that some years ago the Club did get a formal transfer of title from Goddard ("for just $1.00!"). Some time in the late 1960's the truck underwent a major motor job and about four years ago received two new pistons and rings. Rice had found these by a good stroke of luck with a dealer of International trucks in New York State who happened to have some of the by-now-very-hard-to-get parts for the 1948 truck. The truck, which is basically "elderly red," has a different-colored door on the passenger side. That's a replacement for the original which got "stoved in" 15 or 20 years ago. Rice scrounged the replacement from a KB-5 International truck which he found along the side of a road where a farmer had junked it.

WE'RE EASY TO GET TO:

The Green Mountain Club will hold its fourth Autumn Outing for members at Mountain Meadows Campground in Lincoln, Vt. on Friday-Sunday, September 18-20. This year the event will be hosted by the Burlington Section. The campground is located on the west side of Lincoln Mountain about midway between the Jerusalem and Battell Trails. The season normally closes Labor Day, but once again Lum and Marylin Melendy, the owners, have agreed to reopen this weekend for the exclusive use of the GMC.

Most of the 52 spacious campsites offer a spectacular view of Lincoln Mountain. Each has electricity, a fireplace, picnic table and running water. Modern lavatories with hot showers are nearby, and a dumping station for camper units is available. A brook runs along the back of the campground, and there is a pond for swimming and fishing, a recreation hall, and playground facilities for children.

The event's chairperson, Dot Myer, has announced that attendance will be by reservation only. So complete the form below and return it to Club headquarters by September 4. Upon arrival September 18 or 19 each holder of a reservation will be assigned the campsite of his or her choice from those not yet assigned. There are modest charges for hot showers (take quarters) and for firewood. No pets will be allowed. To simplify arrangements, there will be no catered meals. Grocery stores are within three miles. For further information contact Dot Myer by mail at 7 Patrick Street, South Burlington, Vt. 05401, or by telephone 7 to 9 p.m. at 802-863-2433.

Participants may arrive as early as noon Friday and remain through Sunday afternoon. IMPORTANT: Those arriving Friday are strongly urged to do so by 11 p.m., when the camp gate will be locked for security. For those arriving no more than an hour after that time, someone will be on duty to reopen the gate. Indicate your expected time of arrival on the reservation form, if that is your case. If an unexpected delay en route will result in a post-11 p.m. arrival, call 802-453-3982 before that hour.

A variety of hikes will be offered on Saturday and Sunday. Two of these will be a hike up the Sunset Ridge Trail on Mount Mansfield, and a hike on Snake Mountain in Addison. There will also be an opportunity to visit the Audubon Nature Center in Huntington. The Saturday evening program will feature a slide show of this summer's GMC expedition to Yellowstone National Park. So come one and all for a beautiful and invigorating fall weekend.

Axes, Machetes, Bugs And Pumpernickel Mark Last Ten Miles Of LT

By Robert L. Hagerman

INTRODUCTORY NOTE: As noted in recent LTN's, this year marks the 50th anniversary of the completion of the Long Trail, the 260-plus-mile "Footpath in the Wilderness" which has become so well known to so many hikers. That completion came the summer of 1930 when two University of Vermont faculty members cleared the last ten or so miles of LT from Jay Peak to Milepost 592 on the Canadian boundary (formal opening to the hiking public followed in the spring of 1931). They were Dr. Charles G. Doll, then of the University's geology department, and Phillips D. Carleton, an instructor of English.

The two were engaged for the job on a salaried basis (not all the original clearing of the LT was done by volunteers). They followed a route which Professor Roy O. Buchanan and his brother, Bruce, had marked out earlier that year. Doll and Carleton worked throughout the summer, completing the job in early September. It should be noted here that two other University of Vermont men briefly assisted in the project. They were William (Bill) Behney and Joseph (Joe) Elder. And in 1931 Doll and Carleton returned to the wilds to build the Laura Woodward Shelter on the north side of Jay Peak (the structure burned in 1955 and was replaced by the present shelter there).

We're pleased to report that one of the two men is still very much of this world. He is Dr. Doll of Essex Junction, now-retired longtime state geologist for Vermont, who will be 83 on Aug. 22. Sadly, Carleton, a resident of Charlotte, died on Jan. 7 this year. Back in 1930 the two men were relative newcomers to Vermont. Both had resided in Providence, R.I. and were graduates of Brown University (Carleton in 1920, Doll in 1924). Thus on Oct. 11, 1931 The Providence Sunday Journal Magazine carried a long feature about the two former Rhode Islanders and their work in clearing the Long Trail and building the new shelter.

Presented here are extracts of that part of the article which focuses on the former. Interspersed (in italics) are present-day comments (and one little sketch) by Doll about this long-ago experience which represents such a milestone in the history of the Long Trail.

Their "initiation" as woodsmen came on their first night out last summer. Stopping in a deserted two-story mountain house on the slopes of Jay Peak, the men had made beds of fir boughs on the floor.

The next morning they followed a direct path to the top of Jay Peak, 3861 feet above sea level.... This spot marked the starting place of the trail work which was to keep them occupied for three long months. Here, armed with light axes and machetes, they set about clearing the trail northward, following the blaze marks left for them by Prof. Buchanan weeks before.

*My recollection is that the blazes could be followed with little difficulty, excepting in places where new foliage covered them.... A few changes were made here and there because of some formidable obstacle in the path or better tramping ground.... Where short changes were made in the trail we, of course, [axe-] blazed the trees. The blaze painters followed us sometime later. Could have been Roy Buchanan's LT Patrol.*

Their work of cutting a path through the tangled virgin forests was no easy task. Trees that barred the way must be chopped down. Windfalls across the path must be removed or cut through. Frequent tangles of swamp maples made use of their axes impossible, the springy boughs rebounding like rubber from the blows of the blades.

*The forests were not "virgin"... [though I have a photo which shows a giant of virgin time, now deceased, probably too husky to tackle at the time the ridge was lumbered.]*

Rainy days made their work difficult - and there were many such days in the early summer of 1930. Days of drought followed, however, ideal days for trail work, and delays of June were made up in the late season.

At the start of their work, each night the men returned to the mountainhouse shelter. As the clearing of the trail progressed - on a good day the men would advance a half-mile or more along their route - a more conveniently located camp became a necessity.

A waterproofed cotton shelter had been brought along for such an emergency. This was pitched along the trail and fir sidewalls built around the open ends of the shelter.

The shelter was a large heavy sheet of cotton cloth (an ordinary bed sheet) which we waterproofed with a zinc oxide solution. It served us well, bouncing the heavy raindrops off its surface during those thunderstorms that came trumpeting out of the west. The sidewalls consisted of interwoven fir bows which were quickly installed when rain threatened (no radio warning, but eyes trained westward).

Out of this camp the men worked each day, frequently moving the camp as the trail advanced northward. Each camp move meant a search of surrounding territory for a site where fresh water was available. To find a constant water supply in drought days is no easy thing to do.

The barking of foxes and the noise of hooting owls often disturbed the quiet of the night, but this annoyance was nothing to that caused by insects during the day.

Before setting out for the trail the men stocked up with citronella and a "fly dope" recommended by the natives. For the first few days these were freely used, but proved of little aid in driving off the midges and black flies that swarmed from ferns and brush cut down by the workers' machetes.

"Perhaps we became immune to the insects. Or it may be we were too busy to notice them. At any rate, after a few days we 'cached' the bug lotions along the trail, lightening the loads of our packs," one of the men (Doll) told the writer.

This problem of keeping their pack loads at a minimum weight was an important one for the men, as it is for all hikers. In addition to their axes and machetes, extra woolen clothing, blankets, shelter tent and other camp equipment, they were forced to carry sufficient foodstuffs to last them for days....

*My pack was of heavy brown canvas (I still have the old standby) with a lump line for heavy loads, of which there were some, which was good neck exercise.*

To carry canned goods were out of the question. A substitute for this necessary article of diet was found in imported dried soups. A plentiful supply of these and dried fruits were packed in their equipment.

Instead of white bread, more wholesome rye "pumpernickel" was carried - large three pound and six pound loaves. One of these loaves will last a camper for several days. The trail makers made their loaves go the full time limit - and more.

During their three months in the mountains they tasted no fresh meat except while on trips to their source of supplies. Bologna and dried meats were substituted for steaks and chops.

With such a limited bill-of-fare, it is not surprising that the men emerged from the woods several pounds lighter than they had been on entering. Yet neither man showed unfavorable evidence of his loss of avoirdupois.... When they finally arrived at Post 592... both men were in perfect health, eager to return for another summer on the trail.

OTTAUQUECHEE REGIONAL LAND TRUST

The ORLT is a non-profit, tax exempt conservation corporation based out of Woodstock, Vermont. It is a membership organization, professionally staffed, which was founded in 1977 to protect productive agricultural and forestry lands and significant natural areas in Vermont. As a land trust, the ORLT seeks permanent protection of forest, agricultural or scenic lands by holding perpetual easements, conservation restrictions, or development rights placed on the land, or by owning land itself in fee and managing it directly.

A variety of techniques are used by the ORLT to obtain land or rights in land. Often land or land rights are donated to the ORLT, with the donors benefiting from savings in estate taxes, property taxes, capital gains taxes, and federal and state taxes. Where the land or land rights must be purchased, techniques like the bargain sale, purchase with limited development, use of charitable investors, and charitable remainder trust approach have been successfully used. Last year, in fact, the ORLT was able to protect over 3000 acres of land in Vermont through these means.

ORLT director Richard W. Carbin is a strong supporter of the Long and Appalachian Trails (as well as a GMC member), and has through a grant with the National Park Service supported my work with the Appalachian Trail Project Office over the past three years. In the face of sharply decreased funding for the A.T. Project, more direct involvement by the ORLT in the Trail protection process is being considered.

LT/AT Local Management Plan

With the purchase of a substantial corridor of land by the Federal government for protection of the Appalachian Trail, detailed plans for the management of the Trail are needed. To keep Trail maintenance and management decentralized and in the hands of the traditional volunteer maintainers, the National Park Service has drafted a Comprehensive Plan which serves as a broad, umbrella document containing specific Local Management Plans prepared by each of the 31 maintaining Trail clubs like the Green Mountain Club.

The Long Range Planning Committee, under the direction of Larry Van Meter (now Executive Director of the Appalachian Trail Conference), began work on our LT/AT Local Management Plan as long ago as September of 1979. After much effort by the Committee, a Local Management Plan was completed and given preliminary approval by the GMC Board of Directors on January 10, 1981. I assumed the chairmanship of the Committee in February, and since that time the GMC Plan has been embellished with a Purpose and Introduction sections, and undergone review by the Vermont Agency of Environmental Conservation, The Green Mountain National Forest and the ATC New England Regional Management Committee. We hope to have the Plan formally adopted, after minor revision, by AEC, GMNF and ATC before the end of the year.
