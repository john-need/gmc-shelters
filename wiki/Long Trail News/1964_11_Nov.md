TRUSTEES' MEETING—Oct. 10, 1964

The fall meeting of the Trustees of the Green Mountain Club was held at the Club's office in Rutland. Bob Humes, the new president, conducted the meeting.

The meeting was attended by the following trustees: Messrs. Rolston, Humes, Coleman, Rohrbaugh, Randolph, Goddard, Buchanan, Attenborough, Rice, Bartlett, Smith, Scott, Gormly, Miss Schon, Miss Hardy and Bob Deemer, for the U.S. Forest Service. Miss Hinchey, the club secretary, was also present.

A motion was made, seconded, and passed that reading of the minutes of the last meeting be omitted.

The various section reports were heard. They were of a routine nature for the most part.

Committee Reports

Annual Meeting—It was the consensus of all present that this was a very well-planned and successful meeting. Harlan Farnsworth certainly deserves much credit.

Appalachian Trail Conference—Bob Humes reported that this event was a successful venture. The affair paid for itself; in fact, showed a balance of 81¢ profit.

Conservation—The activities of this committee were reported by Bill Bartlett and Marion Hardy. The various sections of the club are asked to appoint a conservation committee man. Bill stated the difficulties encountered in communication with the club membership. He would like space in the Long Trail News. Marion Hardy reported on the meeting of the Vermont Natural Resources Council, held at Mount Ascutney.

End-to-End—Bob Attenborough reported that 12 certificates were awarded this year. This makes a grand total of 162 to date.

Guide Book—Chairman Rolston stated that the current edition is going fast. 4500 copies were printed, 2974 already gone. Sales have averaged 6 books per day for the past 16 months.

Trust Fund—Ted Goddard, Chairman. Only change since May 1st was a two-for-one split in holdings of A.T. & T. Income for the period was $1246.88 and current value of the Trust Fund is approximately $73,000.

Historical—Alan Gormly, chairman. No special report but will look over the material during the winter months.

Intersectional 1964—Fred Smith reported that the event was attended by 234 people and yielded a profit of $120.01. A motion was made and seconded that this amount be donated to the Boy Scout Council. This motion was defeated. Another motion was made, seconded, that the amount of $100.00 be given to the Council. This motion was so voted. The balance of $20.01 is to be earmarked for next year's intersectional.

1965 Intersectional—Ben Rolston said that Camp Plymouth has been contacted to use their facilities for 1965.

Publicity—John Rohrbaugh, chairman. Five new form letters have been printed and are ready for use. John has been extremely busy during the past year.

A motion was made and seconded that the Long Trail News editor may edit an 8-page paper when she has enough newsworthy material and deems it necessary. Motion passed. Clerk to notify Peggy Pons of such action.

Trail and Shelters—Roy Buchanan of the Long Trail Patrol reported on the year's activities. Eleven weeks' work on the trail, Wiley Lodge was built, trail cleared from Chilcoot Pass to Canada, Sunrise Shelter built, acrylite put in windows at Cooper Lodge, Hazens Notch and Bolton Lodge. The new relocations of the trail have all been accurately measured.

A motion passed that discussion of organizational memberships be tabled for this meeting.

There was much discussion on the signs for the Appalachian Trail. The publicity committee is to attend to these and select the design, size, etc. The Vermont Highway Department is expected to erect these signs.

John Rohrbaugh offered a motion that the By-Laws of the Club be printed in the February issue of the Long Trail News as they now exist. This motion was so voted.

Meetings adjourned at 4:10 P.M.

LLOYD WEEKS, Clerk

LONG TRAIL PATROL

The Patrol worked for 11 weeks this season. The lumber for Wiley Lodge required 60 man loads to be packed up a mile from the end of the truck road. Half of this was moved in by the New York Section trail gang that Don Wallace led. This camp was built early in July on the slope of Robbins Mt. high up in Honey Hollow.

Later in the season a new Sunrise Camp, donated by Mortimer Proctor, former President of Green Mountain Club and former Governor of Vermont, was built a mile south of Brandon Gap. This camp was built according to U.S. Forest Service plans on their land about 300 feet from the old Sunrise Camp.

During the summer, trails were cleared from Whiteface Mt. to Canada and the camps in that region were repaired where needed. Late in the season about two miles of the new trail from Gorham to Bolton Lodge were relocated so as to lose less altitude in Honey Hollow and to get farther away from civilization (whatever that is). The whole trail between Gorham and Bolton was measured. It was found that these two camps are now 14.00 miles apart rather than 8.8 miles as shown in the Guide Book. So Canada and Massachusetts have been shoved 5½ miles farther from each other. Quite a convulsion of nature!

BURLINGTON SECTION

Up The Trail A piece—

The Burlington Section is still much on the move which our Spring and Summer schedule proved, and our Fall and Winter schedule shows increased activities.

Let's see what we did these past months.

Jim Forrester was joined by sixteen for a bit of climbing in his favorite Mt. Abraham and Mt. Ellen country on July 19th.

The Burlington hikers took to the water like ducks when thirty-four put fourteen canoes and a kayak into the Missisquoi River on July 26th. Shirley Strong was the "head paddler" on that trip down the river. In the mouth area several crafty ones rigged improvised sails for a bit of canoe sailing in favorable winds.

Jim Williams led a group up to Gorham Lodge and a climb up Camel's Hump on Aug. 1-2.

Thirty-eight adults and children had a family outing at Dr. Burnett's Keeler Bay camp. The fall-like dreary day cut off all ideas of swimming but Doc had most of the gang out sailing in his sailboat. All had a "peck of fun" with the grown-ups sailing and the young-uns making use of the trampoline.

Rod Rice paced a group to Burnt Rock on Aug. 16th.

Next came the most wonderful Intersectional week sponsored by our Section with Jan and Harris Abbott at the helm. What a place to hold such a big spread—Mt. Norris Scout Reservation.

Fifty odd went to the Underhill State Park area on Sept. 13th. to climb up the west side of Mt. Mansfield or to Cantilever Rock and were blessed with ideal weather. After the hiking and climbing the group gathered at the Park for a feed of boiled corn on the cob prepared by Joan and Andy Buchanan. Was it ever good ... yum, yum!

Our final hike and climb before this writing was in the Adirondacks and it was a real healthy one. We eight hit the trail for the 8½ odd mile tramp in to Little and Big Haystack, just out from Keene Valley. We spent Saturday night, Sept. 19th, at Bushnell Falls, some sleeping in the lean-to and some under the stars.

Three of the party working on the ADK 46'ers added Saddleback and Basin Mts. to the trip. The weather was beautiful and we all had a happy but tiring trip.

Connie Roessler of the Mountain Club of Maryland was with our group on this trip.

My apologies to the Montpelier Section for not getting the news for the August issue in on time due to a misunderstanding but here it is—and more.

Our last reported hike was to Butler Lodge and the Forehead on March 21 with still-to-go on our spring schedule a sugar-on-snow party, a hike up Mt. Elmore and our Annual meeting. Well, since then we've had all those activities and many more.

I'm told the sugar-on-snow party with Doris Farnsworth as leader drew about 50 people, including Ben Rolston; I was unable to attend. Seven of us led by Dave Morse climbed Mt. Elmore on April 19 and enjoyed a wiener roast after the climb. Over 50 people attended our annual meeting and it looks as though we may have to look for bigger quarters next year. Our paid-up membership is now 45.

Re-elected president at the annual meeting was Dave Otis; treasurer Harlan Farnsworth; and secretary Marion Gorham. Fred Larson was our speaker, following the covered dish supper.

On May 3 twenty-four persons showed up for a Mystery Hike led by Frances Holmes. This hike was unscheduled and had no advance publicity except being announced at our annual meeting. Only two persons participated in our next scheduled trip on May 10 to Tuckerman Ravine; it's a rough climb carrying skis, poles, and boots but the experience is worth it—and the skiing was good.

On Sunday May 17 twenty-three persons with Ann Otis as leader participated in the short climb to Nichol's Ledge. Although not much of a hike the view is as good as one could ever hope to see. On May 25 five of us participated in the work party clearing trail from Smugglers Notch to Chilcoat Pass and making minor repairs to Sterling Pond Lodge. We re blazed the Madonna Mountain area where the ski trails cut across or follow the Long Trail.

Nine of our members attended the GMC, Inc. Annual Meeting at the Long Trail Lodge on May 30.

On Sunday, June 14 five of us hiked the 9.3 mile section of the Long Trail between Jay-Richford and Jay-Montgomery Center highways, which was led by Dave Otis. On June 27 four persons led by Dave Morse hiked from the Ithiel Falls Campground to Prospect Rock and Barrows Camp.

Twenty-six enjoyed a chicken barbecue and an afternoon of boating, swimming and water skiing at the Desilets Camp at Groton Pond on Sunday July 12. Juleia Desilets was our hostess and Jerry Dix the chef.

NEW YORK SECTION

Lillian Lodge was President of the New York Section. A check for $5 was received from member William O. Wiley. In Lil's usual nice way, a thank-you note was sent. Back came a pleased reply, enclosing another contribution of $25. This called for a phone call from Lil. Soon another check for $100 appeared. This called for a visit from Lil. Out of this inspired interview came the wonderful idea of building and endowing Wiley Lodge.

Mile 0 of the Long Trail is at the Massachusetts-Vermont state line. Mile 255.3 is at the Canadian Border. At Mile 170.44 Wiley Lodge was built. This is between the summit of Camel's Hump and the old Winooski River crossing.

To quote the Long Trail Guide Book, 17th ed.: "The construction of the Lodge in 1949 by the Long Trail Patrol was made possible by a generous gift from William O. Wiley of the New York Section, who has also contributed to a fund for its perpetual maintenance. Especially well built, using log siding, it is one of the Trail's best structures. There are bunks for about 12, and adjacent Gleason Brook furnishes water for drinking and swimming."

In 1963, because the Winooski River crossing became no longer feasible, the Trail was re-located to hit the Jonesville Bridge directly. The new routing left Wiley Lodge off the Long Trail.

The design of most of the cabin camps on the Long Trail is Professor Roy Buchanan's design. An extra nice example of Roy's cabins was built as a GMC exhibit at the Girl Scout encampment at Button Bay in 1962. Since the Burlington Section had built the cabin, the cabin was given to the Burlington Section after the encampment was over. The Burlington Section did not need it right then so they dismantled and stored it. Then a most satisfactory swap was made. The New York Section gave Wiley Lodge to the Burlington Section. They will maintain it in the future and continue to use it. In return the Burlington Section gave the New York Section the cabin from Button Bay that they had in storage.

This last summer the Long Trail Patrol and Don Wallace's New York trail crew backpacked the Button Bay Lodge a distance of more than a mile to a location on the re-located Trail. The Patrol finished building the Lodge on July 3. It is the cabin now known as Wiley Lodge and the Wiley Lodge Fund will maintain it perpetually.

In July our President, Bob Humes, wrote: "We saw the newly completed Wiley Camp, which is in a very nice location in the lee of a huge granite ledge to the west. A very nice spring is close by the camp. Roy B. and the trail crew did a very commendable job." New York Section President John Nuffort wrote in September: "We saw the new Wiley over Labor Day and found it a picture camp."

The cost of building the original Wiley Lodge (now re-named Buchanan Lodge) was $600. Wiley gave $2500. The remainder drawing interest in savings bank has paid for all repairs and insurance on the Lodge and has meanwhile grown to approximately $3000. So it is quite clear that the Wiley Lodge Fund, as it is known, can rebuild a lodge whenever one wears out (most of these camps last about 35 years) and leave enough money to grow and build still another one when one wears out and so on indefinitely. It is the only lodge endowed so that the fund will pay for its replacement. A tremendous thing was done with $2500, not at all an astronomical sum.

Maybe the day will come when every camp on the Trail will have its own fund to take care of it forever. Some would need more money than the Wiley one did. Some would need less. Maybe there is nothing some trustee would like better that to have someone say: "Is there a camp on the trail that could be taken care of for $1000?" Maybe he could reply: "There sure is and we'll name it after you."

EDNA ROHRBAUGH, Reporter

Across the valley's span

Of other mountains frown
All from this spot that's halfway up

Wild berry bushes huddle near
Again, the breezes sigh

That also stands hardby
And just beyond this wooded knoll
A stream comes tumbling down!
For this is only halfway up
Not way up on the crown.

To make one feel quite small
And yet again—it's low enough
To have the heights enthrall
One revels in Spring's virgin greens
And venerates Fall's brown
All from this spot that's halfway up
As well as halfway down.

But tho' it's only halfway up
It lifts the spirit high
And tho' it's not quite halfway down
It communes with the sky

All weave for it a crown
And raises one THRICE halfway up
And never lets you down!

HAVE YOU HEARD?

The Ski Touring Council is hard at work locating and developing "white trails" which will be suitable for cross country skiing.

Interest in the sport of skiing has increased tremendously in recent years and although new ski areas are constantly being developed to meet this need, many skiers are weary of the crowded slopes, the long waits on lift lines and the up and down hill routine.

So if you are a skier who would enjoy the peace and beauty of the woods in winter, try ski touring.

"A-HIKING WE DID GO"-Part IV
by ERIKA N. HEININGER

Saturday, July 1st, 1939

Regretfully, we left Stratton Pond, and pushed on to Swezey Camp. The flies were still biting. Things seemed awfully quiet after leaving Willis Ross Camp.

Swezey was a little two-by-four which reminded me of a fishing shanty. It had a huge old stove. Before we could make a fire in it, we had to remove a mother mouse and her brood to safer quarters.

We spent the evening talking about the prospect of seeing our parents the next day. The first week had been packed with fun and excitement. None of us had been homesick.

Sunday, July 2nd

We started off early, eager to see the folks from home.

Around two o'clock, we met them. Then I, who had been so proud because I had not been homesick all the week, burst into tears and cried like a baby when I saw Mom and Dad.

Thus passed the first week of our trip,—a trip which lengthened into several weeks instead of five. The first week was a period of adjustment. After that we became reconciled to each other; became accustomed to our heavy packs; to getting up early and hiking all day; to hiking in all kinds of weather; to going hungry Saturday nights before we got new provisions; to hanging up our packs at night and putting the butter in the stream; to eating sardines; to porcupines; to flies; to cows, and most important of all to climbing endless mountains.

We had the varied experiences of being serenaded by hill-billies; of playing nurse to a fellow hiker for three days; of staying overnight at a house; of telephoning from a general store; of climbing Bolton in the rain and a thunder storm and walking four miles soaked to the skin; of baking a pie at Butler Lodge on Mansfield; of hitch-hiking fifty-two miles to see a movie in Newport; of traveling the last three miles to Post 592 accompanied by four cows; and of spending our last night at Journey's End with a poetry-spouting Harvard graduate.

It was a summer I can never forget. There is nothing so thrilling as getting to know your own State. This is especially so if your acquaintance is made by walking through its hilly meadows, climbing its stone walls; scaling its mountain peaks, meeting its people, loving its beauteous scenery.

In such a summer, spent close to nature, one gets a new slant on life and things in general. True, it's hard work, but it brings with it pride and inspiration of achievement. The vigorous outdoor life makes one physically strong and mentally awake.

People from all over come to Vermont to hike its famous Long Trail, which is maintained by a small group of men and women who love the Green Mountains and want everyone to share the wonders of God as seen only "on the Trail."

What Since 1939?

A delightful experience for the girls, the trip also proved most pleasurable for the families who, each Sunday, had wonderful reunions when they met the girls with fresh provisions and clean clothing. What a nice way to spend the summer!

But the hiking didn't stop there. Later when Erika became Mrs. Alan R. Sawyer, they enjoyed their honeymoon on the Long Trail. And now, although living in Washington, D.C., the Sawyers' three girls and two boys think that Vermont is the most wonderful place to come to. (Don't we all!)

Who knows... maybe some day the Sawyers' names will be added to that ever-increasing list of special people called "Long Trail End-to-Enders."

WHY CLIMB?

Did you ever ask yourself why climb? When Mallory was asked why they climbed Mt. Everest, he was said to have replied, "Because it was there."

Do you climb because the mountains are there?

Roy Buchanan heard of another reason for climbing that he'd like to share with us. Ranch Valley in Stowe where Burlington Section has their camp is owned by Craig Burt who, with Judge Cowles of Burlington, did the very first work on the Long Trail. During a very interesting conversation, Craig showed Roy an extract from a letter he had received from his daughter. She wrote that "apropos of nothing" her 10 year old son, John Wright of Hinsdale, Illinois, had said to her, "I like mountains. I like the way they make you feel. I like the way I felt on top of the Nose. As if I were free and could do anything."

Why do you climb?
