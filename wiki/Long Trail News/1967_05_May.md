NEW ENGLAND TRAILS CONFERENCE

The New England Trails Conference had a very good attendance at the 50th Anniversary Meeting held at the University of Massachusetts, Amherst, Mass., on April 1, 1967.

The new Chairman of NETC is Dr. Walter Banfield, a professor at the University of Massachusetts and a member of the Worcester Section GMC. Edwin H. Beach of Cranston, Rhode Island was elected Vice-Chairman, a new post. Hope Packard was reelected Secretary-Treasurer.

Roy Buchanan was made an Honorary Director, another GMCer on that list.

A change in the manner of electing officers was adopted so that the length of tenure was limited to the end that rotation in office would be more stimulating.

Reports and discussions on the trails and shelters maintained by the forty constituent organization were limited to oral reports. The written report will be printed in New England Trails which Hope Packard will send out in June.

John Vondell, GMC past president, gave a twelve-minute report on "The NETC from 1916 to 1967." Fast work!!

Fran Belcher of Boston paid tribute to the late Charles W. Blood who was a long time member—possibly a "charter member."

Art Koerber, another GMCer, brought up the H.R. Bill 4865—"The Nationwide System of Trails." But so much time was taken up because those present were not familiar with the bill which is too involved and hazy for meaningful discussion and effective voting.

Shirley Strong, Past President of the Burlington Section, had a fine map display and a supply of pertinent literature on her espoused subject, Conservation.

George Pearlstein, GMC Trustee-at-Large, has replaced Roy Buchanan on the Steering Committee and Ben Rolston was reelected to it.

In the afternoon session "The Aristocratic Rocks of New Hampshire" was the subject presented by George Ehrenfried, a geologist and mountain climber who knows whereon he treads.

A relaxing rest was followed by a very nice dinner served in the Student Union on Campus.

The evening topic was a "Closer Look at Nature" by Wilfred Kimber, one of the finest nature photographers in the country who holds conversations with his bird subjects.

GMC President John Rohrbaugh and Edna were among the many GMCers who attended the meetings.

VIEWS OF THE MOUNTAINS

Did you see the beautiful picture of Camel's Hump in the February issue of National Geographic? It was featured in Vermont's advertisement with the caption "We made a promise to this mountain"—a promise that it will always remain a landmark of natural beauty. This is wonderful news.

The advertisement goes on to say similar promises have been made to other mountains, but, unfortunately, there is little evidence that these promises are firm ones. Pressures for development of state-owned mountains lands are great and plans are underway for more development of these at the higher elevations. All this only emphasizes the urgency for planning, and then action, so that the mountains will continue to meet the needs of all, both now and in the future.

Some encouraging steps are being taken—the designation of the top of Camel's Hump as a natural area; the inclusion of mountain tops in legislation amending the Vermont Natural Areas Act; and the recently published policy of the Forests and Parks Department which calls for development and implementation of a Land Classification Plan for all its lands and which recognizes the importance of a mountain highlands classification.

Further, a plan has been proposed for a "Wildlands Profile Strip" extending the length of Vermont. It calls for careful planning and use of land in the mountains, especially at the higher elevations, and would tie together various types of areas—recreational, scenic and natural—into a meaningful and unique whole. Its implementation would necessitate cooperative efforts of the State, USFS and private landowners. This plan has evolved during the past year from the concern of interested citizens that the scenic beauty of the mountains and the quality of the various recreational opportunities there should not be lost. It has gained the endorsement of several organizations, including the Green Mountain Club, and many individuals. It is currently being studied by state officials. More details will be available at the Annual Meeting.

These initial steps need to be encouraged and strongly supported if Vermont is to remain the state of the beautiful Green Mountains.

SHIRLEY STRONG
Conservation Committee

GREEN MOUNTAIN FEATURE IN VERMONT LIFE

To all GMCers we heartily recommend the next issue of Vermont Life (Summer 1967) which contains an outstanding article on the Green Mountains. A mite of geology plus ample physiography focus on "how" and "what now" of the distinctive features of the State. The natural resources have been developed with an eye on conservation and proper maintenance of the beauty that is everywhere so evident in all seasons of the year.

The desire to share the peaceful quiet of the countryside in spring, summer and fall with hikers and riders, as well as the glistening snow of the mountain slopes in winter has brought so many guests from out-of-state. Hopefully they will remember that many many others will want to enjoy the same pleasures in the future and so help to preserve and improve these opportunities.

The pictures by John Vandell and others are superb!

Benjamin Stein's specially drafted Green Mountain map view of the State from high over the Adirondacks looking east. With Lake Champlain in the foreground the major rivers, lakes, cities and mountains are identified, and the LONG TRAIL winds its way along the main ridge from Massachusetts to Canada. In the distance the Connecticut River outlines Vermont's border with New Hampshire. A Special Map reprint, designed for framing, enlarged to approximately 35 x 12 inches and printed in two colors on heavy paper will be available at a nominal cost.

So don't miss it—Vermont Life—Summer 1967.

ATC CONFERENCE

The Seventeenth Meeting of the Appalachian Trail Conference will be held at High Hampton Inn, Cashiers, N.C., on May 20-22, 1967, with the Georgia Appalachian Trail Club as hosts. As you may recall we GMCers hosted the last conference three years ago at Stratton Mountain.

A stimulating program is being planned with an innovation this year of a session that is divided into several groups, each considering a different subject and reporting back to the whole body. Also the Board of Managers will meet prior to and following the general sessions.

Those who will be attending the Conference should contact GMC President John Rohrbaugh re: GMC representation. Also any members of the New York Section who are able to attend the Conference are asked to contact N.Y. Section President Margaret M. Pons re: delegates.

With the Bureau of Outdoor Recreation study on a Nationwide System of Trails published under the title Trails for America (Superintendent of Documents, Government Printing Office, Washington, D.C. 20402. Cost $2.25) and the house and senate holding hearings on the nationwide trailways bills, there is much thought and talk about our present and future trails. A notable quote from Stanley A. Murray, Chairman of the Appalachian Trail Conference: "Throughout such times, we try to preserve the Appalachian Trail without noticeable change—no change, that is, except for the changing colors, the shifting winds, and the endless progression of animal and plant life that only God has dominion over."

KILLINGTON SECTION

Late in January the Section held its Annual Meeting. Our financial condition is healthy but we have lost a little weight membershipwise. New officers elected were Terry Gulick, President; Janet Martin, Vice President; Churchill Scott, Secretary and Treasurer; Elizabeth Weeks, Trustee.

It was decided not to rebuild Buffum Lodge at the present time as it is not necessary to have a shelter at that location, and a new camp at some other spot may be more useful at a future date.

Bald Mountain, a part of the Aiken Forest, is a 2,000-foot elevation only a couple miles from Rutland. It was suggested that this area should be protected from commercial development and used for the less destructive forms of recreation only. The officers were instructed to cooperate with other groups to this end.

John Vondell presented his unique slide collection and entertaining comments.

So the Killington Section is off to another year of hiking and eating.

As far as we know our trails and shelters are in good condition. Some needed work on Tucker Lodge will be carried out this year.

See you around.

MONTPELIER SECTION

Spring is here and it seems strange to be recounting January activities in April knowing the material won't be published until May but here goes.

On Saturday, January 14, we held our Annual Oyster Stew Hike to Taylor Lodge. This year we made it an overnight.

We hiked up (or down) the lake (some on skis) and back via the old railroad bed. On Wednesday, February 22, we had another moonlight hike using snowshoes and skis followed by slides and refreshments at Dave Morse's.

Our section though small is active—and note the amount of eating we do.

PIONEER VALLEY SECTION

The exceptionally heavy snow fall in the Pioneer Valley has turned all our recent hikes into snowshoe trips and I for one have enjoyed this very much. Snowshoe travel is becoming quite popular, with even the "Wall Street Journal" praising it with a recent article. Our hike in the Berkshires in March was on snowshoes and after a climb over the mountain and the crossing of Goose Pond, we enjoyed the warmth and shelter of the cottage on the Pond owned by the Deans, leaders of this hike.

We snowshoed on following Sundays in Blanford, Mass., Plainfield, Mass., and other local spots sharing the trails many times with the snowmobiles that are increasing in number. On Saturday, March 25, Gil Myrick and I went to the Mass.-Vt. line on snowshoes on an inspection trip of the lower section of the LT. The snow was four to five feet deep, almost covering some of the paint blazes on the trees. We covered over nine miles round trip and I was tired and sunburned when we had returned to the car. The section of the trail we covered seems to be in good condition and we hope to check the rest of our section before the annual meeting in May.

Many of us plan to attend the New England Trail Conference April 1 and on April 2 we will hike to the Mt. Tom State Reservation near Holyoke, Mass. A walk in the Pleasant Valley Wildlife Sanctuary in Lenox, Mass., is tentatively scheduled for May 7, and an overnight hike and campout is planned for the early part of June near Ascutney, Vt. Our plans cannot be definite as work parties may interfere. This will be a busy year since we have a new shelter to build and some trail work to do.

Some of our members think that a joint hike with other sections would be fun and a good chance to meet and chat with fellow members of the GMC. If you like this idea have your Chairman of Activities write to our Chairman, Mrs. Dorothy Knight, 152 Lucerne Road, Springfield, Mass. It has been suggested that we could meet some other club about half way between our two areas for a hike and that way cut down the distance we would both have to travel.

RICHARD METCALFE, Reporter

WORCESTER SECTION

This crazy New England weather really played havoc with some our best laid plans this past winter. What do you do when it gets to feel like Spring in the middle of January? In spite of it all, we did get out and had some very enjoyable hikes.

Our first scheduled hike was up to the Rutland Prison Camp (long since abandoned). This was supposed to be a snowshoe hike!! You guessed it ... it was raining! (Didn't have too large a group.) This was followed by hikes in Shrewsbury and in Howe State Park in Spencer. The weekend of January 21 saw some of our people really "living it up" at the Northfield Inn, and some of the stay-at-homes rescheduled a hike to the Rutland Prison Camp. This time the weather was nice. We were quite surprised when we came across the State Police checking fingerprints on a stolen safe ... way out there in the woods.

February 12 turned out to be a rather cold day but 12 hardy souls went out for a hike in the Barre Falls Area.

Finally, on February 26, we actually had a snowshoe hike. Although the group was small in number, a very good time was had by all. By actual count, they went over or under 16 barriers—eight barbed wire or wood. This trip, in the vicinity of our Camp, wound across fields, through woods, and over the hills. At one spot they got a very good view of the Moose Hill Ski Area, the real Moose Hill, Burncoat Pond, and Cedar Meadow Pond.

On March 5, a small group of hikers enjoyed fresh air on a hike in West Boylston.

The weekend of March 11-12 saw some of our group up at the former Birchwood Inn, in Temple, N.H. Altogether the weather was a bit warm—a good time was had by all!

Saturday, March 18, was one of the events of the year, our Sugar-on-Snow Party. This year it was held at the First Congregational Church in Auburn. The ladies of the Church put on a very tasty ham dinner for us, and this was topped off by our SUGAR. Jim Green sure has mastered the art of boiling that maple syrup!!! To make this perfect evening complete, we were treated to two wonderful slide shows. Nellie [illegible] showed us her pictures of "Christmas in Florida" and Gert Woodbury showed some really spectacular scenes taken at AMC August Camp in the High Sierras. A real hearty "well-done" should be given to the committee that planned all this: Helen Vickstrom, Barbara Christensen, Peggy Pearson and Shirley Faucher.

We have some real nice hikes and other outings planned for the coming months. Included in our plans are canoe trips, camping weekends, hikes for everyone work parties, strawberry social and many other events. One planned hike in particular may interest some of our members in the Pioneer Valley area. On September 17 we plan a hike somewhere in or about the Holyoke Range.

Note: Any GMCer will be welcome at any of our affairs, etc. For details call or write to us at the following address: Fred Eldridge, 174 College St., Worcester, Mass. 01610, phone 617-754-7312.

FRED ELDRIDGE, Reporter

CARDIGAN INVITATION

Cardigan Lodge, owned and operated by the Appalachian Mountain Club, is an attractive mountain inn located in the heart of the Lakes Region in Central New Hampshire. It is about ten miles west of Newfound Lake, and northeast of the town of Bristol, at the end of a winding gravel road, nestled in the Shem Valley. A thousand acres of AMC owned woodland, known as Cardigan Reservation, surround the Lodge and adjoin some 7,000 additional acres of State Parks. Included in this reserved area are 3,121-foot Mt. Cardigan, whose ledgy, subalpine summit dominates the area, several lesser peaks, scenic Welton Falls and nearly fifty miles of trails. The Lodge is peacefully isolated from the busy world and is an ideal vacation or weekend retreat for adults and children who enjoy the out-of-doors.

The Lodge and Meals

The Lodge is operated on an informal family-type basis. Its accommodations are private (rooms for 3 to 6 persons), semi-private, or bunkroom style. One may stay in the Main Lodge, Little House (the original Shem Ackerman farmhouse), or in one of the six two-person platform tents in the nearby peaceful woods. The total capacity is about 75 persons. Linen and blankets are supplied. All guests may enjoy the facilities of the Main Lodge which include a living room with library, a fun room, and the picture-windowed dining room which doubles as a function room for square dances, movies, lectures, and other activities. The Little House also has a cozy living room with fireplace and game room which all may use. Meals are designed for hearty mountain appetites and are served family-style at long tables, with no one to count the "seconds." Trail lunches are available for all-day excursions. On occasion cook-outs are held on the lawn or at nearby points of interest.

GMC members have been extended the courtesy of AMC Member rates for accommodations at Cardigan.

For additional information folders, rate schedules, and reservations during the season, June 16, 1967 through September 11, 1967, contact Manager, AMC, Cardigan Lodge, RFD, Bristol, N.H. 03222. Telephone 603-744-8011. For information out of season contact Cardigan, Appalachian Mountain Club, 5 Joy St., Boston, Mass. 02108.

FORUM-EXCELLENT NEWSLETTER

The Vermont Natural Resources Forum is a new publication prepared by the Extension Service, U.S. Department of Agriculture, University of Vermont. In the first issue (November 1966) Governor Philip H. Hoff explained its purpose as follows:

"The Natural Resources Forum will provide Vermonters with, first, a way of getting up-to-date and pertinent information about the wide range of subjects in the natural resources field, particularly as they affect Vermont, and, second, a way for Vermonters with questions or suggestions in the natural resources field to route them into one central place for study by the committee and distribution to the proper agencies, organizations, and officials."

From time to time, in our section news, we've read about canoe trips on Otter Creek. Of interest to all canoeists is the following article reprinted from the January issue of FORUM:

"Vermont has about 130 access areas for fishermen, very few for canoeists. A dedicated lot, the paddle set has long cast loving glances at Otter Creek as one of the few rivers in the state suitable for long distance flat water canoeing.

"The Otter Creek Soil and Water Conservation District, fresh from its 25th anniversary, is paddling hard toward the same goal.

"District officials decided to emphasize the clean-up and development of the creek. They sponsored a tour for agency officials in July to dramatize the potential and unspoiled beauty of the stream, which starts in Danby and outlets in Lake Champlain at Ferrisburg, a distance of about 100 miles. This fostered the idea of a trail from the headwater to Vergennes.

"In August, SWCD put the idea before the public at a general meeting. The reaction was overwhelmingly in favor of the plan. The next morning, sponsors and public joined forces for a canoe and boat tour of the river.

"Proponents say stream zoning is a natural because the creek is unsuitable for power boating anyway. All compatible uses such as fishing would be permitted.

"As part of the plan, access areas and small camp sites (reached only by water) would be purchased.

"Presently, the committee is working on a development plan for the canoe trail and on a concrete proposal to put before the legislature.

"That's the development side of Otter Creek. As for the clean-up, the other anniversary goal, that's also progressing. All communities over 2,000 are completing sewage disposal plants. Rural sewage is being converted to septic tanks and leach fields.

"Otter Creek stands a good chance of becoming the first river in the U.S. to be completely free of pollution from city and town sewage.

"Happy anniversary, SWCD."

ASCUTNEY TRAILS ASSOCIATION

The Ascutney Trails Association was formed in January 1967. Its purpose is to maintain present and future trails on Ascutney, to build and maintain shelters, to hunt out special points of interest to which trails may be opened, to gather items of history and anecdote on the Mountain, and to publish a guide book.

Under the leadership of Mr. Herbert G. Ogden and his son Herbert G. Ogden, Jr., they have ready for sale an attractive guide book giving a very interesting account of the beginning of the trails in the Green Mountains in 1825.

The illustrations, some old, some new, include a two-page contour map of the Windsor Trail, the original trail on Ascutney Mountain. This trail is cleared and ready for use now, while the Weathersfield Trail will be cleared in the summer of 1967.

"Guide to the Trails of Ascutney Mountain" will be available on May 1, 1967. The price is $1.25 in stores; $1.10 by mail (Ascutney Trails Association, RFD 1, Windsor, Vt.); or $1.00 person-to-person. Mr. Ogden will have copies of the guide book at our Annual Meeting. Proceeds from all sales will be used for Trail and Shelter expenses.
