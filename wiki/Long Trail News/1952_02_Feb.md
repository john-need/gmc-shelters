"STATE OF THE UNION"

   It does seem as though the President might take his cue from another President and report on the "State of the Union" of our Club and at the same time send a cordial greeting to all members.
   From reports and information gathered here and there all sections have been participating in and are planning a variety of most interesting activities. These should be of great interest to members and prospective members. The Worcester and Killington sections keep me informed of their plans and I read with interest their schedules wishing that I were able to attend some of their outings. Their activities chairmen plan well. I am sure they feel well rewarded when the attendance is good on the outings which they spend considerable time in arranging. Why not make them really like the job by going out oftener?
   Miss Tye tells me that most of "her" members, members-at-large have accepted the necessary increase of dues and that her roster is almost on par with former years. As to those who have not "re-enlisted" as yet, I am sure she will be very pleased to hear from you. Your dues are truly needed for trail and shelter maintenance and for general expenses.
   There are in storage in headquarters in Rutland many old cuts formerly used in the printing of the Long Trail News. The Board is considering disposal of most of these for their value as old metal. Should anyone have an idea as to how they might be used to better advantage, may we hear from you? Miss Tye is well pleased with our new headquarters in Rutland and would be glad to have any members who may be in Rutland stop in for a visit.
   One might say that our financial condition is nothing to bragg about. Now please do not have the impression that our treasurer does not keep a keen watchful eye on our finances, but the rub is, that we do not have a sufficient balance to do as much as we wish for the improvement of trails and shelters. Our income is just about static but our expenses, like all others, must follow the curve of inflation which as you all know is on the up and up.
   It is, however, a pleasure to report that a gift of $100.00 to our treasury was recently made by a member now living in Illinois who still returns to beautiful Vermont each summer. Are there others who can be as generous and will aid a most worthy cause? We have also added one new life member to the roster and I am certain that Miss Tye would like to add other names to that same list.
   I am sorry to report that one of our sections (Wyanokie) voted to disband. They kindly voted to send their treasury balance to the main Club. We gratefully receive their donation of $94.00. Some of their members are members of the New York Section. We cordially invite any who are not affiliated with any other section to become members-at-large and thus keep their contact with us and help in the educational and conservational work of our Club.
   The annual meeting this year will be held at the Long Trail Lodge on Saturday May 31st. This is a three-day week-end so should encourage a large attendance. It will give an opportunity for hiking and camping on the trail as well as attending the meeting. (Will the demon campers of the New York Section please note?) As yet a program has not been arranged so watch for details in the next "News" edition. We should have all sections represented. Members-at-large are most cordially invited to be with us.
   It would not be right to end this report without saying that many reported last year the trail was in excellent condition through most of its length. The trail chairman and his helpers and the section's trail workers all did a splendid job. The work they do is probably more often criticised than it is praised, human nature being what it is, but just put it down in capital letters in your book that they did a bang-up clean-up job. I am sure all real trailers realize that and are grateful to those hardy workers.
   So endeth this reading with an admonition to keep plugging and advertizing your Club, keep after delinquents and especially seek out new members. Plan for a showing and arrange through Miss Tye to borrow our wonderful set of slides for schools, church meetings or clubs. A prepared lecture accompanies the slides. Help your Club to grow and grow and grow.

ROY WOODELTON.

NEWS FROM THE BURLINGTON SECTION

The Burlington section of the Green Mountain Club elected Miss Beverly Thorpe, a UVM junior, as its president for 1952 at the annual meeting Jan. 9th in the Waterman Building.

The meeting opened the club's 42nd year of activities. These include support of the Long Trail, social events and year-around hiking and mountain climbing events.

The election of officers and business meeting, presided over by Harold Collins, president, was preceded by a dinner in the dining room.

The group elected: President, Beverly Thorpe; vice-president Capt. Claude W. Johnson; secretary, Mrs. Joyce Fellows; treasurer, Ernest E. Smith; property chairman, Donald Remick; trail committee chairman, Don Havens; outing chairman, Elton Beal, and entertainment committee chairman, Ruth Aseltine.

Reports from the club officers and committee chairmen were heard, as well as the president's report.

A complete report was given on the rebuilding of the James P. Taylor Memorial Lodge in Nebraska Notch after its destruction by fire a year ago.

Collins presented Mrs. Joyce Fellows, in behalf of the club, with a set of silverware as a gift to her and her husband, Jean, now in the armed service in Germany.

Sam Hatfield presented two large photos of Mt. Mansfield each to Collins and to Roy Buchanan, Long Trail patrol chief.

A brief pause in the meeting honored the memory of Prof. L. B. Puffer, long time Green Mountain Club member.

New Shelter Planned

It was proposed that a shelter of some sort be built between the Bolton Lodge and Taylor Lodge.

This would provide a break in a tough hiking stretch of trail between those two lodges which are located seven and a half miles and three mountains apart. Don Havens was elected chairman of a committee of three to look into the matter.

Ed Bartlett showed colored movies entitled "Green Mountain Land," produced by the Vermont Development Commission. Several members showed kodachrome slides of trail and Vermont views.

About 80 attended the supper and meeting. Miss Peggy Jones and a party of five came from Barre.

The dining room was decorated by Misses Barbara Beal, Beverly Thorpe and Mrs. Joyce Fellows. They used green hemlock tips, candles and unique Long Trail signs of Elton Beal design and creation. Each plate had a personal place card and program of the meeting.

MOUNTAINEERS HONOR PROFESSOR BUCHANAN ON 70TH BIRTHDAY

Fifty-seven Green Mountain Club members swarmed into the home of Prof. R. O. Buchanan on his 70th birthday anniversary. The group assembled at the home of Richard Cowles before going to the Buchanan residence.

Dick Cowles led in group singing of several Christmas carols, accompanied by Miss Barbara Beal at the piano. Leland Brown showed colored slides of his last two trips to the West Coast. They included shots of the Great Desert, Rockies, Pacific coast and many National Park areas.

The club president, Harold Collins, addressed the group briefly and then in behalf of those present and the several absent well-wishers presented Professor Buchanan with a gas lantern and a purse of money.

Mrs. Don Havens, Mrs. Harold Collins, Mrs. Larry Shorey and Miss Beverly Thorpe served refreshments.

Acts Younger Than 70

The 70-year-young mountain climbing enthusiast and retired UVM professor's activities and appearance are in keeping with a man 20 years younger. Just this past summer he actively supervised the construction of the new Taylor lodge, packing up over the steep approach trail with the youngest of his trail crew.

Since 1920 he has been an active man of the trail, directing the construction of 23 Long Trail shelters and working the trail yearly besides climbing and hiking for fun. He has climbed all the major peaks of the East, including the highest, Marcy, Mansfield, Washington and Katadin, many of them several times.

Climbs Adirondack Peaks

With one of his favorite hiking pals, the late Prof. L. P. Puffer, he completed the strenuous accomplishment of climbing 46 peaks in the Adirondacks, many of them trailless, to become a full-fledged member of that rugged group of mountain climbers, the 46er Club.

Besides hiking, the wiry little professor enjoys snowshoeing, skiing and skating. Besides being a long time member of the Long Trail Patrol, he has been president of the Local Section, Green Mountain Club, as well as the club at large, and has served as club trustee for many years. His colored slides and humorous remarks make him much sought after as a speaker.

'Sage of the Mountain Trails'

His love of the outdoors, trail and mountains and his way of life add up to make the "Sage of the Mountain Trails" the supreme and esteemed comrade and loyal friend of the large circle of men and women, young and old, who have hit the trail with him.

Because of the great distance, the professor's two sons could not be present but his brother Bruce of Brattleboro was on hand. Pres. Harold Collins, who headed the party arrangements, announced that several not present far and wide had sent well wishes to Professor Buchanan. Several of that group had only met the professor as he was working along the trail.

(The above article was written by Larry Dean for the Burlington Free Press. The party was held on December 13, 1951).

REPORT FROM THE BRATTLEBORO SECTION

On Thursday evening, January 3, 1952, thirteen members of the Brattleboro Section of the Green Mountain Club met at Dave Dunklee's home to discuss the president's (Bruce Buchanan) motion to disband. John Mushovic, our new member from Greenfield, arrived wearing a bright red necktie which he said "was not suitable for a funeral." His remark caused laughter and a defeat of the disbanding motion. The members then discussed the recent new by-law requiring at least 10 adult members in a section. The group decided to trust those who had not yet paid their 1951 dues so that the section would have more than enough members to be a section. About this time someone began discussing the recipe for baked crow (as given over the radio that morning).

'Jerry' Stone and Ernest Barre reported that they had climbed Mt. Washington in October and wanted recognition for their effort. But, on further inquiry the truth was that they drove up in an automobile and got stuck at the frost line where the car wheels spun around backwards and clothes meant for a Forestry convention were not adequate for winter hiking.

The treasurer reported $57.80 on hand. Discussion favored its use for rebuilding Sweezy Camp.

The present officers were re-elected for another year. President, Bruce Buchanan; Vice President, Blanche Carpenter; Secretary-Treasurer, Louise Dunklee.

Committees were named as follows: Trails Committee Ray Pestle, chairman with John Mushovic, Oscar Stone and Dave Dunklee working with him. Program committee, Blanche Carpenter, chairman, with Whitney Hartshorne, Mrs. Charles Bonner and Eleanor Stone assisting her. Publicity, Louise Mullen and Mary Stone. Membership, John Mushovic, Bruce Buchanan and Ernest Barre.

Bruce Buchanan showed some of his brother's pictures. Roy had some very good pictures of the north end of The Long Trail as well as a few of the south end. We all enjoyed them.

During the evening, Bruce reported that Roy wanted to know what panthers did with the clothes. Dave Dunklee's answer was, "Why worry about the clothes. Panthers eat a deer-hide, hair and all. They also bury their food and haul their food into their dens." (This refers to the Panther story that appeared in the "News" some time ago.)

We know we will all miss the wit of Lou Puffer in THE LONG TRAIL NEWS. Let's report all wit of our meetings in his memory and we suggest that it be a Puffer Column.

LOUISE DUNKLEE

In reporting activities of the Brattleboro Section for 1951, Mrs. Dunklee said that participation in the April, May, and June part of their program was encouraging but "the rest of a good program met with one misfortune after another. The weather man poured water on most of it. Individual problems added up so that the rest of the program was forgotten. Bruce Buchanan, our president, was so discouraged that he proposed a motion that we meet and disband our section."

We are certainly glad that the motion "to disband" was defeated. Congratulations to the Brattleboro Section for showing determination and stick-to-itiveness. Best wishes go to them. Their January 1952 meeting report sounds like one from a good healthy section.

REPORT FROM THE WORCESTER SECTION

One of the few pleasures left to us in this battered old world is the chance to bring happiness into the lives of our friends. The Worcester Section had that chance on November 25, 1951.

We had the pleasure then of paying our respects to Al and Phyllis Williams on the occasion of their 25th wedding anniversary. Al is the chairman of the Trustees and has supervised the building of our camp. It was necessary to resort to a bit of subterfuge in order to keep the affair as quiet as possible, so we called on our newly-weds, Arthur and Virginia Olson to take the spotlight and they responded nobly.

Sixty-five members and friends gave Al and Phyllis a sum of money and a deep feeling of their appreciation for what they have done for the club.

Joe and Laura Frappier, Dr. Bob and Rose Steele, Rhea Mosher and Jessie Phillimore were the committee in charge.

On December 1st, we held our annual dinner-meeting and election of officers at the Franklin Manor, West Boylston, Massachusetts.

A reception was held from 6:30 until 7:00. Dinner was then served, and it was excellent as the sixty members and guests agreed.

President John B. Knowlton presided at the business meeting which followed. As we heard the reports read by the officers and committee chairmen we began to realize the amount of teamwork it takes to keep an organization like G.M.C. going.

Our membership is 149.

NOTES FROM NEW YORK

During the past three months and for February, the New York Section has scheduled 8 Saturday outings, 15 Sunday outings and hikes, 5 week-end outings, and 11 week-ends at Camp Thendara.

On these outings we have visited museums, libraries, and churches. We have sketched, explored caves and skated. We have cleared trails and hiked. We have also looked at some excellent pictures and enjoyed the good meals at our two dinner meetings.

On February 1st our annual dinner dance, complete with Hawaiian music and singers, grass skirts and leis, will undoubtedly draw the usual good crowd.

Our Vermont work for the coming season will probably consist of maintenance work and trail clearing.

John Nuffort, our Vermont Chairman, is consumed with curiosity about reports that there is a cave on Camel's Hump. Every once in a while these rumors crop up anew and we wonder if there can be any truth in them, or if the stories are just to tease the poor New Yorkers who cannot get up to Vermont to explore around as much as they'd like to anyway. Does anyone know of a cave on Camel's Hump or have any information on the subject? If so, do drop a line to John Nuffort, 73 Hamilton St., Madison, N.J.

The Trails committee chairman, Craigue Perkins, reported that our section of The Long Trail is in good condition. There are a few blow downs north of Spring Lake and some young evergreens are growing up on the trail between Pico and Killington.

Interesting kodachrome slides of trips taken in Maine, New Hampshire, and Vermont were shown by Carl Newhall. Betty Ball showed slides taken on a Gaspe trip last summer.

CLARENDON SHELTER NEEDS REPAIRS

Clarendon Shelter is an old favorite of a great many Killington Section hikers. It was built in 1921 and has weathered many a storm. Now it is old and leaky, but still a welcome sight to many who hike over The Long Trail.

Situated in a beautiful pine grove, close to an ever flowing brook, it is one of the beauty spots of The Trail.

The shelter needs a new roof. It also needs some type of wall siding. During the past years the sides have been repaired by nailing roofing paper over worn boards. While this is an inexpensive method of repairing the walls, it doesn't last very long. Perhaps it would be less expensive in the long run, to apply something more durable. This is something for the Shelters committee to work out.

Another idea that has come to mind is, closing the shelter in to make it a more weather proof, all season camp. A window could be put in the back of the camp and hinged doors, similar to the Spring Lake camp doors could be installed. This would provide a closed camp for winter use and for summer use the doors could be swung open.

GLEANINGS FROM CAMP REGISTERS

From the camp registers along the Trail, those who follow read with great interest of the weather, flora and fauna, menus, and philosophical comments of hikers. A register recently filled and removed from Willis Ross is probably typical in content of those used elsewhere along the way, and a source of some interesting comments and observations. Of course those responsible for the camp are always particularly pleased to read "The camper of the 21st left this place in excellent shape after a lot of hard work. We are leaving it that way and hope it remains so." This is particularly interesting as the "camper of the 21st," Norman Burns, had written "Found the camp looking like the proverbial outhouse. The stove pipe was gone with the exception of a couple of lengths wired to the roof. Spent most of the afternoon putting that in working order with the aid of a tin can. Found tins of opened and unused food on the floor, dirty dishes all over and frozen coffee in the pot on the stove, bits of rotten food in the bunk."
Thank you, Mr. Burns, for being a good camper. Sorry we can't say the same to those who made your chores necessary.

Porcupines cause many candid remarks along the way. "Eppy left his 35 mm. camera inside its $8.50 leather case on the outside table, and the porcupine just ate up the case and didn't as much as touch the camera." Of another porky, a camper wrote- "-we'll see if he shows up again tonight. If he does methinks we'll have roast porcupine for Easter dinner!" And two who had just come in from the south reported. "The Hedgehog Rotary Club of Caughnawauga had its annual celebration last night forcing us to withdraw in disorder this morning after a sleepless night." Less annoying, but almost as prevalent, are the mice. One girl wrote, "Don tells me I had a little mouse reclining on my shoulder all night. Thank God I am a sound sleeper!"

Entries having to do with birds and flowers sometimes give us a fair picture of which may be found in one area of Vermont. One early April entry mentions "hawk, robin, chickadee, catbird, king bird, bluejay, owl. Partridge drumming mating call continuously." In July of the same year another reported "Pair of Kingfishers on lake and winter wrens, juncos, etc. Saw Blackpoll Warbler and American Three-toed Woodpeckers-Ruby and Golden crowned Kinglets, and-a red-breasted nuthatch."

One or two honeymooning couples seem to register each year.

A party who stayed several days wrote "Would appreciate company. None in sight at noon." Apparently they got their wish, for later the same day they wrote of the arrival of a party of 32, and on the following day reported five more campers and some picknickers. "Just like Coney Island," was their comment toward the end of that day.

An entry we particularly don't like to find, especially since two trail shelters have been destroyed by fire in recent years, is the following one indicative of one of our worst trail problems, "Yesterday evening just after dark we discovered a fire smoldering in the woods. It may have been burning for some time." Nor do we like to find, in a camp that we try to keep in good condition, a comment that "much carving of initials and painting of walls was freshly done."

(Incidentally, the paint used to daub "fool's names" on the camp had been left there to use renewing blazes, so-if you get lost because the blazes aren't fresh, whose fault is it?)

THE PROGRESS OF FORESTRY 1945 TO 1950

Across America an exciting drama in forestry conservation has taken place since 1945. To find out what has actually happened, The American Forestry Association has assembled facts and figures from many sources. These are now available in a report of 90 pages.

The survey was made to determine the trends of events since World War II and to point out the strong and weak points in the whole forestry conservation movement.

The tremendous task of fact-gathering was carried out by a committee of government, state and private experts. It brings under one cover, a documented record of forestry progress in one of the most critical periods in the forestry conservation movement in the United States.

Published by The American Forestry Association, 919 Seventeenth Street, N. W., Washington 6, D. C.
Price $3.00

HOW TO MAKE GOOD PICTURES

This book is not exactly new but it is the new edition of a guide that was first published in 1912. The book is divided into four main fields—the what, why, and how of photography in general; specific types of photography; light in the darkroom, dealing with the techniques of developing, printing and enlarging; and how to put your snaps to work. This little book is a goldmine of information.

WATER—OR YOUR LIFE by Arthur H. Carhart

Conservationist, Arthur H. Carhart, tells the story of water and what it means in the lives of all of us. He has done the job in a masterly fashion, factually, and dramatically. He tells of our dependence on water; our waste of it; our abuse of it through the pollution of streams; our denudation of watersheds that no longer hold the water or the soil it runs over. The book is packed with information, warnings, and signposts to help us co-operate with water so that we may survive.

Published by Lippincott Co. Price $3.50

VERMONT, BEAUTIFUL AND UNSPOILED?

The Vermont Roadside Council is busily at work endeavoring to keep Vermont beautiful and unspoiled. Towns in Vermont are being encouraged to take "LOCAL OPTION" governing the use of roadside advertising. Some fifty towns will make the try at the next town meeting.

Vermont has scenery to sell. Thousands of visitors come to the state to enjoy its grandeur and colorful beauty. The highways serve as windows of the state; why block the view with unsightly curtains?

The simple ordinance recommended to be voted on is, "No outdoor advertising structures, devices or displays shall be erected, reerected, or maintained within the town of . . . . . . . . without written permission of the board of selectmen."

Vermont is perhaps caught in the bill board rash to a lesser degree than some states. But it is rapidly catching the fever, and sore spots are breaking out all over.

It is a known fact that aside from marring the beauty of a view, billboards along heavily traveled roads create a serious traffic hazard. To read a message from a billboard the driver must take his eyes and his attention from the road. It may be for only a second, but a car going 60 miles an hour covers 88 feet in one second; at 40 miles per hour, 59 feet. Too often this means collision and even death.

SNOWFALL BY NIGHT
By RUTH SEYMOUR VESELY

Go forth when strangely velvet ground
Receives the steps without a sound,
And search the dark until you see
The glory of an ancient tree
Renewed by phantom veils of white.
Your burdened heart, on such a night
Of slow, cool peace must surely now
Weigh less than snowdrift on a bough,
And Heaven seem a nearer place
As unseen wings caress your face.
Walk alone by night to know
The healing benison of snow.
