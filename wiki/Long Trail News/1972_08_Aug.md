BUSINESS MEETING

How to accommodate the thousands of people who are using Vermont's Long Trail system and still provide a quality experience without destroying the environment was the recurring question that arose at the 1972 Annual Meeting of the Green Mountain Club held May 27th at the historic Meeting House in Weathersfield Center, Vermont. The Green Mountain Club is the founder of the Long Trail and continues to be the organization responsible for its supervision.

Green Mountain Clubbers were informed of two statistics that were evidence of the increased use of the Long Trail system. One was that the last edition of the Long Trail Guidebook is now sold out despite the fact that the Club had published 10,000 guidebooks just one year ago in anticipation of it lasting two to three years. The other was that total membership of the Club had increased by seventeen percent in one year even though there had been no unusual efforts to increase membership. There are currently 3,328 members in the Club.

Several new programs were announced at the meeting that will help to deal with the increasing number of people using the trail system. The main undertaking will be a greatly expanded "Caretaker Program" at the more heavily used shelters on the Long Trail. OPINION PLEASE?

Herbert Ogden a Killington Section member and frequent contributor to the NEWS, chaired a lively discussion following the Annual Business Meeting. The provocative topic was basically the duties and responsibilities of the Main Club to the Sections and vice versa—the obligation of the Sections to the Main Club.

A week previous to this discussion Herb expressed his opinion to Killington Section President Ray Secor as follows:

"RFD #1, Windsor, Vermont
May 21, 1972

Ray:

I have concluded, after Saturday's meeting, that I can no longer volunteer my wholehearted services for the Green Mountain Club as a member of the Killington Section and now tender my resignation as director and shelter chairman of the Killington Section. This is not a sudden decision. In recent years it has seemed to me that an unhealthy trend has been strengthening as Sections falter and Main goes "big time."

I am inclined, in any such situation, to go either whole hog or get out. I find this trend so disheartening, over a period of years, that I can no longer go whole hog.

As I indicated in my "memo" on Saturday and sought to point up during the seminar, I feel that the present trend can lead only to the Vermont Parks Department. This trend involves standardization of too many facets of trail and shelter maintenance, directives from Main, permission from Main rather than advice, salaried executive secretary, the executive-committee-of-the-executive-committee (Advisory Board?), a stifling of individual initiative and responsibility in the Sections, a position that the Sections are creatures of the Main Club rather than the other way around—and several other considerations.

I have, in recent years, spent considerable time and money on the Long Trail as an active member of the Killington Section, in hopes that my perception of that trend might be clouded. That hope no longer exists. I do not wish to play an active role in the dissolution of the volunteer organization once known as the Green Mountain Club.

Herbert Ogden

Do other members have opinions relative to this topic?

RE: END-TO-END REPORTS

The Long Trail Guidebook lists as the End-to-End requirement "... a brief written summary of the highlights of his End-to-End achievement." Several times lately I have received a report, written on a form supplied to Scouts and others to help them keep track of their progress. Unfortunately, the total record often consists of dates opposite the various sections, with occasional comments such as "no trouble" or "good view."

This is not a satisfactory report. I would like a little more detail than "July 1970" or "May 1971." Some people write several pages, including shelters used, times and distances travelled, trail and shelter conditions, etc. This is very useful, as well as interesting. However, the report does not have to be that long. A one page report would be satisfactory, as long as it includes the "highlights" of the trip.

IRA T. ELLIS, JR., Chairman
End-to-End Committee

APPALACHIAN TRAIL CONFERENCE

Over 500 people attended the Appalachian Trail Conference held at Plymouth State College in Plymouth, N.H. on June 16, 17 & 18, 1972. This Conference marked the 50th Anniversary of the Appalachian Trail.

Many of our own GMC members attended. Several were involved in the proceedings in various ways. Gardiner Lane led a very successful Group Discussion workshop on Saturday titled "How to Prevent Undesirable Trail Use." The ideas developed during this and the other workshops will be studied by ATC Committees and influence the future development of the Trail.

One of the highlights of the meeting was a Field Workshop trip to Mt. Liberty to view the Concentrated Use Area around Liberty Spring we have heard so much about. 90 people made the hike up the mountain for this workshop.

Governor Sherman Adams, former Governor of N.H. and aide to President Eisenhower, was Toastmaster. All of the speakers were most interesting and inspiring. The theme of the Conference was Preserving the Natural Environment of the Appalachian Trail During the Next 50 Years.

The facilities provided by the College, both for dormitories and meeting rooms were very comfortable and convenient. The cafeteria served excellent meals.

The three days of meetings were followed by a week's schedule of hikes and activities in the White Mountains.

Many thanks are due our neighbors, The Appalachian Mountain Club for hosting this excellent conference. The next meeting will be in the Tennessee area in 1975.

ROBERT W. ATTENBOROUGH

BRATTLEBORO SECTION

On March 26, 1972, thirty-two members of the Brattleboro Section gathered during the afternoon and early evening at the home of Arthur and Carol Westing in Westminster West. Those who came early hiked up the pinnacle, about six miles over snow covered fields and mountain terrain. Some traveled on cross-country skis or snowshoes. Those who found the snow too deep for hiking, walked along some of the back country roads and saw many birds.

About 6:00 P.M. everyone gathered in the house for the annual Sugar-On-Snow party. After the syrup finally reached the sugaring-off stage, the group enjoyed the repast of sugar-on-snow, pickles, doughnuts, and coffee, sitting around the cheery fireplace in the living room or just talking in the kitchen. Everyone had a most enjoyable evening.

Between the second week of May and the second week of June, several work parties, in spite of the rain, managed to clear trail between Bourn Pond and Bromley Shelter. More has to be done at Bromley camps and some bridges need attention, but the trail is cleared and the camps are usable.

Many members in small groups have been hiking various sections of the trail, some working for their end-to-end certificates.

LOUISE H. DUNKLEE, Reporter

BURLINGTON SECTION

Burlington section lodges Taft, Butler, Taylor and Bolton have been staffed by caretakers for the summer. A praiseworthy innovation is the two girls who have been hired as caretakers at Taft Lodge. Kenn Boyd reported that June's unpleasant weather (wet and cold) slowed hiker traffic considerably. A limited amount of trail work is done by caretakers as well as their other duties.

Ruth and Newell Briggs led a group of about twenty on one of the more challenging hikes at the beginning of the season. Leaving Burlington at 7 a.m. for the White Mts., they lunched on Mt. Liberty, sharing summit and view with about fifty additional hikers. They completed the three-sided hike by descending the Flume, a scenic but steep mile long section.

SALLY S. SPEAR, Reporter

KILLINGTON SECTION

Hikers on the part of the Long Trail that is maintained by the Killington Section of GMC will again this year find a caretaker in the Pico-Cooper-Tamarack Shelter area. Be prepared to pay a small fee (slightly less for GMC members who can produce a current membership card) to stay overnite at these shelters.

PIONEER VALLEY SECTION

Two joint hikes with the AMC, one with the Connecticut Section, one with the Bennington Section and a PVS hike with our President Bob Boucher have been the total of our pleasure hikes to date. The rest of our time has been spent on work parties with the heaviest and hardest work being at Congdon Camp and vicinity. One party went south to clear the trail of a jungle of downed trees left by loggers. Our work there was made a whole lot easier by the two chain saws which Bob Poole and Fred Perry carried in and used. On that trip it was found that some low down thief had stolen our stove at Congdon, so the next work party in that vicinity saw Fred carrying in another stove to replace it. Also at that time a pit was dug and the toilet moved, and the trail cleared to the north. The trail north and south of Seth Warner has also been cleared and an overflow tent site has been roughed out and marked 200 feet north of the shelter.

At this writing (the end of June) the trail is a mess from the rains we have had and the heavy traffic that is using it. More work is necessary, in places, because of the high water level. Also in the planning stages are a couple of Primitive Camp Sites to help relieve the congestion at the shelters.

We welcome members-at-large and other hikers on our hikes and work parties and information on our schedules may be obtained from our activities chairman, Anne Boucher, 148 Dorset Street, Springfield, Mass. 01108. Telephone (413) 736-4078.

EDNA WILLIAMS, Reporter

CAN GMCers HELP?

The following letter was received recently and is published in the hope that some GMCers will be able to assist Donovan Bowley with his project.

"For several years, I have been engaged in an ecological-botanical project on Mt. Mansfield in Vermont. The early forest cover of the region is of particular interest to me.

"Would it be possible to make an appeal through you and the Long Trail News to GMC members who have recollection of the early days of the Long Trail on Mt. Mansfield, or of the construction of the lodges there? If anyone has dated photographs I might examine, I would be especially grateful to them. Logging operations, clearings, wood roads, and fires, as well as wind/ice storms that caused extensive forest damage are of interest as well.

"I find that early visitors to the region have made scanty reference to these subjects, and feel that the best way to learn more about them is to discuss the mountain with interested parties familiar with the region.

"As I have been working on a flora of the region as well, I would be greatly indebted to members of the Club who are Botanists and who have collected in the Mt. Mansfield-Smuggler's Notch area if I might be allowed to examine their collections for pertinent material."

GMCers who can offer help should contact Donovan R. Bowley, Boston University, Department of Biology, 2 Cummington St., Boston, Mass. 02215.

SUGGESTIONS FOR USE OF THE LONG TRAIL BY
BACKPACKING GROUPS

1. THE GUIDE BOOK OF THE LONG TRAIL, with its detailed description of the Long Trail System, is a must for any trail trip longer than a Sunday walk. This is especially true for the leader charged with the safety of young people. The LT GUIDE is obtainable for $3.00 p.p. from the GMC below).

2. Complete pre-trip planning is of utmost importance. It can be the difference between a rewarding and a disappointing experience. Leave a detailed day-to-day itinerary, including all escape routes, with some responsible person. Consider total mileage, trail terrain, proper selection of food, clothing and equipment. Be prepared for rain.

3. Avoid weekends and holidays; also, those overcrowded areas like Camel's Hump, Mansfield, Stratton Pond and Little Rock Pond when possible.

4. Competent qualified leadership is crucial. Leaders should have basic first aid knowledge, and training in trail and mountain leadership.

5. Small groups are essential for adequate supervision and for enjoyment of the Long Trail. A maximum of ten recommended, with one leader for each five hikers. Larger groups should be split into two self-sufficient groups of ten or less, spaced at least a half day apart, or starting at opposite ends of the proposed route.

6. The time has passed when space at a shelter was certain. Shelters are limited in number and their capacity usually small, averaging 6-8 hikers. Groups must plan to sleep out, and so must carry lightweight plastic tarps or tents. It is unfair to crowd other hikers in the shelters.

7. The maximum stay at any shelter area is two nights. Groups planning longer stays, or not backpacking, should use those camping areas adjacent to the side trails of the Long Trail System.

8. Be courteous. Share the shelter area with other hikers, making the late comer welcome. Groups must refrain from excessive noise and disturbing behavior.

9. Fire permits are not needed, but groups must use only those existing fireplaces at shelter and designated camping areas. Better, use the lightweight trail stoves or sterno. These are required in high elevation areas like Camel's Hump and Mansfield where all open fires are prohibited.

10. Live trees must not be cut or damaged. At high elevations, even deadwood shouldn't be disturbed. The traditional evening campfire can no longer be justified in the interest of clean air and preserving the remaining forest.

11. Increased use of the trail has necessitated having caretakers at many of the shelters. A small fee is requested for support of this program.

12. Please protect the water supplies. All washing (personal and dishes) must be done away from the water supply—not in it.

13. Leave nothing but footprints—trash and garbage detract from enjoyment of the shelter sites and the trail environment. Remove trail litter and leave campsites clean. All dumps are being removed. If you carry it in, carry it out.

14. Seek information. The Green Mountain Club will be pleased to answer trail inquiries and provide advice to those groups planning Long Trail trips.
