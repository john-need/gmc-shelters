PROPOSED BY-LAW CHANGES

The following proposed by-law changes were reviewed by the Board of Trustees at its January 20 meeting and will be submitted to the membership for approval at the Annual Meeting on May 26, 1973.

A. It is proposed to amend Article I as follows:
     Change: Add 8. A person may become a Contributing Member by making an annual contribution to the Corporation. A Contributing Member may not vote or hold any office. No age limits shall pertain to this class of membership.
     Reason: A new Section 8 is proposed to establish a Contributing Member classification. This classification would be for persons desiring to contribute to the maintenance of the trail and shelter system they have used and enjoyed but who have no interest in the activities and operations of the Club.

B. It is proposed to amend Article II, Sections 1 & 4 as follows:
     Change: Delete "22" in each section.
     Reason: Delete the stated total number of Trustees everywhere that it appears. It is not necessary to state this number because the number of Trustees from each Section, etc. is specified elsewhere in the by-laws. We have been amending the by-laws too often, just to change this un-needed number.

C. It is proposed to amend Article II, Section 4, as follows:
     Change: Members-at-large "3" to Members-at-large 4.
     Reason: Members-at-large are our largest single group of members and they are under-represented. The intention is to propose the raising of this number by one a year until the total is 6. The Trustees will then cause two to be elected each year.

D. It is proposed to amend Article II, Section 4, as follows:
     Change: "Green Mountain National Forest Service" to read Green Mountain National Forest.
     Reason: To provide for the proper designation of the Green Mountain National Forest (two places in this section). This is an oversight which has gone unnoticed or unmentioned for many years.

E. It is proposed to amend Article IV, Section 7, as follows:
     Change: Second sentence, new words in italics "The Executive Advisory Council shall consist of not more than nine members of the Club, at least two thirds of whom shall be present or former Trustees, and shall include the President, the Clerk, and not more than seven additional members."
     Reason: Article IV, Section 7, was adopted in May 1972, with the understanding this would be added to restrict the Executive Advisory Council to members of the Club.


A MESSAGE TO ALL WHO MAINTAIN REGISTER BOOKS ON THE TRAIL AND IN THE SHELTERS

When new register books are placed on the Trail and in the shelters (and during the periodic checking of these books), please furnish pencils with No. 1 or No. 2 leads only (preferably the softer No. 1 lead).
     In the evaluation of dozens of register books, and the tabulation of valuable statistics from these books, I have found, regretfully, that most entries made with hard pencils (No. 2½ or No. 3 leads) are illegible and of little value, especially if those entries were made in damp weather.
     Therefore, all of you Trail workers involved with register books, please furnish pencils with soft leads so that all entries will be legible and will thus permit us to utilize this valuable information in our maintenance program and trail-use studies.

BEN ROLSTON

By comparison for the last five years, we had 26 in 1968, 49 in '69, 50 in '70 and 68 in 1971. Fourteen states, the District of Columbia, and Canada were represented. As nearly as I can tell, there were 74 adults and 31 juniors. 56 were At Large members, 8 were section members, and 41 were non-members, or did not indicate their membership.

It is interesting to note that the number of juniors is the same this year as in 1971, while the adults went from 37 to 74. This was partly due to the almost complete lack of youth group completions.

The youngest member, Allan Bristow, completed the trail just before his 9th birthday. His walks included a number of overnight trips, on which he carried his own equipment and some of the common equipment. The oldest member, and proud of it, was Lloyd Shildneck. He started his End to End hikes at age 70, and completed the trail three years later.

It is a good thing there is no time limit for completion. One member took 52 years, and another 40 years. Several people completed the trail in 16 days of walking. However the fastest time I have seen, although not continuous, was that of two young men from Connecticut. They spent six days in 1971, and nine days in 1972, for a total of only 15 days on the trail. This record is even more impressive since they did the southern 90 miles, with packs, in only three days.

I would like to thank all those who sent in detailed comments on the trails, shelters, caretakers, etc. These have been passed on to the proper people. I apologize, for the GMC, for some of the wet areas on the trail, but as most of you know, this was the wettest hiking season I have ever seen.

IRA T. ELLIS, JR.

OUR FORMER SHELTERS

Many of the hikers who have completed the entire 2000 miles of the Appalachian Trail have called the Green Mountain Club the "shelter-buildingest club on the entire Appalachian Trail." I'll buy that! We have torn down probably more shelters than any other club has ever built.

As a result of many hours of studying all twenty editions of our Guide Book, and the reading of copies of the Long Trail News dating way back to 1922, I have compiled a listing of what I call "former" shelters—shelters which once were but now aren't.

I am herewith submitting my listing, for three reasons. First, to provide some history of our immense shelter-building projects of the past six decades. Second, to help bring back some memories of those old shelters to those readers who used them (or perhaps helped to build them). But my third reason, and the most important one, is to ask our readers to help evaluate the accuracy of this listing. Perhaps some "old timers" can provide dates of construction for those shelters which I have indicated as "date unknown." There may be inaccuracies in some of the dates given. AND, some deletions or additions may be required to update the listing and improve its accuracy.

So, HELP! I welcome your comments (send them to 323 12th Street, Schenectady, New York 12306). Now, start reading (and reminiscing)...

Dear Editor,

   I am greatly concerned over the inadequacy of the Wilderness Preservation Act of 1964. There are two important areas which need careful reconsideration. First, the law allows the extension of the mining and mineral leasing laws until December 31, 1983. This is contrary to the basic intention of the Wilderness Act. Which is to preserve the virgin lands in there natural state for future generations. Secondly, the acquisition of new lands to the wilderness system is too slow. As a result virgin forests we so need are being raped and assimilated into commercial forest never to be of its former and greater value.
   The need for wilderness lands is great. Not only are these lands invaluable as far as aesthetic qualities to the urbanized American, but also for scientific study and the preservation of wildlife.
   According to the Fish and Wildlife Service, 78 sp. of mammals, birds, fish and reptiles in the United States are endangered to the point of extinction. We must act now, by relaying our concerns to the Legislature.
   As a member of the Club, I would greatly appreciate your cooperation by informing the other members of this pending doom to prospective wilderness areas.

Sincerely,

STEPHEN MOORE

Dear Editor,

   So Little Rock Pond Shelter had to be moved because it was "in the wrong place," with resulting "rapidly deteriorating conditions." We are two End-to-Enders who did most of our trip in the years 1943-1945, and for us the few days we spent at "Little Rocky," as we have come to call it, were about as close as we have ever come in our lives to earthly paradise. This island gem, set in conditions of intimacy with waters lapping at its foot and the mountains across the pond ever-visible from the shelter, with its shelter that opened out to rocks that sloped down to the pond, made possible a life lived equally in land, sky and water.
   Well, what was paradise for two, three or five people might become hell for twenty or thirty or how many? But let it be recorded that for two people this was once the most ideally located of all camps and shelters on the Long Trail. In a different context, Talleyrand summed up feelings just like ours: "Ceux qui n'ont pas vecu sous l'ancien regime n'ont pas connu la douceur de vivre." (those who have not lived under the old regime do not know how sweet life can be).

Sincerely yours,

Mildred Siegel
Armand Siegel

Editor's Note: Little Rocky has been a favorite of many GMC'ers including yours truly, and no doubt many concur with Mr. & Mrs. Siegel in their fond recollections.

AMC ANNUAL RANGE HIKE

The Annual Range Hike, scheduled for July 14 thru 21, 1973, will provide an opportunity to hike approximately 50 miles of scenic trails between Franconia Notch and Pinkham Notch in the White Mountains of New Hampshire, including 35 miles of Appalachian Trail, and a chance to reach the summit of 10-20 of the 4,000-footers.
   The AMC Hut System will be used for bed and board, thus pack weight can be kept to a minimum. The prospectus will include the schedule of the hike and a list of necessary equipment.
   Two sections are planned for 1973, and each section will accommodate 36 persons (18 male and 18 female).
   Section ABLE will traverse the Presidential Range starting at Franconia Notch on Saturday, July 14, ending at Pinkham Notch Camp on Saturday, July 21. Section ABLE will be conducted over rough and often steep mountain trails and should be attempted only by those in good physical condition who are accustomed to strenuous exercise.
   The companion group, Section CHARLIE, will be based at Pinkham Notch Camp (on Route 16) during the period July 14-21 with the exception of three optional nights in easy-to-reach AMC Huts. This group will take short day trips arranged to be less demanding from a physical standpoint but giving an introduction to the White Mt. trails, including opportunities to view the area from above treeline.
   The cost will be the same for each section—$135 for members of AMC, ADK, ATC, GMC, Sierra Club and $140 for others. Registration fee of $25, deductible but not refundable, is due June 10, 1973.
   For further information contact Range Hike Co-ordinator: Cecil M. Jones, 72 Chester St., Worcester, Mass. 01605. Phone: 617-852-5886.

SKI TOURING COUNCIL'S PROGRAM

The Ski Touring Council is celebrating ten years of ski touring promotion. During the 1972-73 winter sport season, the non-commercial membership committee prepared a schedule that included one hundred sixty workshops and tours in New England, New York, Pennsylvania and Canada.
   The Council especially recommends that beginners attend a workshop where they teach the essentials of ski touring technique, equipment, clothing, waxing, safety measures and arrange for special easy tours.
   The Council's Schedule, besides listing events, gives precise information on meeting points, length and descriptions of each tour with the names and telephone numbers of the leaders.
   Leaders are all volunteers, dedicated experienced ski tourers and many are members of outdoor and mountain clubs and of the Nordic Ski Touring Patrol.
   The Schedule includes a special chapter devoted to the forty-three areas which have ski touring instruction and equipment rental throughout the season. It also lists the cross country races sanctioned by the Eastern Division of the U.S. Ski Association and gives details of the "Ski for a Medal" project.
   A copy of the Schedule is available from Rudi Mattesich, President, Ski Touring Council, Troy, Vermont 05868, at a cost of $2.00 (sent with order) to cover production costs and mailing.
   The Council also publishes a Ski Touring Guide with information on all aspects of ski touring equipment, technique, safety measures, waxing, how to cut and mark trails, how to set up a ski touring program. The major part of the Guide is devoted to listing and describing the ski touring places in the east. The cost of the Guide is $1.75 (sent with order) to cover printing and mailing.
   During the past ten years of promoting touring, the Ski Touring Council has played a major role in making ski touring the fastest growing ski sport nationally and of helping many rediscover the great, beautiful winter world.

BRATTLEBORO SECTION

Fifteen members of the Brattleboro Section visited Lye Brook Falls near Richville, Vermont (just south of Manchester Depot). The falls were approached by two groups. One group led by Rex Coleman walked up the old lumber road from Richville about 2½ miles to the old overgrown railroad bed which the group followed about 1½ mile down to the falls. The other group led by Frank Dearborn, our section president, started at Branch Pond and hiked about 7 miles down the old lumber road to the old railroad bed and into the falls. Both groups met at the falls, ate lunch and hiked down to Richville.

The 150 foot high falls, half hidden by tall trees was pretty and interesting with the crumbling layer rock formation and spectacular height.

GUIDE BOOK CHANGES

On Page 142 of the 1971 Edition of the Guide Book of the Long Trail, there appears a notice which reads:

"Changes in the Trails and Shelters will be published in the Long Trail News in a form suitable for cutting out and pasting into your Guide Book."

A listing of GUIDE BOOK CHANGES appeared in the February 1972 issue of the Long Trail News, and some of these changes were incorporated into the Second Printing of the Twentieth Edition of the Guide Book in 1972. Further changes, occurring in 1972, and details of planned relocations are given in the following listing:

Page 29: A primitive camping area has been established near Seth Warner Shelter, with tenting spaces, fireplaces, and toilet facilities being offered for overflow camping use.

Pages 34 through 36: Completion of the relocation between Route 9 and Glastenbury Mountain Shelter is planned for 1973. Hikers should not attempt to follow temporary plastic-strip marking; when the route is ready for travel, it will be adequately blazed and signed, and the former route will be abandoned. Fay Fuller Camp has been removed. NO CAMPING OF ANY KIND is permitted in the Fay Fuller area or up-stream along Bolles Brook. Until completion of the relocation (and its new shelter), hikers should be aware of the 12-mile distance between Congdon Camp and Glastenbury Mountain Shelter.

Page 42: The completion of the relocation between the Stratton Road and Stratton Pond is planned for 1973. Hikers must use the present route via the dirt road and Grout Job until blazes and signs indicate that the new route is ready for travel.

Page 54: Willis Ross Camp was destroyed by fire in the spring of 1972. Replacement plans are indefinite. A primitive camping area will be in use at the Pond, a caretaker will be in residence, and a nominal camping fee will be charged during the 1973 season.

Page 56: The two shelters at Little Rock Pond were moved to new sites in 1972. The Island Shelter is now located 1/10 mile north of the outlet of the Pond, on a hill just east of the Trail. Lula Tye Shelter is now located 2/10 mile south of the Pond, on a hill just east of the Trail. The island bridge has been removed, and the trail around the west side of the Pond has been abandoned. The Trail to Green Mountain now starts at the USFS signpost at the north end of the Pond.

Page 116: Sterling Pond Lodge was destroyed by fire early in 1972. A new open-front shelter was built in the fall of 1972 to replace the Lodge.

General Notes: The caretaker program will be continued, and perhaps expanded, in 1973, and a nominal camping fee will be charged when caretakers are in residence. Hikers are again urged to comply with the "Carry In-Carry Out" policy of litter disposal; to carry shelter materials to alleviate overcrowded shelter conditions; and to carry pack stoves to help conserve the dwindling supply of firewood at heavy-use areas. See trail-use Suggestions on Page 49 of the Guide Book.

SUCH IS THE WAY

It was a very cold night with much hurrying and scurrying of clouds. The moon kept peeking out once in awhile just to see how her labor was coming. There was a loud clap of thunder up near the summit. Not a very good night to be born, but, none-the-less, it was his night. There was another clap of thunder that lit the night for a fraction of a second. At this moment he was born; small, naked, and yet handsome. For a moment there was no up or down; he did not understand what was happening to him or where his mother was. He blacked out, for how long he did not know, but when he awoke, he was on the ground just below the summit.

He felt very strange lying there, but soon the moon shone down, and that cheered him up considerably. He began to take stock of his world. It was very quiet and peaceful on the mountain. He looked up and saw that the sky was filled with strange little points of light. What a strange and exotic world I have been born into, he thought to himself.

He lay there in the darkness looking up at the points of light for a long time. Then slowly he became aware of a sound that was not made by the wind. It was a soft sound that drew slowly closer, until at last he could see the strange creature that was responsible for it. At first he was afraid of the creature because of its great size and its enormous flat feet. But something in the way it moved slowly and quietly toward the summit dispelled his fear and turned it slowly into curiosity. Why had this creature come up the mountain in the dark to stand on the summit? He wondered at the sweet smell. It came from the small clouds that the creature made from the piece of wood stuck in its mouth. And then he saw a strange glow come from the piece of wood. Could this be where the points of light came from? Maybe this strange creature wandered over the summits leaving a trail of light points in the sky behind it. He thought about this for a moment, and when he looked up the creature was gone. All that was left were its enormous tracks and a trail of light points in the sky.

He was alone again and for a long time he thought about the creature and of the light points. The light points! Where are they? The sky is no longer dark; it is pink. Such a strange world. He began to feel strange as the sky became lighter. It was a good feeling, he decided. As the feeling grew stronger, he could not contain himself, and so he danced and laughed.

He carried on for a long time and then began to feel tired and sick. He could not understand it. The sky was so bright; he had been so happy. Now he was dying. Such a strange world.

He died quietly up there near the summit, and, in the spring, the spring beauties will grow on his grave.

Such is the way with snowflakes.

EDWIN F. ROBERTS, End-to-Ender No. 459
