TRUSTEES MEETING
APRIL 17, 1948

RUTLAND LIBRARY
   The meeting was called to order by President Goddard.
   The trustees present were White, Green, Wiley, Holden, Goddard, Mould, Puffer, Boyce, Tye, and Perkins.
   The minutes of the previous meeting were read and approved.
   Mr. Holden reported for the Winter Sports Committee. Due to apparent lack of interest in organized skiing, he recommended that the Club discontinue its affiliation with the Eastern Amateur Ski Association subject to the approval of the May meeting.
   Mr. Holden added that affiliation with the U.S.E.A.S.A. entitles all sections of the G.M.C. to the benefits of affiliation, which include proficiency tests, participation in competitions and Ski Patrol activities. Individual members of the sections are entitled to join the U.S.E.A.S.A. under the reduced fees which are available only to members of an affiliated club.
   The various Sections reported as follows: Mr. Goddard for Bennington and Manchester, Mr. Green for Worcester, Mr. Field for Killington, Mr. Fay (by letter) for Proctor, Mr. White for Pleiad, Prof. Puffer for Burlington and St. Albans, and Mr. Mould for Sterling.
   Mr. Boyce reported as Treasurer and for the House Committee. Both reports were accepted. The financial condition of the club was shown to be satisfactory, though not affluent.
   Miss Tye reported as Corresponding Secretary. It was voted to order 15,000 Long Trail folders provided the State pay half the expense and take half the folders.
   A request for permission to tear down the garage and employee's dormitory buildings at the Lodge was granted. The disposition of the salvaged lumber is to be subject to the approval of the House Committee.
   A request for permission to serve all legal beverages at the Long Trail Lodge was discussed at length.
   It was moved that approval be given if arrangements could be made satisfactory to both the House Committee and the Treadway management. This motion was lost by a vote of 5 to 4.

From an old issue of the News we cull this so-called poem, which was there printed with the note, "Found in an old Lodge Register." Does anyone claim it?

ODE TO THE BEAN

Hail to thee, gentle bean!
Mere vegetable thou never wert,
But, even tho encased in tin thou com'st,
Like to the grub once called ambrosia,
Whereon the Gods of old did sup,
Thou art.

Packed full of vitamins and calories and food,
Besides tomato sauce and pork,
Thou givest to the legs more strength,
The lungs more wind to climb,
Or curse aloud the cool and smoky fire.
Thou fillest up the heart with peace,
And not alone the heart is filled, when thou
Dost from thy heated can pour forth
Into the quickly emptied plate.
But Ah, Alas, and Oh, Alack for him
Who, in the said tight can dost fail
To make an orifice, or punch a hole,
Before he sets it on the stove to heat.
For that he failed in this small thing
Full many a hardy hiker has gone supperless to bed,
And cabins have within with beans been sprayed.

Mainstay of armies, and of navies too,
What say the Scriptures,
That man cannot live by bread alone;
'Tis true!
He must have beans!

PUBLICITY!

It is not often that the Green Mt. Club rates the leading editorial in a prominent Vermont newspaper, but here, for your edification and enlightenment, is such a one from the Rutland Herald of March 11. The Herald has always been a friend of the club, and we like the Herald too.

LONG TRAIL MAINTENANCE

During its 38 years of existence, the Long Trail has grown increasingly important as one of Vermont's best known attractions. It is annually enjoyed not only by many Vermonters, but by hundreds of nature lovers from other states and even other countries. But like anything else, it requires care and attention year after year to keep it attractive and in the best possible condition.

A plan for reconditioning the trail and its shelters this summer was recently proposed by T. N. Goddard, president of the Green Mountain club. He suggested an August Camp of members who would work in groups and spend from four days to a week at a camp or lodge. They would do what repair work was necessary during part of the time, and also enjoy hiking, mountain climbing and other attractions of the trail.

It's still too early to know how much work will be necessary this year. Not until the snow is gone from the trail, usually early in June, will it be possible to see how much damage has been done and what shape the trail is in. But there's sure to be a need for repair and reconditioning.

The Long Trail extends the entire length of this state, covering more than 267 miles. More than 50 shelters have been erected along the trail, from eight to 10 miles apart, ranging from open lean-toes to closed lodges made of logs. A New York City resident hiked over 40 miles of the trail this winter. He reported that porcupines had been active in most of the shelters. But this was the least of the damage usually done.

Each year there is re-blazing to be done, blown-down trees to be removed, brush cut away and shelters repaired. While some parts of the trail may require little attention, in other places considerable work is necessary to put the trail in good condition.

The Green Mountain club is concerned with maintenance of the trail. This organization has members in practically every state in the Union. The club is composed of sections, and these sections are responsible for certain parts of the trail. The New York City section, with more than 200 members, sends some of its members to Vermont to work on one area. The Worcester, Mass. section is responsible for another part of the trail, and there are many Green Mountain sections in Vermont, each with its own area.

Miss Lula Tye, secretary of the Green Mountain club, reports that the trail attracts the very finest type of person. She says that it appeals to those who like to get away "from the hustle and bustle of modern living and get close to nature." She declares that professional people form a large part of the membership, and that the members take a vigorous personal interest in the trail. Some return after their first visit to work on the trail because they know what it means to them and to others like themselves.

This sort of interest can't be bought. It is spontaneous and it testifies to the importance of the Long Trail as a Vermont attraction of the first order.

The Green Mountain club was organized by James P. Taylor, now secretary of the Vermont Chamber of Commerce. He wanted people to be able to see, at close range, the high peaks of the Green Mountains, and the Long Trail was the result of his efforts.

During the war, it wasn't possible to keep up the trail as it should have been. But since the war, maintenance work has been resumed. From every indication, this year the trail will get more attention than it has had in a long time. It should have. The Long Trail is worth all the effort it takes to maintain it as one of Vermont's most important scenic and recreational attractions.

Dear Miss Tye—

in Japan, in the February "Long Trail News" reminds me that the Green Mountain Club is registered in a guest book on top of Fuji—unless American bombers blitzed the shelter hut. I spent a year in Japan back in 1930-31, and climbed Fuji before leaving. It is a 12,000-foot climb, long but not at all hard. You go up part way the first afternoon, spend the night in one of the shelter huts, and start for the top before dawn, to see sunrise from the summit. The only trouble was that there wasn't any sunrise when I was there, but only pouring rain and clouds. However, I went on up, and at the top did what all good pilgrims do—had the hut man stamp my bamboo walking stick with a brand showing I had got there. I registered in the guest book, and identified myself as a member of the Green Mountain Club, New York Section. The hut man and I "had very good time," though neither of us understood much that the other was saying. Japanese are great climbers and hikers, and the winter week-end trains out of Tokyo always looked like snow-trains out of New York, with their forest of skis, and knapsacks bumping everywhere. It made me feel very much at home.

Sincerely yours,

WEB FEET AGAIN

When we wrote the little article "In Defense of Web Feet" for the last issue of THE NEWS we did not realize how many people would be interested in it. From the response to it, it seems evident that there are still several persons whose thoughts on winter outdoor recreation are not exclusively given up to skiing. We have before us four letters from those who read the article. Some of them actually took it to heart. One is from our former president, Walter Atwood, in Palisades Park, N. J., another from Robert Draper in Little Neck, N. Y. and a third from Lucy Bagley, of the Sterling Section in Morrisville. I don't think she would mind my quoting from her letter (I haven't asked her). She says: "After having struggled a number of hours yesterday over ideal snowshoeing terrain on 7 foot skis (propelling myself with poles cut for me by Mr. Morris Greene), I was particularly susceptible to your 'In Defense of Web Feet'. For some time I've had my eye on a pair of good snowshoes. Since reading your essay in THE LONG TRAIL NEWS, I've rung up the party concerned and obtained them. Now for more cheerful hiking!"

The fourth letter was from the Librarian of Dartmouth College (maybe some of our readers didn't realize that this paper was filed in the classic precincts of the Dartmouth Library), Mr. Nathaniel L. Goodrich. Among other things, all interesting, he says: "Although I have been skiing for nearly thirty-five years—in fact it seems probable that I was the first one to ski the Mansfield Carriage Road—I still do quite a bit of snowshoeing. In fact a group of members of the Appalachian Mountain Club have been getting together once a winter for some pretty strenuous mountain climbing on snowshoes . . . . You may be pleased to know that there is someone else who enjoyed your article, and feels the same way you do about snowshoeing. [I am.] Aside from that, my chief excuse for writing you is that I disagree violently with one of your statements . . . You say that 'excellent harnesses can be made at home.' I am quite skeptical about this, and the fact that nearly all snowshoe harnesses are almost useless is to my mind a serious reason for the decline in snowshoeing. I have yet to see a conventional snowshoe harness which will very long give the shoe any rigidity on the foot. These bindings require constant adjusting, and at best they allow the shoe to wobble in all directions." He goes on to explain a special binding made in Boston, which of course makes it summa cum laude with the Appalachian Club boys, and if you want to write to me I'll tell you where he says you can get it. I still think I can make a pretty good pair of harnesses, but I'll hedge to this extent, if I could get the right kind of leather. Since the last man to run a harness shop in Burlington was murdered last winter I don't know whether I could find any in the city or not. What does somebody else think? (I mean, about home-made snowshoe harnesses.)

Also, does anyone wish to dispute Mr. Goodrich's claim to be the first to ski the Mansfield Toll Road? If you do, be sure to have your evidence in good form and properly authenticated.

Burlington Section Outings, as reported in the Burlington Free Press of April 19.

GREEN MOUNTAIN CLUB
HOLDS THREE OUTINGS

Outing Chairman Katharine Collier reports that the local section of the Green Mountain Club's triple outing this past weekend was well attended.

A group of 27 gathered in Colchester Saturday afternoon for a brisk field and woodland ramble around Colchester Pond. Sam Hatfield headed the group.

Entertainment Chairman Kenneth Fisher headed the group's annual maple sugar party Saturday night. The group gathered at the Colchester Grange Hall where the Young People's Society of the Colchester United Church served the Mountain Club followers.

Sixty-seven ate supper and topped it off with the traditional individual plates of snow daubed with syrup. The group then square danced, Harold Collins called for the dances.

The third phase of club activity was in the Hinesburg Hollow area Sunday afternoon. Prof. L. B. Puffer headed a group of 25 that climbed the steep slope from the hollow to the top of Red Rocks. Mr. and Mrs. Al Quance were guests on Sunday afternoon's outing.

On May 2, 37 Burlingtonians enjoyed a trip to the Sterling Section's fine Lodge at Beaver Meadow. There was new snow under foot, but fun elsewhere. Henry Mould, with Mrs. Mould and Freddie, were on hand to welcome the visitors.
