SUMMARY OF THE FEBRUARY 7 TRUSTEES MEETING

The February meeting was attended by 28 trustees, officers, and guests. A brief summary of actions taken, that are not reported elsewhere, is given below.

There was discussion on insurance for the Long Trail Camps and liability insurance. The president will appoint a committee to look further into insurance.

It was voted to order 15,000 "Short Hikes" folders with the GMC authorized to pay approximately $760 for them with reimbursement from the State of Vermont for 10,000.

In order to improve the addressing for the "Long Trail News" and other mailings it was voted to pay $650 for the purchase of new addressing equipment.

The publicity committee was authorized to include the shelter limit usage policy statement of two days, previously adopted by the GMC, on new signs for shelters when the present supply is replenished. This statement will also be included in the new guidebook.

Robert Williams reported on the meeting of the Appalachian National Trails Executive Council. The entire Appalachian Trail has been photographed and the State of Vermont will have a copy. A description will be published in the Federal Register. Many problems regarding the type of agreement and the responsibility for agreements are not worked out.

The trustees decided to support Gov. Davis' "Clean-Up Day" to be held on April 11, with each Section encouraged to include this in their Spring plans. (Since the trustees' meeting the date has been changed to April 18, and it will now be called "Green-Up Day.")

The trustees voted to recommend passage of legislation introduced into the Vermont legislature to provide regulation and control of ecological disturbances at higher elevations.

There was a general discussion of the trail situation on Mt. Mansfield and Mt. Killington. Gondolas are expected to be run in both of these areas this summer. There was also a discussion on the trail relocation in the Glastenbury area.

The cost of publishing the "Long Trail News" will probably be about $800 above the $1200 budgeted this year according to editor Pons. This information led to a discussion on dues, budgets, and the need for a GMC office. The president was instructed to appoint a planning committee to look into establishing an office for the GMC.

The nominating committee was appointed by President Strong: Rod Rice as chairman, Bob Attenborough, and John Rohrbaugh.
GEORGE PLUMB, Clerk

1970 INTERSECTIONAL

The GMC Intersectional this year will be held at Camp Timberlake on Vermont Rt. 100, between Plymouth Union and West Bridgewater, across the Killingtons from Rutland. It will break with tradition in running from Saturday, August 22 through Sunday, August 29 which is NOT Labor Day. Labor Day is at its latest this year and could be too late for camping comfort. In addition, the earlier dates should remove all conflict with school openings.

Timberlake is one of the Farm and Wilderness Camps whose campers built Tamarack Shelter for the GMC last year. The camp is on Woodward Reservoir and will give us ready access to The Long Trail from Bromley Mt. to Middlebury Gap. Other good hiking locations, e.g., Mt. Ascutney and Vermont's east-west portion of the Appalachian Trail are nearby. The sightseeing attractions of Woodstock, Plymouth, Rutland and Proctor are nearby.

We will bring our tents as usual, but there are rough wood shelters and open cabins near the tent sites that can supply added protection for cooking, meals, or sleeping. Camper trailers can also be accommodated.

The program will be much like previous Intersectionals, with emphasis on our anniversary year and on interests of young people. A going farm with animals is part of the camp and will appeal to children. An adjacent lake for water sports, and a broad playing field are available to use up the energies of youngsters, and revive the spirits of oldsters.

It's bound to be fun. Try to come for the whole period, August 22 thru August 29, but we'll be glad to see you any length of time because the Intersectional is really all about Green Mountain Club people.
GRANGER L. KAMMERER, Chairman


TRAILS AND SHELTERS

The newly established Connecticut Section has now assumed responsibility for a section of The LT and it is eagerly looking forward to doing its share. Its assignment includes The Trail from the top of Glastonbury Mt. to the junction of the Kelley Stand Road and The Trail into Stratton Pond, plus maintenance of Caughnawaga Shelter.

Much thought and effort has gone into planning for a relocation of the LT between Rt. 9 and Glastonbury Mt. The Bennington Section is busy finalizing plans and the trail will probably take a westerly route up Bald Mt. and north along the ridge to Glastonbury Mt. This will mean trail work and a new shelter in this area. Meanwhile hikers should use the present route and Fay Fuller Shelter.

Expansion of ski operations continue to create problems on The Long Trail. One of the several trouble areas is at Middlebury Gap where expansion of the Ski Bowl Area has interfered extensively with The Long Trail's route. Hopefully, arrangements for some relocation here can be made before summer.

It is very encouraging and heartening to have an increasing number of applicants for work on The Long Trail Patrol this year. By the time this News reaches you we should be all set and ready to go with a LTP crew this summer.

In view of increasing use of The LT, the idea of re-establishing caretakers at some shelters, in areas where the traffic is particularly heavy, is being considered. Plans are indefinite as the News goes to press and it may not be possible or feasible to do anything this summer. However, it may be necessary in the future. Hikers might find this would offer a more enjoyable time on The Trail and the small charge would be most worthwhile. It is felt it would be most beneficial in terms of trail and shelter use and maintenance.
ROD RICE, Chairman

NEW "SHORT HIKES" FOLDER

A new "Short Hikes on the Long Trail" folder is now available. Our previous edition has been revised and updated, and now appears as a companion folder to "The Green Mountain Club and You."

It includes information regarding The Long Trail System, the GMC and the Guidebook. Also included are descriptions of 7 short hikes on the Trail, with their locations indicated on a map of Vermont.

The GMC sends this folder out in response to general inquiries regarding The Long Trail. It is also distributed by the Vermont Development Department, the U.S. Forest Service and the Department of Forests & Parks.

Since this publication introduces many people to the Trail for the first time, it is important that proper use of the trails and shelters is emphasized. In this revision we have encouraged hikes to scenic points rather than specifically to shelters (these being provided primarily for the overnight hikers) and stressed trail etiquette, such as care with fire; use of only dead wood; safeguarding water supplies; our carry-in, carry-out policy; and the importance of the Guidebook for extended hikes.

Thanks go to George Pearlstein, Ben Rolston and Shirley Strong for the text revision. For its attractive cover design and general layout, we are indebted to Jane Brown, member of the Burlington Section.

NEW ENGLAND TRAIL CONFERENCE

The New England Trail Conference was held on March 28 at the University of Massachusetts in Amherst. It was good to see over thirty GMCers in attendance this year.

During the business meeting, reports were heard from member organizations on trail and shelter conditions. Ben Rolston reported on the Long Trail and trail activities, including plans for our GMC Day May 16. Les Rolston, Executive Director of ATC, brought news of centennial legislation. Maps and descriptions will soon be filed in the Federal Register and major route changes thereafter will require congressional action. He pointed out an increasing interest in AT hiking.

The afternoon sessions were divided into five discussion groups with enough time allotted for each person to attend two. These were—The Appalachian Trail Today, Multiple use of hiking trails, Problems of trip leadership, Maintaining the natural environment, and Trail making and trail standards.

It was resolved that the NETC should oppose, pending information on possible alternate locations, two pump storage sites to be installed under control of Northeast Utilities. These sites are in Falls Village, Conn., and Sheffield, Mass. It was felt that this was a threat to the last wild mountain area in Conn., and to the AT in the town of Mt. Washington, Mass.

In the evening, C. Francis Belcher, Executive Director of the AMC, gave a fascinating lantern-slide lecture called "History of the White Mountains." BETTY CHURCH

MR. GREEN MOUNTAIN CLUB

As we reminisce the past years of the Green Mountain Club, on this its 60th Anniversary, individuals that played an important role in the Club's history come to light. Such men as James P. Taylor "sparked" the idea the "Foot Path in the Wilderness" along the Green Mountain Range from Massachusetts to the Canadian Line, at a meeting in Burlington on March 10, 1910 and fanned the dream into a healthy flame. Then there was Prof. Will S. Monroe who cut his way southward through the forest over the mountains from the Hump towards Battell Forest. He opened the first long link in the starting of the Long Trail. We are grateful to these two men, Judge C. P. Cowles and the many others who pioneered the "Foot Path in the Wilderness."

However, the man who has continually given the greatest number of man hours to the trail movement is one of those "dumb professors from the University of Vermont." (A name fondly dubbed by Storekeeper Maramore of Lowell, Vt. a long time ago). That professor's name is Roy O. Buchanan and he needs no introduction to most of the Green Mountain Club membership, far and wide.

ROY'S WET WEATHER SANDWICH TECHNIQUE!

Roy stumbled into the Green Mountain Club through a self-imposed dare. Back around 1921 he learned that the Burlington Section was having an afternoon walk across the ice to Juniper Island. He figured he'd like to go along to the island too, even though the group could be a "bunch of high-brows." After, Roy remarked: "I went along and they seemed like a great bunch so I've been tagging along ever since." In time he became deeper and deeper involved in the Club, first with the Burlington outings and work trips and then interest spread to the Parent Club and he soon became a familiar figure at the Club's Annual Meetings at The Long Trail Lodge and elsewhere where his helpful influence was felt.

Back in the early days of his initiation The Long Trail was not completed "border to border but almost." There was a stretch from Jay Peak north to the Canadian Border not yet scouted out. In those early days Roy and Brother, Bruce, were doing a lot of hiking here and there. In 1929 Bruce decided to get rid of the "almost" and he and brother Roy went up on several trips to scout and blaze out the last section to the Border. Early in 1931 Professors Charles Doll and Philips Carlton (UVM) cut the route out, thus completing the dream of James P. Taylor. A big celebration by the Club was had as flares blazed from peak to peak along the length of the Green Mountain Range.

The Trustees, at their 1931 Annual Meeting at The Long Trail Lodge, came to the conclusion that the Trail would not be properly serviced by the Section Members alone, and it was voted to establish a so called Long Trail Patrol to work those sections not covered yearly. President Mortimer Proctor gave a boost to the Patrol by presenting it with a new truck, Truck Number 1. Wallace Fay was named chairman of the Patrol Committee, Willis Ross was chairman of the Trails and Shelters Committee. Fay and Proctor figured that Prof. Buchanan had nothing to do during the UVM summer vacation period and sold Roy on the idea of taking over the leadership of The Long Trail Patrol.

THE "DUMB PROFESSOR" AND TRUCK NO. 1

Each July and August from 1931 to 1968 found Roy shaping up his crew and actively directing them as they cleared trails, repaired or built new camps and shelters, and erecting his famous out-houses. These out-houses are masterpieces, known for their uniqueness and often open front designs. As Patrol Boss he directed the building of 37 camps and shelters along the length of The Trail and under his supervision sections of the trail were relocated to improve the trail system from time to time.

23 Hillcrest Road was long the "jumping off place" for The Long Trail Patrol operation with wife, Helen, often the host for lodging and meals. It continues as a focal point for Roy contributes significantly in an advisory capacity to the Long Trail Patrol activities.

When Roy was not out checking a piece of The Long Trail or working it, he was meandering off to the White Mountains, Katahdin or the Adirondacks with his brother Bruce, Lu Puffer (a constant hiking pal) or with his Burlington Section group. The mountains and trails are his second home and love. Lu Puffer and he completed the 46er Club qualification in the Adirondacks together after making many summer and winter trips to the region for that purpose, when trail work was over for the summer. Roy has climbed 104 peaks with altitudes greater than Mt. Philo—wonder how many, hereabouts can top that score...?

He has missed only one or two of the Green Mountain Club's Intersectional events, including week-end and later the Annual Intersectional week-long camps. In all these events he is the "life of the party." His warm fellowship towards all and his quaint stories of the Trail and home country endears him to all that gather around.

Who has not heard how the tires of old Number 1, Long Trail Patrol truck furnished meals to pookies up in Dave Kinnett's sheep pasture (where the Jay Ski development now is)? Who does not know how the Patrol Boss returning with supplies one day found his crew had over sampled Deuso's birch beer? Who has not heard how the Border Patrol hung around camp all day, until The Long Trail crew's return to check if any were [illegible] who illegally come over the Border? Imagine, before the era of Hippies, seeing a big splatter-footed Long Trail Patrol member walking up the middle of main street in fashionable Manchester, barefooted. Roy can tell you about these and many

Roy is said to have claimed that he could cut more trail faster than most anyone else. Daan Zwick agrees, in part, but states: "Someone always had to follow along after and cut out the top branches so that a regular-sized fella could walk through."

VERMONT'S ENVIRONMENTAL LEGISLATION

The 1970 Session of the Vt. General Assembly adjourned April 4th and some significant legislation was passed to protect the state's environment.

A specific bill to regulate and control use of lands above 2500' and highlands of similar fragile ecology was part of the administration's environmental "package." It was strongly endorsed by the Green Mountain Club. This bill passed the House but time ran out in the closing days of the Session and it was not acted on by the Senate. It seems certain to be re-introduced next year.

The need to protect the higher elevations was clearly recognized, however, and protection for these areas was included in the comprehensive environmental bill, H. 417. This bill calls for no development for "commercial, industrial or residential use above 2500' " unless a permit has been obtained.

The environmental bill H. 417 was one of the Administration's major pieces of legislation to provide for environmental control and protection. The bill establishes an Environmental Board and nine District Environmental Commissions and gives them broad powers governing construction or development for commercial, housing or industrial purposes. The Board is instructed to adopt a development plan, a land use plan and an interim land capability plan for the entire state and to hold public hearings to assist in its consideration of these plans. The plans must be submitted to the District Commissions for their comments and to the Governor for his approval. Club members should watch closely the operations of the new Board and the new District Commissions.

Another bill of special interest to the Green Mountain Club (S. 18) was passed this year, having first been introduced for consideration last year. This legislation will allow any of the natural resource departments of the state, or a municipality, to acquire undeveloped land, or rights to land, to protect it from development and preserve it for its scenic qualities and permit its continuation in forest, agricultural or other undeveloped use. It will permit state natural resource agencies, with the approval of the Interagency Committee on Natural Resources, to buy, or accept as a donation, or exchange, or buy and lease back to the prior owner, or lease, or acquire an option to purchase either the land itself or rights to the land, such as easements, etc., limiting the use that can be made of the land. This is enabling legislation and establishes the basic principle. An agency would need funds—or lands suitable for exchange—to take real advantage of the provisions of this act. It is, however, a potentially very important and helpful piece of legislation with much promise for the future protection of Vermont's scenic natural resources—perhaps even The Long Trail! NATIONAL RESOLUTION RE MOUNTAINS

A resolution recognizing and lending support to conservation efforts in Vermont was adopted by the National Wildlife Federation at its Annual Meeting in Chicago in March.

The resolution, submitted by the Vt. Natural Resources Council (Vermont's affiliate member), emphasized that mountain summits and high elevations provide a dominant feature of the landscape, a unique ecological habitat, a fragile environment especially vulnerable to disturbances by man, a valuable source of water, and an exciting and special environment for "recreating."

It was, therefore, resolved that the National Wildlife Federation "hereby recognizes the value of mountain summits and high elevation lands as vital but fragile natural resources and calls upon conservationists and resources managers to support efforts to preserve the mountain profiles for esthetic, ecological, educational, recreational, and inspirational reasons.

Vermont's conservation program to protect the mountain summits and slopes from misuse and to preserve mountain wildland areas is being spearheaded by the newly organized Green Mountain Profile Committee. The GMC has lent its support to this Committee's action program.

IN MEMORIUM

Eugene F. Bamforth died February 26, 1970 of intestinal cancer. Former Vice-President and Trustee of the Green Mountain Club; Long-time member-at-large, a member of Montpelier Section and an honorary life member of the New York Section; many time End-to-Ender, Adirondack 46er, an early member of the New Hampshire 4000-footer Club, and A-T hiker from Maine to Pennsylvania; and a willing and indefatigable trail worker. Gene Bamforth: a quiet shy little man, with a big smile; an institution at annual meetings and intersectionals; a regular consultant on guide book revisions and trail conditions; an incomparable bushwacker and trail-painter.

Gene was already retired (as a civil engineer?) and a legend when I first heard his name in 1950 and met him in 1951. He was a regular advisor to the New York Section's Vermont Trails Chairman and he outlasted several of us; he always knew the trail and shelter conditions before the crew hit the trail in early June. (How, I don't know; it still amazes me as snow usually limits hiking on the Monroe Skyline until late May.) He once suggested that the Monroe Skyline needed new signs, and then drew up a layout for each of these 180 odd signs indicating location and arrowheads; a job that required hiking almost all of the approximately 50 miles of trail in Vermont maintained by the New York Section. When the signs were produced, he installed them; in most cases he had to return a couple of years later to install a second set of signs because of defects in the manufacturing process for the first set. Over a period of 3 years he painted the blazes on this same set of trails; this effort left the landscape unmarked except for the neatly painted 2 by 6 inch area but he returned to his car with paint all over himself including his eyeglasses.

Gene Bamforth was a solitary hiker in all sorts of weather. I ran into him late one fall on Scragg Mountain on a cold and rainy day; he had bushwacked up the ridge on the other side while we got moderately wet just hiking up the shorter trail. In later years, he usually camped in his car, first a Jeep station wagon, then a V-W microbus. Both vehicles he had personally fixed up with some of the comforts of home including running water and I often found him parked along side of a back road, frequently drying his clothes after a day of bushwacking or hiking in the rain. Earlier, he must have been a strong backpacker as he covered a lot of terrain; his name occurred at least once in any L.T. logbook I perused. I believe there isn't a hill in the State of Vermont that he hadn't stood on and there weren't many in New England; he knew all the old logging roads and retired trails, and where the blowdowns were the worst on every prospective trip that I asked him about.

I think one of Gene's favorite areas was the Worcester Mountain Range. The several trails on White Rock, Hunger, and Worcester Mountains were well maintained solely by his efforts. Within the last couple of years, Gene marked a trail across the ridge from Hunger to White Rock which is a very pleasant hike any time of the year.

Gene was a retiring individual who always had a wide and a friendly greeting for a friend. He and Roy Buchanan had a humorous discussion after one annual meeting about whose ridge ought to get moved away from whose cabin or vice-versa; a subject Gene was somewhat sensitive about since he believed that buildings and landmarks should not be named after living persons. But, in a group, he never seemed to be at ease the way he was with only one or two others. He always attended intersectional but usually hiked alone or with Roy MacDonald. Normally, he scouted the trails near the intersectional area just before its first weekend and anybody who wanted the latest information on trail conditions could get it by asking him.

Gene is gone, his long-continuing efforts on behalf of the Green Mountain Club will be missed by many. This observer already misses him.
D.M.W.

My children tell me that this can be done at an expense not to exceed $300.00. Yours faithfully,

(signed) R. W. HULBURD

BURLINGTON SECTION

Our Annual Meeting in January was as enjoyable as ever—good company, good food, good entertainment. The theme of the evening was the history of the Club and the Trail, in line with our 60th Anniversary Year. Table decorations were in the form of mobiles suspended from the ceiling and made up of old Guide Books and outing schedules, and replicas of hiking boots, snowshoes, lodges and other "structures" found along the Trail. Excerpts from old log books kept in lodges and shelters in years past made fascinating reading. Daan Zwick who was caretaker at Taylor Lodge several years ago gave us an informative and entertaining talk on the history of the Mt. Mansfield region illustrated with lantern slides and old photos which he had specially processed for the presentation. Many of these brought comments from members of the audience, particularly our senior member, Roy Buchanan who, at 89, possesses a keen wit and that rare sense of humor that makes him appreciate even a joke on himself. At the business meeting Harris Abbott, Newell Briggs and Russell Farnsworth were reelected President, Vice President and Treasurer respectively, and Marion Holmes was elected Secretary.

In the spring cleaning department—by the time this appears in print not only will work have begun on trail clearing and lodge sprucing up and renovating, but all of Vermont will be a cleaner place. April 18 has been designated Vermont State Clean-Up Day, and we will do our share by picking up rubbish that was buried under snowdrifts all winter along roadsides bordered by the Long Trail. Considerable work will be done on Puffer Lodge, and there is talk of doing away with the dumps at Cascade and Bolton Lodges, asking campers to carry out their "left-overs."

The bunk's-eye view taken by Madeline Miles of Bev Remick shows her stirring that pot of oyster stew for the 17th group of hungry hikers who made the somewhat precarious climb to Taylor Lodge for this annual event. Probably few people are aware of the rather romantic beginnings of this now traditional supper hike. Bev and husband Don have been "serving" the Club all these years, starting during college days before they were married. The outing is quite famous, and was once featured in the New York TIMES. It has become so popular, attracting as many as 62 one year when there was scarcely space to raise spoon from bowl to mouth, that it is now limited to members only.

We're trying a new kind of "activity" this spring—an evening instructional program designed to make our treks through the mountains a little safer than some of them may have been in the past. This hiking and climbing information session will include instruction in first aid, the use of maps, compasses and the Guide Book, and tips on packing and carrying equipment.

Our spring and summer outing schedule has just been released, and if we participate in half the activities listed we should be in good shape by the end of the season—unless we choose only those marked "E." Outings are now rated for us in advance as E (easy), M (moderate), or D (difficult). A good idea we think, so we know how much energy to take along.

ELIZABETH THOMPSON, Reporter

One of the most popular features in the New York Section is our Camp Thendara (T-20) in the Harriman Section of Bear Mountain Park. EQUIPMENT FOR THE LONG TRAIL

"The camp equipage carried is a matter of personal experience and desire, but a good rule is to wear woolen underwear with stout, serviceable overwear. A heavy close buttoning coat is better than a sweater. An extra suit of underwear and several pairs of woolen stockings should be carried if the hike is to last more than a single day. Heavy, high shoes will be found easiest on an all day's hike. Any of the several makes of knapsacks will carry all that one really needs. For dishes a tin or enamel plate, cup, knife, fork and spoon. A hand ax in the party is essential. For sleeping out, a light tent with floor cloth and screening is desirable, and a wool puff of good size will keep you warm. For food, bread, (preferably made up in sandwiches) and bacon will keep you going with little weight to carry; tea, sugar and small cans of condensed milk. Pictures tell an interesting story of trail experiences and it is hoped that a good camera may accompany you on the trip. The Club is always glad to receive photographic prints. Many can be used to good advantage in making lantern slides."

The above excerpt is from The Guide Book of the Long Trail, published in 1917, the first guide book published by the Green Mountain Club.

VICTOR H. JOHNSON, Historian
