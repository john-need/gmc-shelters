  Tescher spoke stirringly of his many years

  bury described the original construction of
  the Shelter, which he and Murray Stevens
  supervised in the early 1960's. The dedica-
  tion ceremony was organized by Jane Cur-

    The portages were really much more than
most of us expected. They were longer,
roughter, and not large enough at either end
for all 12 of our canoes. At one point there
were 32 canoes from 4 groups converging at
one portage, although we generally saw
only a few other people each day.
    Offsetting that was the serenity of the
wilderness, the absence of motors, the fre-
quent swimming stops, and some good fish
 to round out the freeze dried meals. Everyone
had at least one fish dinner. Among the catch
were pickerel, walleye pike, bass, and nor-
thern pike .
    The weather was a delight. There were a
few showers as we searched for our second
day's camp, but Marshall got a fire started
anyway. Then on the 10th day it poured
several times. At one point we had to stop
and drain the canoes. We were already wet
so paddling at least kept us warm. On we
went, until the lightning started. That came
quite fast, almost as fast as we paddled for
the nearest point ofland, which just happen-
ed to be the largest campsite we'd seen yet,
and was a wlecome place to spend the night.
The next morning was cloudy as we return-
ed to the outfitters, but the afternoon was
clear, sunny, and hot, as 8 of our 11 days had
been.
    Returning to civilization was the usual
scramble to cold drinks, ice cream, fresh
meat, and green salads. Capping that off was
the entire group going out for crepes in Old
Montreal, before getting a private car on
Amtrak for the farewell leg of our excursion.
    On Oct . 1, most of the group reunited for
a barbeque and picture sharing marathon in
Jericho, Vermont.

KILLINGTON EASEMENT
           TRANSFERRED

   The controversial 102.03-acre easement
that will enable a relocation of the Long/Ap-
palachian Trail over Little Killington Peak
was transferred by the Sherburne Corpora-
tion into National Park Service ownership on
September 29, 1983. Two views from the
planned route, scheduled for opening next
summer, include a vista from Little Kil-
lington summit toward Shrewsbury Peak,
and a glimpse from a clearing north of Little
Killington toward Mendon Peak .

ON BUILDIN
            The Brattleboro Section is pleased to an-
         nounce the completion of Spruce Peak
         Shelter. The shelter marks more than one
         year of cooperation between the Section, the
         Manchester District, U.S. Forest Service, the
         Rutland Community Correctional Center,
         and the Youth Conservation Corps.
            Spruce Peak Shelter has been built to
         replace Bromley Camp which suffers the af-
         fects of being located too close to a road. In
         the fall of 1982, members of the Section and
         the Forest Service succeeded in finding a site
         with water near the Long Trail on the ridge
         overlooking Manchester at a point 3 Y:I miles
         south of Routes 11 and 30.
            During the past winter, Mark Brown of
         the Brattleboro Section drafted plans for a
         12' x 20' semi-enclosed log structure with a
         4' x 20' front porch. Approval of the plans
         was granted by the Forest Service, and they
         proceeded to haul 9, 000 board feet of red pine
         logs to a point near the building site. In order
         to minimize damage to the area, this was ac-
         complished early in the spring on snow cover.
            A crew from the Rutland Community
         Correctional Center (RCCC) worked
         through a cold, wet spring peeling the logs,
         and milling out lumber on a chain saw rig.
         Their efforts continued through the summer
         and fall, as they have opened up a large view,
         removed stumps, improved drainage, and
         helped expand the shelter site.

A SHELTER
    The Brattleboro Section spent a total of
eight weekends through the spring, summer
and fall building the shelter. With the excep-
tion of the chain saws, all work was ac-
complished with hand tools. Most technical
decisions, and many of the more refined saw
cuts, were made by Mark Brown.
    The first weekend saw the site cleared,
foundation holes dug, stone pillars built, and
the four sill logs installed. During this time,
"Mark's Rule" was that if two workers could
lift one of the foundation stones, it wasn't big
enough! Almost as much stone lies below
ground as above it.
    On the subsequent weekend, two bags of
concrete were packed in to solidify the foun-
dation stones. One bag and a gallon of preser-
vative helped to form a 90 + pound pack for
60 + year old section member Doug Riggs.
    The logs wne hauled by hand 200 yards
to the building site with the assistance oflog
carriers. The largest logs, weighing close to
1,000 pounds, required ten members to drag
and carry them to the site.
    The logs were laid up in a painstaking pro-
cess which used chain saws to level each suc-
cessive course, and notch the ends. A transit
was instrumental in assuring that all of the
walls were level. The lower courses were so
large (up to 14"), that the biggest spikes
available (12") were ineffective. An auger was
used to drill through these courses, as well

   As indicated on the pictures which accom-
pany this article, the shelter boasts a metal
roof, four windows, bunks for up to 18, a
substantial porch with enclosed storage space
overhead, and a minimum of milled lumber.
   Final work will include the Forest Service
building a permanent outhouse. The RCCC
is continuing to improve the site around the
building. After the logs have dried for a year,
the section plans to chink the spaces between
them. Discussions are underway with the
Forest Service about the possibility of install-
ing a wood stove at some point in the future.
   For the many who have taken part, the
construction has been most rewarding. The
shelter incorporates a level of craftsmanship
seldom seen in trail shelters. Initial register
entries compare the shelter favorably with
any along the Long or Appalachian Trails.
   Acknowledgement should go to the Youth
Conservation Corps for the improvement of
the trail to the building site. The Forest Ser-
vice should be thanked for providing all of
the materials and most of the tools. The
RCCC should take credit for turning out
lumber which would be the envy of many
lumber yards.
   If the involvement of any individuals or
institutions in the construction of the shelter
has been overlooked in this account, this
writer offers his apologies.
