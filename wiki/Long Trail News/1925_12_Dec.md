THE LONG TRAIL NEWS
Published by The Green Mountain Club, Inc.
Brandon, Vermont. December, 1925

The name "Green Mountain Club" has been officially changed to "Long Trail Lodge", it has seemed best to follow suit and adopt a corresponding name for this paper. The reason is the same in both cases: the new name is more distinctive. "Green Mountain" is in common use in Vermont as part of the title of institutions of various kinds.

ANNUAL MEETING.

The annual meeting of the Green Mountain Club will be held on Saturday, January 9, 1926, at the Berwick Hotel, Rutland, Vt., commencing at 11 a.m. All members of the Club, either at large or of sections, are entitled to attend and vote, and it is hoped there will be a large attendance. A Board of Trustees and a clerk will be elected, and other business transacted. Matters of interest will be discussed. It is probable that there will be an illustrated lecture in the evening, and hikes will be arranged Sunday for those who stay over, on snowshoes if the snow is there, and an excursion to the Lodge for those who wish to go there.

The following amendments to the by-laws have been proposed, and will be voted on:

I. That an auditor be added to the list of officers in Sect. I, Art. II.

II. Sect. I, Art. III. To be amended so as to permit the nomination of the annual meeting to be made by members paying a subscription to each member. (This has been the practice of late.)

III. Art. [illegible]. Providing that annual dues of members of sections shall not be less than one dollar.

Special Meeting of the Trustees.

On Saturday, Oct. 24, a special meeting of the trustees was held at the clubhouse. Eleven of the trustees were present, viz., Messrs. Cooper, Congdon, Cleveland, Dean, Marsh, Monroe, Proctor, Ross, Taylor, Voter and White. Also W. M. Fay, Chairman of the house committee. A report of the results of the season at the clubhouse was presented by Mr. Fay, from which it appeared that business during May and June and a part of July was poor, owing to rainy weather, but during the remainder of the season it was very good. The gross receipts were large, but expenses were also, so that only about $400 was saved as profit. The new office room was a great convenience, and the hostess proved a very useful functionary in receiving the guests and attending to their wants. Reports of various committees were heard, and a caretaker for the Lodge was engaged. It was voted to change the name of the clubhouse to the "Long Trail Lodge". There were some suggestions and discussion in regard to changing the methods of patrolling and caring for the trail, with a view to keeping all parts of it at all times in better condition than possible under present methods. All agreed that something of the kind should be done, but the question was raised as to where the money was coming from to meet the increased expense and at this point the debate ended with no definite action taken.

President C. P. Cooper offered his resignation as president to take effect Dec. 1, on account of his removal from the state on that date and it was voted that the vice-president, Mr. Proctor, should appoint a committee to draw up resolutions expressing the regret of the trustees, and their appreciation of the good work which Mr. Cooper has done for the Club during his term of office.

Farewell Dinner to President Cooper.

Charles P. Cooper, of Rutland who
has been president of the Club since
1917, having tendered his resignation
to take effect Dec. 1, by reason of
leaving the state for business reasons,
a farewell dinner was given him and
Mrs. Cooper Nov. 14, at the clubhouse.
All of the trustees were present, with
the exception of Mr. Dockham and Mr.
Tucker, and the wives of several were
there, too. Including guests, twenty-
nine sat down to the excellent and
bountiful dinner which had been pro-
vided. A flashlight photograph of the
party at the long dining table was
taken, and then the party disposed of
the viands with the appetite which the
mountain air always provides. An
orchestra from the Proctor furnished
music. The after dinner speakers whose
names appeared on the menu were
Messrs. Taylor, Monroe, Congdon and
Ross, and in addition to these several
others spoke briefly. All expressed
regret at President Cooper's departure,
and hope that he might sometime
return and all voiced the gratitude and
appreciation of the trustees, the Club
at large, and all who have used the
Long Trail, for Mr. Cooper's long and
unselfish service on the trail, in
preparing the guide books, as executive
and representative of the Club at
meetings and conventions, and in other
ways. Who can most nearly fill Cooper's
place? is the question now uppermost
in the minds of the trustees. Vice-
president Mortimer R. Proctor acted as
toastmaster. Mr. Cooper was presented
with a purse of $210 in gold, $150 of
which, being the amount of the presi-
dent's expense appropriation for the
year was contributed by the Club, $25
by the Burlington section, $25 by the
New York section, $10 by the Worcester
section. Also a gold watch suitably
inscribed, from the trustees, and a
framed picture of a mountain scene,
from the Bennington section. Mr.
Cooper was evidently much affected by
this tribute, and could say but little
beyond expressing his gratitude for the
gifts and the kind words.

Trail News.

The trail committee of the Club
now has charge of all the trail north
of Smugglers' Notch, for 10 miles
south of the Lincoln-Warren Pass
and from Lake Griffith to the Daniel
Camp on the Wardsboro Road.

The Marjorie Hulburd section is
now complete, another camp being
available there, called the Parker
Camp.

The trail committee has made two
trips to the Massachusetts line to
change a portion of our trail to con-
nect with one in that state, and three
trips scouting out a new route to con-
nect our trail at Sherburne Pass with
the Dartmouth trail at Norwich, and
on into the White Mountains. It is
safe to say that early next spring
these two links will be complete, so
that the Appalachian trail over our
trail from the Massachusetts line to
the clubhouse and thence on into New
Hampshire will be ready for use.

The Killington section cleared their
part of the trail and painted blazes
early. A new open camp was built on
the south-east side of Mt. Pico, look-
ing out on Killington, Little Pico and
Ascutney. It has a stove and fire-
place and bunks for twenty. It is con-
structed of peeled balsam logs with a
steel roof, and cost over $450. Several
new walks were made extending out
from the clubhouse, and a new trail
made around the west side of Pico
and one around the east side of the
ridge south of Pico, so that they con-
nect and make, with the Long Trail,
a figure 8. These trails and walks are
being made so as to provide long and
short walks from the clubhouse (now
Long Trail Lodge) leading to some
point of interest or scenic value.

A three mile tramp from the Lodge
brings one to the new steel tower on
the summit of Mt. Pico, a gift from
Mortimer R. Proctor for the use of
the forestry department of the state
and for trampers on the trail as an
outlook. The sightseer stands 35 feet
above the ground, and 20 feet above
the tops of the trees, and the panorama
spread before him is a fine one,
including a long stretch of the Green
Mountains north and south, the
White Mountains and the Adirondacks.

The proctor section, with some assistance from the trail committee, has built new camps at Noyes Pond and at Goshen, the latter a large enclosed steel hut, a gift from ex-Gov. Redfield Proctor, and a new steel roof has been put on the old Goshen camp, the three being together. The Middlebury section have also built a new camp on their part of the trail.

Further work has been done at Jay Peak and three more large road signs have been erected, at Smugglers' Notch, at the Johnson road and at the Lincoln-Warren Pass.

The caretakers at Taft Lodge on Mt. Mansfield built a new trail from the lodge along the side of the mountain to the Cave of the Winds. In the opposite direction the trail has been extended, passing thru a narrow crevice only a few feet wide with towering walls on either hand, 100 feet long, called "Wall street." Farther on it joins the "Garden of Eden" trail, which winds thru beautiful mountain scenery along the east side of the mountain up to the hotel.

The Middlebury section has taken over that part of the Monroe Skyline trail lying in the Battell forest, viz., from a point ten miles south of the Lincoln-Warren pass to Middlebury Gap.

The Du Val trail, so named in honor of Guy Du Val, of Brooklyn, N. Y., its promoter, who will finance it, has been scouted out by him and others who have been over the ground three times, and decided on the best route. Men have been engaged to work on it during the winter, and it is expected will be completed in the spring, ready for use. It will be marked, like the Long Trail, by painted blazes and built overhanging steps and steps will be cut at the wire fence. It will start off Brandon farm, three miles north on the road, follow an abandoned old mill road alongside a dashing brook, an easy, unfrequented wood walk, thence north on the Four Corners Wood road thru the woods, thence north to the top of a wooded hill, it emerges on one of the finest open hills commanding a magnificent view. The view from this point has been named the Couching Lion, a short distance east is the main Green Mountain range. In the far distance to the west appears a long stretch of the Adirondacks, while between the two mountain ranges is visible a rich and varied landscape of woodland, hills, valleys, hamlets and farms. Thence the trail proceeds, crossing several brooks, till it reaches the brook flowing down the long hill from the top of the Brandon-Rochester pass, and follows that stream for the most picturesque part of the trail up the hill till it meets the Long Trail where it crosses the road near the foot of Mt. Horrid. This, we believe, will be the first branch trail connecting a railroad town with the Long Trail, will make a charming walk, and we predict will meet with great favor with trampers who wish to reach the Long Trail on foot, and who dislike dodging automobiles in a hot, dusty highway. This trail will be about six miles in length.

The new trail from Camel's Hump to Bolton, work on which has been going on for two years, was completed this year by Prof. Monroe and New York and Burlington members. It is four and a half miles long, and affords many fine views, which the old trail entirely lacked.

Couching Lion vs. Camel's Hump.

Some of the Vermont papers have discussed recently the relative merits of the above names for one of the prominent mountains on the Long Trail. Some of them, of course, calling it "Crouching Lion." This mountain, formerly known as Camel's Rump, is generally called Camel's Hump, and has been for many years. It presents various appearances, according to the view point of the observer. From some points it looks somewhat like the hump of a camel, from others like a saddle, and from still others like a lion extended at rest. There is a tradition that Champlain, or one of his company, sailing along the lake which bears his name in 1609, applied the name Le Lion Couchant to it, but we believe there is no written or printed evidence of this. There is, however, good authority for this name. Book Notes.

On the Roof of the Rockies, by O. J. R Freeman, Dodd, Mead & Co., $5. An account of an expedition through the Canadian Rockies, abounding with some of the most striking Alpine scenery in the Western Hemisphere. Well illustrated with 63 half-tone reproductions of photographs, and printed in large type.

Summits of the White Mountains. By Walter Collins O'Kane. Houghton Mifflin Co., Boston. $2.50. A silent little handbook for an excursion in the White Mountains, containing maps and illustrations, and containing much useful information. We are delighted to learn that Prof. O'Kane is at work on a similar book on the Green Mountains, to be published during the winter.

An Allegory.

The Waterbury (Vt.) Record, at the conclusion of a long article on the Long Trail, says:
"If the Long Trail were to be metaphorized, let us call it Life. The mountains are obstacles, which once conquered give strength to go on; the valleys are evil temptations which once entered must be left behind and overcome by more climbing. As a man goes over the Long Trail, so he goes through life. He has his ups and downs, his rocky places and his smooth romance trails. If he keeps on, his destination is happiness and strength; if he weakens he must yield to other and remain forever in the 'valley of despair.'"

A good word, but what does it mean?

The Green Mountains.

Written at Waterbury, Vt., by Hannah Gale Luce, in 1857.

Oh, love ye not our mountains,
Our mountains old and grand!
Clad ever in their robes of green—
The pride of our dear land.

The Switzer loves those Alpine peaks,
Where sweep the clouds along—
So worship we our own green hills,
And cherish them in song.

And were I in a foreign land,
Mid classic halls of Rome,
I'd turn from all to fondly gaze
Upon my mountain home.

I'd see among my native hills
The cottage neath the trees—
The tall elms waving gracefully
To music in the breeze.

The bright Winooski flowing near
Through waving meadows green,
The lilacs when the robins sing,
When earliest flowers are seen.

The distant church spire bathed in light,
Like shaft of burnished gold—
The green where roseate children play,
As in the days of old.

Old Mansfield rears his rugged face,
Upturned to meet the sky;
And south, the "Couching Lion" rears
His beetling crags on high.

Full many an ancient legend wild,
I've heard the aged tell,
Of precious ores in caverns hid
And kept by mystic spell.

An Allen's dust reposes now
Nearby the quiet lake.
No more those brave "Green Mountain Boys",
The forest echoes wake.

But treasured be, in every heart,
The love it bears for them—
Each mountain seems their monument—
The winds their requiem.

Yes, dear to us, our mountains green—
The home of virtues rare—
And dear their noble hearted sons,
And daughters good and fair.

Here my freed spirit seeks a home
Above all earthly ills,
Here may my humble grave be found,
Amid our verdant hills!
