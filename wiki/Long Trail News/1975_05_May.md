 CAMEL'S HUMP RANGER PROGRAM
 MAY EXPAND TO MT. MANSFIELD

   Last year over 13,500 people climbed Camel's Hump and over 60,000 people made it to the ridge on Mt. Mansfield by either foot or mechanized means. In the face of such heavy use of what are probably the two most distinctive and fragile peaks in Vermont, a comprehensive protection program is essential if these areas are to be preserved in anything approaching a natural condition.
   Toward this end, the cooperative GMC-State of Vermont Camel's Hump Ranger/Caretaker Program was established last year and functioned smoothly and effectively as reported in the February Long Trail News. In 1975 all indications are that this program will be expanded to include Mt. Mansfield where the existing, effective, state-run ranger program will, as on the Hump,

be contracted directly to the Club in order to save money and improve coordination.
   Besides the main Club and the Department of Forests & Parks, a number of other organizations will be contributing to the program including the Burlington and New York Sections of the GMC, the University of Vermont, and the Mt. Mansfield Co. The Backcountry Research Program of the U.S. Forest Service will also be involved, continuing and expanding its work on the rehabilitation of arctic-alpine tundra and the disposal of human wastes begun on Camel's Hump last year, and beginning a study on trail degradation.
   Tentatively, five ranger positions have been filled (three on Mt. Mansfield, two on the Hump) and eight caretakers plus two alternates have been selected. Together they bring with them 16 "man-years" of Long Trail ranger/caretaker experience, thus virtually assuring the Club and State of a competent performance.
   Operating on the assumption that most damage is done by ignorant rather than malicious hikers, low-key, on-site educational encounters with the hiking public will, as in previous years, provide the foundation of the program. To be sure, many sites require substantial physical rehabilitation work. In order to halt degradation, a reasonable balance of such work and of educational efforts must be made, and ranger-caretaker cooperation with section, state, and Youth Conservation Corps crews is very important.
   The Camel's Hump-Mt. Mansfield Program serves to show that the Green Mountain Club, as the official "guardian" of the Long Trail, is taking this huge responsibility seriously and is willing and anxious to pursue it with the help of all interested organizations.

                                         LARRY VAN METER
                                         Program Director

         TRUSTEES EXPAND SERVICES
         UNDER 1975-76 BUDGET

   The Trustees approved a balanced budget for the 1975-76 fiscal year on March 22. $43,700 total income is predicted for the year beginning May 1 with $35,938 for expenditures and $7,762 earmarked for necessary reserves. The new budget provides for an enlarged Long Trail Patrol and expansion of GMC responsibility to include the Ranger-Caretaker programs on both Camel's Hump and Mt. Mansfield.
   Joseph Sposta, trustee representing the U.S. Forest Service, reported briefly on the developing program for the two Wilderness areas in Vermont.

Permits will be used to obtain usage statistics and these will be distributed without fee including some issued by our Caretakers in the area.
   Harry Peet, Director of Caretakers, reported that 24 invitations had been sent to the most qualified candidates. The Trustees benefitted from discussion of Richard Church's Ad Hoc Committee on Caretaker problems and possible solutions in this growing program. It was concluded that we would not attempt a survey of hikers at this time. John Nuffort announced that Dr. Church would be Chairman of the independent Caretaker Committee and that Terry Gulick would succeed him as Chairman of the Group Services Committee.
   Larry Van Meter will be Director of the Camel's Hump-Mt. Mansfield Ranger program. This will involve close cooperation with the Caretakers who will be directed by Harry Peet from a Ranger post on Mansfield, aided by Trail Supervisors Lee Allen and Preston Bristow, Jr., veterans of two summers at busy Stratton.
   George Pearlstein and the eight-man Long Trail Patrol, aided by Bennington, Brattleboro and Worcester Section and other volunteers, will begin to construct the basic new Long Trail routings in the Stratton and Glastenbury areas this summer and will complete the necessary work the following summer, all under an expanded Cooperative Work Agreement with the Green Mountain National Forest administration.
   Vice Pres. Ralph Bryant reported he is near completion of writing assignments for the forthcoming GMC History. Former Senator George Aiken, who wrote a chapter of the previous GMC history, is expected to prepare an introduction for the new one. Debbie Barlow has been approached to edit the material.
   New sections may be formed at St. Albans and Middlebury. The latter is named the Breadloaf Hiking Club and already boasts 23 members including Elsie Washburn, former GMC Clerk.

1975 GMC INTERSECTIONAL

Here is a rundown of plans for the 1975 Intersectional scheduled for Aug. 24-Sept. 1 and sponsored by the Worcester Section.

Location—Chesterfield Boy Scout Camp of the Great Trail Council just off Rt. 143 between Williamsburg and Chesterfield in west central Massachusetts.

Approaches—If approaching via Interstate 91, leave at Exit 19 for Northampton, pick up Rt. 9 and proceed west to Williamsburg (about seven miles), pick up Rt. 143 west and continue 4.2 miles to the GMC Intersectional sign, and, following the signs, continue to the campground. If approaching from the west, pick up Rt. 9 in Pittsfield and continue east to Williamsburg and proceed as above.

Facilities—Excellent dining and recreation hall; a large field to use your trailers and a parking lot for cars; platforms for tents; running water and toilets in each area plus a waste disposal system; some tables and a shower house; a private pond; all kinds of stores within a half-hour drive.

Bring—Camping gear; a stove, food and utensils; a folding table and anything else you need for your comfort.

Activities—Camping, hiking, swimming, canoeing, and a good nature trail; excellent area for nature photography (and Hans Fritsch has again offered to run a Nature Photography Workshop); compass instruction and, we hope, an "orienteering" course; square dancing; group dinner (bean-hole beans supper by our own Worcester chef); and an auction (bring what you can to auction off). If we get the right response, we will try a pot-luck supper to help keep your cost down. Rainy-day programs for both children and adults. Baby-sitting? Mothers interested in alternating care of children in a pooled-care arrangement and teen-aged members interested in paid baby-sitting should indicate this on their registration blanks. Bob Heck will be in charge of the hiking program and Bob and Ann Humes will be in charge of the canoeing program. Our entertainment committee will be in charge of all special activity programs. What's been listed here is just a start; we are still working to fill out and improve the program.

Restrictions—Other recent Intersectionals have begun on Saturday but this one begins at 12 noon Sunday, Aug. 24. Don't come sooner as this is the earliest we can allow registration and not interfere with the regular closing of the camp for the Scouts' own season. This was the first restriction I had to agree to in hiring this camp. Please leave your dog at home. If you must bring the dog to camp he will have to stay on leash at all times, both day and night. No cars are permitted on any campsite road; cars must stay in the parking lot (carts will be available to help you carry your gear to your campsite). Please do the Intersectional and yourself a favor and obey these restrictions.

Fees—$1.00 per person per night or $7.00 for eight nights; family rate is $3.00 per night or $15.00 for eight nights. Camp headquarters will be plainly marked and will be the Registration and Information Center; Norman and Arlene Raynor will be in charge here. Please make this your first stop as you enter camp.

LESTER M. VIELLE, Chairman

Recognition Corner

CHARLES MONTY

We, the members of the Jay Section, are proud of our first president and what he has accomplished. We would like to share him with you.

Charles Monty started biking in New Hampshire in the early 1960's. He introduced many Boy Scout and high school groups to the delights of hiking and camping. He moved to Newport but kept on with his first love. He would take Boy Scout and high school groups out and would work on the trail and teach them the basics of camping.

Then in the 1970's he wished for a hiking club to care for and maintain this section of the Long Trail. With constant encouragement from Perry Tooker and Shirley Strong, then GMC President, he and a couple of friends finally started to organize many interested people. 1973 saw the first meeting for this section and in 1974 we became officially recognized as the Jay Section.

We feel that if it hadn't been for this enthusiastic man we probably would not have a section up here to care for. He has boundless energy and really cares about what happens to our environment.

All of these are reasons why we were so sorry to see Charles Monty move back to New Hampshire but we know he will never forget us or this section of the Green Mountain Club.

Thank you, Charlie, for laying the groundwork so we have an easier and more enjoyable time up here at the Jay Section. We know we will see you on the Long Trail again.

THE JAY SECTION

The Sections Report: Some Are Up, Some Down

Editor's Note: Sitting in the Editor's Chair for the last few issues, we've experienced a growing realization that, while basically similar, there are significant differences among the GMC's 11 local and regional Sections. With this in mind we invited Section reporters to review activities of the past year and provide an assessment of their present strength. The response is printed below (we didn't hear from Worcester Section and a few of the reports are actually in the more traditional vein). While we understand the Sections are supposed to make such reports at the GMC's annual meeting (but don't always do so!), we don't believe these reports have ever reached the Long Trail News. We hope that providing them here will be of interest to both Section members and Members-at-Large.

BENNINGTON SECTION

The size of the Bennington Section membership is at least holding firm with 58 members in 1974 and 57 members so far in 1975.
   The section is now scheduling two outings a month of all types: short walks, day hikes and work trips. While the outings are well-publicized and open to non-members as well as members, the turn-out generally has been limited to three or four. Exceptions have been when Betsy Paulsen, our new GMC trustee, shows up with a dozen or so high school students.
   A number of major work projects are in the offing for the section. This summer we will help in the major heavy relocation of the Long Trail in the Glastenbury Mt. area by cutting about four miles of new trail from Vermont Rt. 9 to the vicinity of Little Pond. Also planned for 1975 is establishment of a primitive camp site at Hell Hollow Brook. Next year the section plans to construct an open shelter at this same site.

JUANITA BENKENDORFER, Reporter

BURLINGTON SECTION

Special recognition was given to Roy Buchanan at our Annual Meeting in January. Roy, at 93, is still an active member of the GMC. He hikes with the section on an occasional outing but admits that he has "slowed down some."
   Seventy outings were held by the section last year with over 400 persons in attendance. Dot Myer received the Hiker-of-the-Year Award for attending 38 of the outings.
   The Executive Board voted to support the Mt. Mansfield-Camel's Hump Cooperative Program and to make a financial commitment toward its realization. The program was to be discussed with the members in detail at a dinner meeting in early May.
   It was discovered by the Trails and Shelters Committees that work parties offering food at the completion of the day's work were eminently more successful. The attendance at work projects soars when food is offered as a part of the package. Consequently, this season's schedule includes outings such as "Labor Then Savor," "Perspire and Retire," "Shovel Out! Shovel In!," "Workandine," "Fix 'n Feast," and "Busy Beavers Gopher Food." With names like this, who can resist?
   About 25 very sturdy new trail signs have been made by the Trails Committee. The South Burlington High School assisted with this project by permitting the use of their wood-working shop.

ALVERTA PERKINS, Reporter

BRATTLEBORO SECTION

The Brattleboro Section held its annual meeting March 18 with 22 members present. In addition to election of officers (see roster elsewhere) Ted Hazelton furnished an interesting program by showing slides of his End-to-End trip on the Long Trail. He hiked short sections at a time over a four-year period. One time he hit the trail for five days and had four days of only rain and only one beautiful day!

LOUISE H. DUNKLEE, Secretary

CONNECTICUT SECTION

Connecticut Section membership, which has definitely been on the increase the last four or five months, stood at 94 as of March 15 with renewals running about 90% ahead of last year.
   Last year, after sensing the section's activities schedule had bogged down, an activities meeting was held during November with 32 members and guests attending. The end product was a four-month schedule which has sparked renewed interest in the section.
   Since implementing the schedule in January, several hikes have had turn-outs of more than 20 persons. Another schedule now in the making will have more diversification, that is, it will have more activities than hiking alone.
   Two work parties in May, 1974 cleared the 9½ miles of Long Trail for which the section is responsible (Glastenbury Mt. to Arlington-West Wardsboro Road). Signs were put up at each terminus announcing to the hiking world our role in the upkeep of this stretch of trail. The wire bunks in Caughnawaga Shelter were replaced by pine boards which, when installed, increased the sleeping capacity to six. Creosote was applied to the new boards as well as to the table and floor at the Kid Gore Shelter.
   In September, a work party led by Herb Van Winkelen replaced the table in the Kid Gore Shelter, shored up the shelter's foundation, relocated the outhouse, hauled out bags of litter and liberally lathered both shelters (Gore and Caughnawaga) and outhouses with creosote. The wooden stairs at Gore had disappeared; however, large stones were emplaced and now provide improvised stairs to the floor level.
   Another work party in November covered the table at the Gore Shelter with metal to protect it from the "porkies." Some trail clearing was also done and along the way the table at Story Spring Shelter was found to be broken up. Salvageable portions were stored in rear of the shelter with the intent of reconstructing them later. The floor in the shelter had obviously been the site of a fire; a hole was burned through same.
   Interest in the section has also been stirred by its monthly membership publication, Trail Talk, which the undersigned is now editing.
   The section's Executive Committee has met five times in the past six months. Results of the meetings, published in Trail Talk, have generated interest and sparked action.
   The Annual Meeting held March 15 was attended by 53 members and guests. In this writer's opinion it was the best attended and conducted meeting in the past four years. The elections produced a group of section officers having a strong interest in the activities of the section and a desire to surpass the improvements made by their predecessors.
   In summary, all actions of the section are "thumbs up," i.e. everything is positive. And last but not least, three section members joined the End-to-End roster in 1974.

ANTHONY H. SHOOKUS, Reporter

KILLINGTON SECTION

When inquiries were made of various officers of the Killington Section about the changes noted over the years in the section, a rather gloomy picture emerged. Not only is membership falling, but the old "Killington Spirit" is diminishing. A comparison made between the Outing Committee report for 1950 and that of 1974 indicates that the average attendance on hikes has dropped approximately 50%. Also, in spite of a slight increase in membership since the 1960's, as of April 1, 1975, the membership is down 17 persons. It is strongly felt that this reflects the discontent and dissatisfaction of the members at the change in leadership and policies of the Green Mountain Club during the past few years. Two people who have been members since the 1930's have not renewed their membership, and a recent past president will not join again. Several other members have expressed great displeasure.

Two specific examples of GMC policies which have been disturbing are as follows: Firstly, the manner in which the building of the Clarendon Gorge Bridge was taken over by the main club after Dr. Harold Howard had spent so much time and effort on it was most undiplomatic. Secondly, since the Caretaker Program was begun at Pico, and then Cooper, the Killington Section has not received any information or correspondence regarding this program. Undoubtedly, better communication would help the relationship between Sections and the main club.

As far as work parties are concerned, Sherwin Start, Trails Chairman, did a great deal of trail work this past year and had the help of a number of section members. But each year interest has lessened which can be a problem since we have five camps and shelters and over 20 miles of trails to maintain. However, during the summer of 1974 our entire section was checked by various members and pronounced in good shape. The opinion of many hikers was obtained and found favorable as were many notes left in registers. Several called it the best maintained section in the state.

Shelter Chairman Paul Howard reports that major repairs were made to the shelters of our Killington Section in 1974. This work included a new floor at Sunnyside, new platform-type bunks at Governor Clement, replacement of glass in several windows at Pico, and replacement at Clarendon of one side of the "cow catcher" fencing which was apparently missing due to someone's need for firewood!

As with so many organizations, a lot of hard work falls upon a few shoulders. However, Killington has a record to be proud of and it has certainly gone its "second mile" in supporting the work and maintaining the ideals of the Green Mountain Club.

FRANCES H. ROBERTS, Reporter

MONTPELIER SECTION

Our section membership has increased greatly in the past year. In 1973-74 we had 28 adult and nine junior GMC'ers while 1974-75 brought 48 adult and 15 junior members. We've also had a dozen or more inquiries about the GMC from prospective members. We thus hope our ranks will continue to grow this year.

During the past fall and winter we scheduled outings for almost every weekend. Some of the winter trips have been made to the White Mountains by the hearty climbers who prefer rugged winter climbing while others have preferred snowshoeing and ski touring in nearby parks and on the trails which we cut in the Berlin area. Our section seems to have a core group of 6-12 folks who turn out for most of the trips. But we did have 30 or more members participate in some of our family outings and trailwork trips. All day outings plus supper were well attended; food always draws a crowd!

We spent a good day in June working on our section of Long Trail, which is the three miles from Vermont Rt. 108 to Chilcoot Pass plus Elephant Head Trail. Sterling Pond Shelter was in good shape but some minor repairs were needed on the outhouse. Later in the summer we had a day of clearing and painting trail blazes up both sides of Mt. Hunger.

As in the past we schedule other activities in addition to hiking and right now are looking forward to canoeing and biking this spring.

WENDY HAMILTON, Reporter

NEW YORK SECTION

We in the New York section often envy you folks up north with all the beautiful snow you have had this winter. However, we were treated to six or eight inches of snow up in the Palisades Interstate Park. Dave Van Dusen led the only snowshoe hike we have had for several years. Unfortunately only four persons showed up.

Since we are in the so-called metropolitan area our activities may be considerably different from other GMC sections. For example, we have Saturday excursions in place of hikes. These take form in trips to the Statue of Liberty, Empire State Building, World Trade Center and so on. We had a very unusual trip to the Metropolitan Museum of Art where we could view the glamorous costumes designed for famous Hollywood stars such as Greta Garbo, Fred Astaire, Mary Pickford, Marilyn Monroe, and Barbara Streisand. It was very informative and we certainly got our walking in! This activity is especially interesting to the less physically-active members and newcomers to the club.

Jim Periale led an 8-mile trip into Sterling Forest, which is east of Greenwood Lake in New Jersey. Snow made it a bit sloppy but all 19 participants enjoyed the old woods roads and trails. Due to real estate developments we are fast losing many of our old trails; therefore a hike like this one is a treat.

Our Trails and Shelters Chairman, Ira T. Ellis, Jr., provides the following report on the nearly 26 miles of Long Trail for which our section has basic responsibility:

"1974 was the busiest year for our committee since I started as chairman. Eight of us spent a week on the trail in early July. Our biggest project was installing a new floor in Buchanan Lodge. We also did routine trail maintenance on all trails not covered by caretakers.

"Larry Van Meter and his crew of Camel's Hump Rangers did extensive erosion control work on the Callahan Trail. This is the shortest way to the top of Camel's Hump and is used by 50% of the hikers going to the top. The crew also designed and built an outhouse for Gorham Lodge. It is a composting type, necessary because of the shallow soil depth. The New York Section helped finance the Camel's Hump program with a contribution of $500.

STERLING SECTION

After a major revival of the Sterling Section about five years ago, the membership count has tapered off from the 75 or so at that time to 47 last year and 42 so far in 1975. A strong core group of officers has, however, kept the section functioning quite well though the continued slacking off of participation in both pleasure and work outings is cause for concern.

We hold pleasure outings at roughly two-week intervals through the summer and fall plus several snowshoe outings during the winter. All of these are day trips and generally are quite close to the Morrisville area where most of our members live. Because of a lack of interest we have had no overnight trips for at least two years. Our activities have also been limited to hiking only and, unlike many of the other sections, do not include such things as canoeing or bicycling.

As part of the two-week schedule we hold three or four work outings each year and think it fair to report that the 8½ miles of Long Trail plus three shelters which we take care of are generally in very good condition. In doing this, however, credit must be given to the Vermont Forests and Parks Department for its help to us in several important trail and shelter projects.

BOB HAGERMAN, Reporter

ANNUAL MEETING PLANS UPDATE

Here's a review with some additional details of plans for the GMC Annual Meeting as announced by Sally Sairs of Montpelier, chairman of the Annual Meeting Committee. The Montpelier Section is serving as host. Please note the May 10 deadline for making meal reservations with Sally (and we hope this LTN has reached you by that time).

Place—Wesley United Methodist Church on U.S. Rt. 2 in Waterbury village about one-half mile east of Exit 10 off Interstate 89 (you actually exit onto Vermont Rt. 100 and that leads you to Rt. 2). All Saturday events including meals will be held here.

Schedule—Saturday, May 24, beginning at 9 a.m. with registration, coffee (at 20 cents a cup) and doughnuts (15 cents each), and running through the day. The business meeting will begin at 10 a.m., lunch ($2.25) at 12:30 p.m., afternoon session beginning at 2 p.m., dinner ($4.50) at 6 p.m. and evening program at 8 p.m. Featured at the latter will be Steven Young of the Center for Northern Studies in Wolcott who will show some slides and talk about a trip to Alaska. (Trustees will hold a meeting following the afternoon business session.) Sunday will begin with a Connecticut Section breakfast at 8 a.m. at the Little River Campground just west of Waterbury village. The Montpelier Section has hikes scheduled this day for the Stowe Pinnacle and Mt. Hunger in the Worcester Range plus Mt. Mansfield and Sterling Pond. Different or additional hikes can be scheduled if desired. For canoeists there is the Waterbury Reservoir at the Little River Campground.

Reservations—Reservations for the two Saturday meals must reach Sally by May 10; contact her at 22 Vine St., Montpelier, Vt. 05602, Tel. 802-223-2940 (after 6 p.m.).

Accommodations—Unless already filled by this time, both camping and regular lodging are available at Nichols Lodge on Vermont Rt. 100 in Stowe about eight miles north of Waterbury. Contact the lodge direct at Box 1098, Stowe, Vt. 05672, Tel. 802-253-7683. Other lodging at the Holiday Inn in Waterbury just off Interstate 89, Tel. 802-244-7822 (no campsites there!). The Little River Campground operated by the Vermont Forests and Parks Department will be open but unless you have a minimum 6-day reservation, campsites are available on only a first-come-first-served basis.

PROPOSED BY-LAWS CHANGES

The following proposed changes in the GMC by-laws will be acted upon by the membership at the annual meeting May 24. (Important Note: copies of the by-laws updated through last year's annual meeting are available on request from the club's Rutland headquarters):

1. Article 11, Section 5 (previously Section 4) will be changed in the first paragraph to read: "... Members-at-Large, 6 ..."

Reason: This number currently is 5. Under-representation of Members-at-Large is being corrected by adding one new At-Large Trustee each year for three years. If this change is approved, two Member-at-Large Trustees will be elected each year in the future.

2. Article II, Section 5 will be changed by adding the following sentence to the end of the third paragraph: Only Members-at-Large shall vote for Trustees to represent Members-at-Large.

Reason: This change is intended to give Members-at-Large the same voting status in the selection of Trustees that members of the club's Sections have.

PRESIDENT'S MESSAGE

My August, 1973 initial message declared an intent to motivate leaders and emphasize communications. There was indeed need for both. Let us share what has happened and started to happen in a fleeting two years while your President's contribution truly was confined to such motivation and communications:

The following have taken place: Executive Secretary authorized; landowner agreements executed; Clarendon Gorge Bridge built; Camel's Hump ranger team formed; Jay Section established; and Section Relations Committee created.

The following are now in process: Executive Secretary moving to full stature as Operations Coordinator in 1975; two new sections forming in St. Albans and Middlebury; and Camel's Hump program expanded to include Mt. Mansfield. Also an expanded cooperative work agreement is being developed to involve the GMC more deeply in the portion of Long Trail in the Green Mountain National Forest which is controlled by the federal government through the U.S. Forest Service.

Two strong Nominating Committees, appointed six months in advance, have made significant contributions to the future by their patient investigation and selection of nominees. Future administrations should continue the practice of inviting a previous member of this committee to become chairman the following year.

SCOUTS AT SUNNYSIDE: ANOTHER SIDE TO THE STORY

We've had an interesting report from M.H. Martin, Jr., Scoutmaster of Boy Scout Troop 1 in Barrington, R.I. about the backpacking trip which he and his Scouts took on the Long Trail last June. The report includes an unhappy reference to the exchange which some Killington Section members had with this group of Scouts as reported in the November LTN.

More of this "other side of the story" in a moment but to summarize the Scouts' trip first, they began at Mad Tom Notch on Saturday, June 22, and came out on the South Pond Trail from Rolston Rest Shelter on Friday, June 28. It sounds like they had more than their share of rain but there were such bright spots as this one described by Mr. Martin: "On Thursday we left Pico Camp again in the rain but by the time we reached U.S. 4 it had stopped. We camped that night at Rolston Rest. This was our last night on the trail and we sat around swapping stories with other hikers. It was a real enjoyable evening."

Then there was the Sunnyside experience three days earlier which the Killington Section report had described. Of this Mr. Martin writes: "As we approached the camp we were met by a female who immediately gave the impression that she owned the entire place and we were trespassers. Without giving us a chance to speak she began to spout rules. First we were told that groups could not use the shelter, although her group had already taken it over. We all had tents and had no intention of using the shelter. Next we were told that cooking should be done with stoves, but at supper time her group had a big wood fire going. We all had backpacking stoves to do our cooking. As for camping near the stream, only our older scouts did this and they were not that close. I daresay they are more conscious of good camping rules than most adults."

Happily Mr. Martin concludes his letter on the following upbeat note: "The Green Mountain Club certainly deserves a lot of credit for the fine job they do on the Long Trail. We all enjoyed our trip and I think all of our scouts learned something about hiking, the wilderness, and learning to take care of yourself. We hope to be back next June to hike the next fifty miles."
