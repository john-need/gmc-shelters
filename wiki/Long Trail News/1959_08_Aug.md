WORCESTER SECTION REPORT

One thing every Worcester GMCer can be sure of is—schedule an outing, and there's rain. Despite the uncooperative weatherman, we've had a full program.

The highlight of our spring season was the annual sugaring-off party which began with a buffet supper in one of our local churches. Jim Green topped his long career as our sugar master supreme by producing some of the best syrup the seventy-two members and guests ever tasted. Afterward the Shrewsbury Players Club presented a one-act play.

Our planned trip to Stratton was rained out, but another trip to York Harbor, Maine was attended by several hardy members who braved a cold, cloudy weekend.

The club camp on the shores of Cedar Meadow Lake in Leicester has been the setting for our other events—the popular strawberry festival, a camp-out over the Fourth of July weekend, and Wednesday night family picnics and swims during July and August. Worcester Appalachian Club members were our guests at the August 2nd outing.

Under the energetic leadership of Hadar Sternstrom and his committee, the camp waterfront has taken shape. In early spring when the water level

        MEMBERSHIP AND PUBLICITY

     Jack Martin, Membership and Publicity chairman sent the following letter to all End-to-Enders. We think the information contained in the letter might be helpful to all members so we are publishing it here.
   "The Hiking and Camping season is now getting into full summertime swing. We hope that you will be able to get outdoors for some of those woodland activities which you love so well. We are writing you to tell you some of the ways in which the Green Mountain Club can help you in what we hope is your individual campaign to further the aims of hiking and camping.
  "The Club has available the following materials which can be obtained from the main office for a modest cost or for the asking in some cases.
  a) Guide Book of the Long Trail, "A Footpath in the Wilderness." Cost $1.50, just now off the presses!
  b) Short Trips on the Long Trail, a descriptive folder which is available free!
  c) GMC Car License Emblems, a cast aluminum emblem which features the Club name and the Long Trail on a state outline. Cost $1.00.
  d) Green Mountain Club pins, 5/8" emblems. Cost 10c.
  e) Green Mountain Club sleeve emblems crafted in felt. Cost 50c.
  f) Foods and Cooking on the Long Trail. A 1959 edition will be off the presses shortly."

Two special Saturday trips are scheduled in August away from Camp—one with lake swimming and one Conservation trip with fine views of the Hudson River.
   Where is there a better place to spend Labor Day weekend than on the Long Trail? Join this back packing event and get to know more of your Green Mountains.
   September opens up a full round of activities with Saturday and Sunday walks, Canoe trips, Trail clearing, Fall get-together, and our Thendara still beckons. We hope many of you will join us at the Intersectional Thendara Weekend. The weather will be good, swimming perfect, trails varied, and food plentiful.
   In the last issue of the NEWS we told you of a coming Bicycle Trip. It's over! It was very successful. Bikes were rented to GMCers by friends of the trip leader. Rental fee was then donated by the children to the local United Fund campaign—both noble ideas. Sixty miles of scenic Northern Jersey were covered by the hearty cyclers. They are already talking of another trip.

                             A SUGGESTION

   It was just about four years ago that the Burlington Section of the Green Mountain Club came up with an idea which has become popular every year. I refer to the annual week of camping during the last week in August or the week which comes just before Labor Day. Trips have been taken to Maine, the White Mountains, and this year to the Adirondacks.
   Our Intersectional hikes have been popular, but the time for travel has been out of proportion to the time for hiking for many. My suggestion is that we make the Intersectional a week of camping in various parts of New England such as Maine, New Hampshire, and our own Vermont with possibly a trip to the high mountains of New York. Travel would then be in proportion to the hiking. Furthermore, for those who can not come for the week, there would be two weekends at the beginning and end of the camping on which they could join the group.
   I do not think that the Burlington section ever had in mind copying the Appalachian Mountain Club, but actually this suggestion would be in a small way like the "August Camp" which has proved so tremendously popular for this club. Probably we may never want to copy their idea of organized camping with a central dining tent, paid cooks, etc., but there is no telling how this idea will work out if adopted.
   Don Havens brought up this suggestion to a few at the annual meeting and the response was so enthusiastic that I dare to make this suggestion to you now. I hope that it brings as much pleasure to the rest of the GMC as it has brought to us here in Burlington.
                                     GEORGE E. SAUNDERS

THOUGHTS OF A NEWCOMER TO VERMONT

We had been to Vermont before, I as a child, we together on a quick swing through New England. But last year we came to Rutland for our first Annual GMC Meeting. The massed green glory of the Green Mountains came as a complete surprise.

New York is crowded. Newspapers are telling about the rat-infested slums. Articles appear constantly on the "population explosion." In our own suburban neighborhood one elm, maple or oak after another is cut down. Each loss grieves us. We still have green to look at out of our kitchen window, but for how much longer? We get away to the woods every weekend the year round. Only in this way can we stand the city.

But the Long Trail—here is a delightful 250 mile long trail, winding through the woods, along the ridges of the Green Mountains from Massachusetts to Canada! We had never heard of it until several years ago. What a dream of those who planned it! What a dream for us who maintain it.

Last year at Vermont we met the people of the Main Club. How kind they were to us! We lost our hearts to them, and to the Green Mountains for good.

We walked a little of the Long Trail last year. Bernie Miller, John and I, from the Cold River Road to the Rutland-Bellows Falls highway, about three and one half miles. We saw the stunning Clarendon Gorge Bridge high over the Mill River. Could that really have been built by one couple! We saw Clarendon Lodge, set in a grove of evergreens. We visualized the good times there had been there. I had never seen a Long Trail lodge before. What a miracle—a whole chain of them from Massachusetts to Canada.

This year, in perfect weather, John and I spent two days on side trails. The day before the Annual Meeting we drove to a point a little west of Irasville, a town about two-thirds of the way up the State. We parked in a hardwood grove, where great short sections of thick logs lay all about in the shade. We sat on one of these and ate our lunch.

We walked up the Barton Trail for 2.5 miles to Glen Ellen Lodge. This side trail and the Lodge are under the care of our own New York Section. The Lodge is one of six under our care. Later we saw two others of our six. According to our remarkable new Long Trail Guide Book, we ascended about 2000 feet in 2.5 miles. Near the top, after climbing over a tangle of huge blow-downs, we suddenly emerged into a sightly clearing, our first on the trail that day. A clear stream crossed at our feet. Beyond it appeared the Glen Ellen Lodge, facing east. We sat on the rocks sloping down in front of it. All out-of-doors was at our feet for miles and miles. The Lodge looked ingeniously designed, usable, comfortable! What a feat, it seemed to me, to bring all those materials to this point—and to any point along the Long Trail.

The descent seemed easy—and of course quicker. Being five feet one inch tall, and having tried to hike with the tall, long-legged walkers of our own Section, I have always regarded myself as pretty much of a duffer when it came to hiking. I was soothed and flattered to find that our time had equalled exactly the time prescribed in the Guide Book; two and one half hours up, one and one fourth hours down.

The day after the Annual Meeting we drove to Huntington Center, about fifteen miles north of where we had climbed on Friday. Here we saw the Burrows Farm, on a sloping green spot, with the magnificent Camel's Hump Mountain hanging over it. No wonder New Yorkers who know enough retire to Vermont as fast as they can. We walked up the Forest City Trail, climbing 2.2 miles, and finally reached the Long Trail. Montclair Glen Lodge is 200 feet to the right and Camel's Hump 1.9 miles to the left. Here for the first time this year we were thrilled to see the white markers of the Long Trail.

Montclair Glen Lodge is set in the woods in a most delectable spot. There were painted trilliums nodding all about, bright green foliage and fragrant grass. We were again impressed with the ingenuity of the design of the Lodge. We read the log book, and were thrilled to see the signatures of veteran hikers. Their names are already legend to us. We ate our lunch in the cool of the Lodge. We were interested that though there were mosquitoes outside the open door, none came in the Lodge. By what ingenuity of design has Roy Buchanan achieved this?

We climbed up to an overlook, and gazed toward Camel's Hump, one and three-quarters hours away. Some other day. The descent was again easier. Our elapsed times corresponded with the Guide Book.

Bernie and Fergie had started at Burrows Farm and gone over Camel's Hump to Wiley Lodge. We drove around to meet them. We saw the rowboat "R.B. Buchanan" on the Winooski River. The New York Section paid for half of this good ship. Its use by hikers cuts out at least six miles of backtracking. We walked up to the deluxe Wiley Lodge—a short distance. This beautiful endowed Lodge is set high over a rushing brook—idyllic! What a wonderful thing to do with money!

It was one of those rare beautiful days out-of-doors that you hate to see come to an end. I couldn't sleep at once that night, thinking how it would be to be spending the night at Montclair Glen Lodge. I expect I would have been wakeful there too for a while. The inevitable little animals would be poking about, rustling, investigating anything new in their territory.

Some time I hope to sleep in a Long Trail Lodge.

EDNA M. ROHRBAUGH
New York Section
