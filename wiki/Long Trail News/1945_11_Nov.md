The Long Trail News

The November bulletin, "Mt. Mansfield Skiing," of the Mt. Mansfield Ski Club, Stowe, Vermont, was recently received. We quote the following:

MAINTAINANCE UNDERWAY

Both the State Forest Service and the private Stowe agencies have small crews engaged in cutting brush on the Mt. Mansfield trails. Although labor is almost impossible to obtain in Vermont this fall, it is expected that most of the trails can be covered before winter. Extended improvements cannot be started until next year, but some needed widening has been done on the Lord Trail and the Skimeister.

FUTURE TRAIL WORK ASSURED

On Sept. 7, 1945 the Club sponsored a meeting of all the commercial agencies interested in skiing facilities on the eastern side of Mt. Mansfield, for the purpose of initiating the establishment of a cooperative plan for financing trail maintenance and improvements.

According to the plan, the State Forest Service will finance all work in the Mt. Mansfield State Forest, and the private agencies will finance work on private lands. The Trail Administrative Committee chosen by the group for 1945-46 is: Albert W. Gottlieb, Charles D. Lord and Sepp Ruschp.

NEW ROAD TO MANSFIELD

True to its promise, the State Highway Department soon after V-J Day commenced construction of a paved road from Stowe to the parking area at the foot of the Lift. This is one of the Department's first post-war projects. The work includes re-grading, straightening and widening in a few places. Although the paved surface will not be completed until next spring, the road will be in first class condition for this winter's travel.

The editor of "Mt. Mansfield Skiing" is A. W. Coleman, 25 Liberty St., Montpelier, Vt., and, although we haven't asked him, we are sure he would be willing to furnish information about the subject of his bulletin to any interested member of the Green Mountain Club.

THE POET'S CORNER

Frank M. Post, veteran hiker and pipe expert* of the Burlington Section, has sent us a poem. We are sorry we have only room enough to print the first and last two stanzas, but you'll like them.

A Long Trail A'Winding

There's a long, long trail awinding
Into the vale of our dreams,
Where the shadows come a'stealing
'Cross the cool and babbling streams.
And our thoughts all wander thither
When the autumn days are come
And our hearts they yearn unceasing
For the cabin we'll call home.

And our long trail is awinding
To a long envisioned rest,
A friendship with the wilderness,
Of all recreation best.
We can smell the cakes and bacon
And the coffee and the beans,
The spider smokes with sausage
And we eat them in our dreams.

The long, long trail's still winding
As our lives go swiftly on,
All too soon the path descending
Leads toward the setting sun.
Our sojourn in the mountains
All too quick will surely end,
As we pack our kit and duffle
And our homeward way we wend.

*He's a smoker, not a plumber.

WE'LL TRY IT

Ray Helbig, Advertising Manager of the Greenfield Tap and Die Corp., of Greenfield, Mass., and a reader of the LONG TRAIL NEWS, has this suggestion for the editor.

"How would it be to collect the 'logs' which hang in a number of the lodges and shelters along the Trail and publish excerpts from them from time to time?

"One of our favorite indoor sports after arriving in a camp is to read the log and compare it with our own experiences. Our own contributions to the logs have not been very inspiring but we have seen some very interesting literary endeavors in them which certainly should be shared with club members, especially those who are so unfortunately situated as to be unable to 'do the Trail' every year.

"Furthermore, I believe that along in February or March when people are beginning to plan their vacations they would be thrilled to read a few items bringing back memories of the joys of the Trail, and induce them to set aside a few days of their vacation for a hiking trip. If log writers knew that their experiences were likely to be printed in the News they might be inspired to greater literary effort."

This sounds like a good idea, and we hope to try it out in the next issue.

BURLINGTON SECTION NOTES

Eighteen members enjoyed the Labor Day week-end in the Bolton region. Eleven spent two nights as guests of E. S. Bryant at Camp Bryant, a mile below Bolton Lodge, while the others were day visitors. Sunday evening when one member, Jean Magrum, came into camp he was surprised to find a large candlelit cake on the table in honor of his birthday. Prof. R. O. Buchanan conducted a Sunday climb to the top of Camel's Hump via the Monroe Farm and the Wilson and Alpine trails, passing the scene of the fatal airplane wreck of about a year ago. These club-maintained trails were the means of approach for the rescue party at that time.

On September 30 our section conducted its first foray into foreign territory since the war. Eleven members left Burlington that morning, by jeeps (one driven by Cora Hutchins and one by Dick Cowles) for an attack on Boreas Mountain in the Adirondacks. One hardy scout even penetrated the hostile lines as far as Elk Lake, and escaped with half a dozen Kodachrome shots. The day was glorious—clear as crystal—and the view from the tower on Boreas (elevation 3815) is one of the finest in the whole Adirondack region. We retreated without loss across the Champlain Bridge, and watched the sun set across the lake as we drove home at the end of a perfect day.

WORCESTER SECTION TRAILS

On October 1 Louis Bigelow, for years the rambunctious leader of Worcester Section work parties, sent the Director of the Long Trail Patrol, Roy Buchanan, the following report on the season's improvements along that section's portion of the trail.

Prof. Roy Buchanan,
Burlington, Vt.

We managed to get one work party up on the trail in July and with the exception of about two miles between Caughnawaga Shelter and Glastenbury Mt. and a half mile or so near the summit of Stratton Mt. the trail was well cleared and much of the way the blazes were painted. If it had not been for a terrific rain storm the whole trail would have been done. The short sections left were entirely passable and will be all right until spring. The boys from Camp Jerog did a good job on the sections from Black Branch to Caughnawaga Shelter. Both Willis Ross Camp and Webster Camp were in need of considerable repair and I got J. W. Allen, a carpenter from Wardsboro to repair Webster. He put on new roofing where needed, new roof boards on the overhang front, new support posts in front, boards on the bunks, built a new table and cleaned out a fireplace someone had built inside the camp. As you know it was our plan to abandon that camp as soon as we could build at Grout Pond and open the new trail but we must keep Webster up until then. Cost us $40.00 to repair it but it is in good shape again now.

Willis Ross Camp was leaking badly so last week I got a gang together and we lugged in material and put a new roof on, dug out a decayed back sill, putting stones under the sleepers, straightened up and fastened front doors, repaired broken bunk, replaced stones under front which had been taken out and used for fireplace in front of camp and generally policed the place. It is now in good condition for winter. There was evidence that some one occupied the camp as a home last winter.

The two open shelters at Stratton Pond are in good shape. Cost us $35.00 to repair Willis Ross Camp but it was a good investment. A camp with a leaking roof goes down very quickly as you know. I consider our camps and trails in the Worcester Section in a very satisfactory condition. Next year if the strikers permit it, we hope to go ahead with Grout Pond shelter and the relocation of trail through that territory. Hope we can all get going again on a normal basis.

THANK YOU

A short time ago a member named Ben Roberts (sounds like a Welshman) wrote Miss Tye to have his address changed from Massachusetts to Vermont, and closed his letter with these touching words: "And please don't fire the editor of THE LONG TRAIL NEWS. I doubt if cutting his salary or doubling his salary will make him either better or worser. Besides it is very appropriate to have a man named 'Puffer' editor of a mountain hiker's paper." O.K., Ben, we'll carry on.
