MINUTES OF ANNUAL MEETING

The Annual Meeting of the Green Mountain Club, Inc., was held at the Long Trail Lodge, Sherburne, Vermont, May 29, 1965.

Greetings were extended to all present by President Bob Humes. Special thanks were extended to Marion Gorham for her work on the arrangements for the annual luncheon and other meeting details.

It was voted to dispense with the reading of the minutes of the previous meeting.

The President gave his annual report. The Club has had a busy and prosperous year, he said. He told of the new shelters built.

Miss Hinchey had nothing to report as secretary, but asked that the various sections send her their membership lists.

Long Trail News Editor, Peggy Pons, asked for contributions of news and any other suggestions.

Treasurer's Report-Ted Goddard, Treasurer, presented his report in printed form. It was accepted as printed.

SECTION REPORTS-

Sterling-Fred Mould: Trail seems to be in good order.

Burlington-Shirley Strong: Reported that Burlington has had a busy year. Many hikes were held and each well attended. Canoeing has been popular. Trail is in good condition. Shelters are O.K. with Taft Lodge to receive a new stove. Section has formed a conservation committee. She discussed the proposed Scenic Parkway and Burlington's position on the matter.

Montpelier-Membership is on the increase. Trails were reported in good shape with work planned for trailmarking and shelter repairs.

Middlebury-No report.

Killington-George Reynolds: Membership has remained constant. Trails are in good shape and shelters in good repair. New toilets are needed at several shelters.

Brattleboro-Rex Coleman: Trail is all cleared with some blazing done. All set for summer hiking.

Bennington-Marion Hardy: Sucker Pond area has been cleared. Shelters are to be repaired. Glastenbury is to be rebuilt on site of old Glastenbury metal shelter.

Worcester-Fred Eldridge: Trail work is done. Some logging operations are between Stratton and Bourne Ponds. Section is very active.

New York-John Nuffort: Reported 433 members. Section is very active. Announced 50th Anniversary would be in October, 1966. Five charter members still are active. Trails are blazed, the new Wiley Lodge is built and trail is all to be cleared in June.

U.S. Forest Service-No representative.

Proposed Scenic Highway

President Bob Humes presented the prospectus of the new proposed Green Mountain Parkway with a letter from Vermont Highway Commissioner, Russell Holden.

Shirley Strong explained that because of the short time available to present plans to Federal authorities, the old Wilgus Highway proposal was used to make up a new prospectus. She added that Mr. Holden is interested in suggestions from any interested groups.

Discussion then ensued with speakers indicating general opposition to the proposal as it now stands. Questions relating to the proposal were raised and reasons were suggested as to why the idea of a parkway was not a good one. Alternative ideas were presented such as putting proposed funds for the parkway into existing roads such as Route 100.

It was pointed out that the Burlington Section, through a section committee, already has done much in learning about the proposal.

It was moved and passed by the members that the Board of Trustees discuss and form a committee to keep in touch with the Scenic Parkway Proposal and keep the membership informed on steps taken.

Conservation Study

Peggy Pons described that New Jersey has launched a statewide effort in studying conservation problems within that state.

Jan Abbott suggested that the Green Mountain Club discuss having political candidates take a stand on conservation. She suggested discussing the idea at next year's meeting and that the trustees also take up the subject.

LONG TRAIL PATROL

The Patrol started work for the season on June 21. They began work in the N.Y. Section area for that will affect more hikers than working farther north. They have renewed the badly battered floor in Buchanan Lodge and have replaced the broken stove leg there. This must be the fourth time that this floor has been repaired. It is the favorite chopping block there. The stove is used as a landing spot when one swings from the cross timbers.

Then they lengthened the Birch Glen bunks so now it sleeps 12 instead of 4–6. These sleepers can be the same length as Don Wallace for the new bunks are 6 feet 6 inches, so Don overhangs less than a foot. (Standard 12 inch foot)

Next they did minor repairs on old Glen Ellen, put on a new roof and creosoted it all over. This shelter was built in 1919, was abandoned in 1933, is rather tired but still offers some shelter.

This week the Patrol went up north where they are clearing up some lumber slashes on Whiteface and between Barrows Camp and Codding Hollow, better known as "Codd'n Holler."

Probably next after that is a job on Jay Peak where the ski development has taken over a mile or so of trail.

Roy Buchanan

BENNINGTON SECTION

A new Seth Warner open-front shelter is being built on the Long Trail by the combined efforts of the Green Mountain Club and the Man Power Training Act. Elmer Hosley of Williamstown, Massachusetts, is in charge of the crew of builders and the State Green Mountain Club is paying the larger part of the price of lumber and supplies, with the Bennington Section GMC contributing $100.

Seth Warner Shelter is just south of the County Road from Pownal to Stamford and is the first shelter on the Long Trail north of the Massachusetts line.

The old closed building has been abused by local campers (not walkers on the Long Trail) and by porkies until it is in very bad shape and it will be taken down. Mr. Hosley tells us that thirteen boys walking the Trail slept in the new shelter last week Sunday night, some on the scaffolding still up in front. At the time, the building had roof boards, but no roofing or sides.

Sometime during this summer, the State GMC Patrol are planning to put up a new log shelter on Glastenbury to replace the old tin hut. It has been found that open shelters are much less subject to vandalism than the closed buildings and those near highways present a serious problem. In fact a few weeks ago, some vandals managed to tip over Deerview Shelter and the local club members are figuring to get it back on its foundations quite soon.

A week ago the Bennington Club, led by Bob Hagerman, cleared and remarked the trail up the back of Bald Mountain. So the side trail up the White Rocks and down to Woodford is now in good shape.

GREEN MOUNTAIN PARKWAY IN VERMONT?

Our Green Mountains and the Long Trail are being threatened by a Scenic Parkway.

Last fall all the states were requested to submit plans for scenic highways or parkways under President Johnson's National Program of Scenic Roads and Parkways. Whereas, most states presented plans for utilizing existing roads, it was the decision of the Governor and various state agencies that Vermont should submit a proposal for a new scenic parkway along the ridges of the Green Mountains (modifying the route proposed by the National Park Service in the 1930's and defeated by state referendum). So, in January 1965, the Vermont Highway Department submitted to the Bureau of Public Roads in Washington the plans for the Green Mountain Parkway.

It would be a low speed road extending approximately 190 miles along the main chain of the mountains, running near the ridge in some areas and near the summits of such peaks as Bromley, Breadloaf, Mansfield and Jay. It would criss-cross the Long Trail more than a dozen times and run close beside it in places. Several spur roads are planned to such areas as Somerset Reservoir, Branch Pond, Shrewsbury Pond, Griffith Lake, Chittenden Reservoir, Silver Lake and Lake Dunmore, and would penetrate the area near Camel's Hump and go through Nebraska Notch south of Mt. Mansfield. It would be essentially a recreation route or way, opening the mountains up to the motorists.

A committee of the Burlington Section which had sought out the facts on just how this proposed parkway would affect our Long Trail, felt that the members of the GMC should be aware of this proposal and therefore prepared an information sheet including the facts as it knew them at the time. This was presented and discussed at the Annual Meeting on May 29th. The Club went on record as being opposed to the proposed route and requested that the committee continue its fact finding and make recommendations for an alternative route or routes. This is being done.

The committee feels several factors should be carefully considered:

1. At the high elevations indicated, such a Parkway would be extremely difficult and costly to construct and maintain. It could be open safely for only five months a year. It would not offer motorists the opportunity to enjoy the beauties of Vermont year round. Initial cost is estimated at $116,000,000 (part of which must be provided by the state) and maintenance apparently will be the state's responsibility. It would be a luxury road.

2. It is designated as a limited access route, yet it crosses more than 80 roads and almost all of these intersections will be at grade level. In the mountain wooded areas such easy access will certainly help to increase problems of police patrol and fire hazard.

3. Vermont is a small state already covered by a network of roads (13,725 miles of them). There are four main routes going north and south—Routes 5, 7, 100 and scenic Interstate 89. Do we need another? Can we afford another?

4. Precious little remains along the ridges of the Green Mountains that hasn't been taken over by commercial recreation and private ownership. Camel's Hump is the only major peak which has not been developed commercially. A parkway as proposed would mean the loss of more of our mountain wilderness area. It, along with its spur roads, would open up many of our natural areas to the casual tourist for despoilation.

5. We have something unique in our Long Trail from Massachusetts to the Canadian Border. Vermont is the only state which can boast a footpath its entire length. For over 50 years folks have enjoyed the natural beauty of our mountains by hiking its more than 255 miles and have gained from the peace and solitude which it offers. It should be preserved.

6. Our Green Mountains are just not large enough to combine ski areas, wilderness and trails with a scenic recreational parkway.

Unfortunately, the Governor feels strongly that we must make our mountains accessible to the many motorized tourists—that in order to be truly scenic this parkway must be routed along the ridges of the Green Mountains.

The Committee, however, feels just as strongly that Vermont already has many scenic roads and that our mountains are already accessible to the motorists in many places. Not only are there many crossroads through the mountains, but there are roads up several peaks, including Mansfield. It is not opposed to the concept of a scenic, recreational route but rather does suggest that money could be used for existing roads which could serve more effectively and economically as a scenic, recreational route.

AN ALTERNATE PLAN: Instead of the Parkway, it suggests that Route 100 might be designated as a scenic highway, that it be improved and beautified with rerouting as necessary, that rest areas and picnic spots be added at scenic points, that some spur roads leading to scenic lookoffs be included. Such a highway would offer the motorists a wide range of recreational opportunities, with access to many camping and picnic areas, many streams and lakes, hiking trails into the mountains, ski areas, scenic areas such as Hazen's Notch, Smuggler's Notch. Further, some of the east-west routes through the mountains might well be improved and also included.

We are sure that if you have spent any time at all on the Trail you are deeply aware of the feeling one gains from being "away from it all." Actually as times get more hectic and tension-filled, we seek out these places even more for they offer us an opportunity to relax and "recreate" ourselves. For many of us today, organized recreation is not the whole solution.

We hope you will sit down now or soon and write to one or more of the following individuals expressing your concern and suggesting that this alternate plan for a year round scenic highway be developed and submitted in place of the Green Mountain Parkway. The Long Trail News

OUT ON THE TRAIL
or
SHELTER BY THE WAGONLOAD

This all began when Louis J. Stare, Jr., of Bass River, Massachusetts, saw the new USFS-constructed shelters at Little Rock Pond in 1963. Impressed with the excellent design of the shelters, and the beauty of their wilderness settings, Mr. Stare decided that HE would build such a shelter and donate it to the Long Trail.

USFS permission was granted for the erection of his shelter on National Forest lands. In the meantime, the Long Trail relocation over Baker Peak was completed and the need became apparent for a new shelter to serve this new trail. So last October Mr. Stare and the writer visited a suggested site, two miles north of Baker Peak and one and one-half miles south of Big Branch, and the site received Mr. Stare's immediate approval.

During the winter months, Mr. Stare built the shelter at his home on Cape Cod. A USFS blueprint was used for material specifications and dimensions. However, special changes had to be made, for the shelter had to be built so that it could be taken apart, moved in sections to Vermont, and reassembled at the chosen site.

The big day—May 8th! During that morning, on USFS Route 10 above Danby, the following arrived: Mr. Stare and his father; his truck containing assorted shelter hardware and tools; a big truck from Cape Cod piled high with the shelter sections; a huge Timberjack tractor and heavy-duty wagon from Manchester; and about 20 GMC and non-GMC volunteers from four states!

Describing in this article the trials and tribulations of moving a shelter three miles is like describing the beauties of the Grand Canyon to someone who has never been to Arizona. It just ain't possible!

I could tell about fording Big Branch; about the mud; about the rocks; about the shifting load; about the continuous side-hill lean; about the unloading and loading and unloading and loading; about the use of the winch to pull the wagon out of a hole so deep that one volunteer still insists he heard Chinese voices under the wheels.

Our success was due only to the magnificent skill of Lee Hewes, our tractor operator. And when we had to quit short of our goal that Saturday afternoon because of near exhaustion and a dwindling fuel supply, Lee kindly offered to return the next morning for the remainder of the haul to the shelter site.

Although Mr. Stare had envisioned his nice new shelter tumbling down the mountain in splinters, WE REACHED OUR GOAL JUST BEFORE NOON ON SUNDAY! After a quick lunch, all hands went to work. With Mr. Stare's guidance, and due to the precision of his construction, the sections of the shelter just fell together and by suppertime the assembly was complete. Only the shingles, a few pieces of trim, and the area facilities remained for the next work trip.

When Mr. Stare applied the hand-painted sign, "BUILT BY L. J. STARE, JR., 1965," he humbly remarked that he felt guilty applying his name to the shelter after so many volunteers had worked so hard to make the completion of his shelter possible.

The following Saturday another army of 17 volunteers assembled at the shelter. The shingles and remaining trim were put on, and the area facilities were completed—fireplace, trail to brook, loop trail to shelter, and temporary toilet and dump. We were sorry that Mr. Stare was not with us to witness Curt Garfield's ceremonial lighting of the first cooking fire by the use of flint and steel.

The final epic of the shelter program came on May 30th when 31 GMC'ers and friends of the GMC assembled at the shelter for the official dedication. Represented were Vermont, Massachusetts, New York, New Jersey, Quebec, and the Norfolk Naval Base. The writer outlined the story of the shelter and extended greetings from Mr. Stare who was unable to attend. Using a plastic bag of Green Mountain Champagne (pure water from the stream below the shelter), John Rohrbaugh, our new GMC President, performed his first official duty of christening the new LOST POND SHELTER.

Now it is fitting that our sincere thanks be extended to Mr. Stare for his generous gift of this shelter; to Mr. Stare's father who assisted in the project; to Paul S. Newcomb and his USFS staff for permission to construct the shelter in the National Forest; to District Ranger Sidney Davis for his fine cooperation in the site inspection and construction arrangements; to Mr. Veterino of Barnstable, Massachusetts, who transported the shelter sections to Vermont; to Christopher Swezey of Manchester Depot who arranged for the tractor and wagon; to the owners of the tractor and wagon for the use of their equipment; and to Lee Hewes of Manchester Depot, our tractor operator, who gave up his weekend so that the shelter could be moved.

And our thanks to our volunteers who helped with the moving and construction: Past President Bob Humes; Trustee Bob Attenborough; Irving and Eric White; Mauri Winturi; George Pearlstein; two of George's Boy Scouts; Paul Roberts of Auburn, Massachusetts; three of Paul's Explorer Scouts; Curt Garfield of Sudbury, Massachusetts; five members of Curt's Explorer Post; Don MacKellar of Schenectady; seven members of Don's Explorer Post; and "water boy" Dorothy Rolston who filled many canteens with brook water for the thirsty workers.

The work is done. Now we shall reap the benefits. As hikers prepare their meals at the shelter, breathe the pure Vermont air, enjoy the wilderness setting of the shelter, gaze at the stars and the endless depths of the heavens above, watch the dying embers of the evening campfire, and restore their strength with refreshing slumber, we know that Mr. Stare's wishes will have been fulfilled.

BEN ROLSTON
