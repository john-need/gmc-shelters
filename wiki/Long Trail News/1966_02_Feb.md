ANNUAL MEETING—MAY 28, 1966

NOTICE OF PROPOSED AMENDMENT
TO THE BY-LAWS

The president of this Club is usually re-elected once. Thus he serves a total of two years. A two year limit is a custom, not a requirement. It is a wise custom for at least two reasons. For one thing, it allows a president a second year in which to finish things he started in his first year. For another thing, it avoids the possibility of one person being in office too long for the good of the Club.

The president must be a trustee. Each trustee is limited to six consecutive years however. A trustee needs experience before being elected president. Our presidents have usually been elected when in their 4th, 5th, or 6th year as trustee. When elected in his sixth year, a president cannot be re-elected for a second year because then he is not a trustee any more. This happened to us recently in the case of John Vondell. It could happen again—although not to the present incumbent.

So, at the annual meeting next May 28th, the Club will be asked to vote on the addition of a new paragraph to our By-Laws. The proposed wording is:

II. 8. The Board of Trustees may re-elect a President to a second and final year in that office without regard to Section II. 5. if his first year as President coincided with his sixth year as Trustee.

"THANK GOD AND THE TRAIL CREWS
FOR THOSE WHITE SPOTS"

I had an experience on the Long Trail recently which prompts me to write the following tribute to the Trail Crews everywhere:

Three of us planned, the first week in October, to hike the section from Ithiel Falls (Vt. 15) north and hopefully to get through to the Canadian line. Saturday, October 2nd, we went in to Barrows Camp on a beautiful Indian Summer day. Sunday we went from Barrows to Parker, in rain most of the way. However, rain doesn't bother us too much; we've seen plenty of that before on the Trail. Fortunately it let up for a while soon after we got to Parker so we had time to get in a good wood supply. The rain continued in the evening and about 8 o'clock we looked out and there was snow mixed with the rain. Later on, as I lay in the bunk, I would occasionally hear "plop"—"plop" on the roof and sides of the cabin and realized that that must be wet snow falling off the trees—and it was!

Monday morning we were up at daybreak and, on looking outside, found 1 to 2 inches of wet snow on the ground and the trees and the air full of dry snow now, driven by a strong northwest wind. How would the going be as we got up onto Butternut and Bowen Mts.? Should we take the Davis Neighborhood Trail back down into Civilization or should we keep going north on the Long Trail to Ritterbush Camp and Vt. 118 as we had planned? We finally decided to start along north knowing that if we found conditions too bad we could turn back to Parker and still go down the side trail.

That was a rough day. There was a fine, dry snow in the air all day with a 30 to 40 mile wind right out of the north. Along the ridges it was so cold we couldn't stop for more than a couple of minutes without becoming chilled through. Trail lunch of crackers, cheese, raisins and chocolate was eaten as we walked. There were 5 to 6 inches of snow on the level as we got to the higher elevations and this, of course, covered roots, stones, small logs, slippery rocks, ice, muck, etc. and made the going really treacherous. Our greatest concern was that if one of us should break an ankle or leg up there that day what would the outcome be? It didn't seem that a person could survive too long in that cold and wet if he couldn't keep moving. The tree trunks were plastered with the wet snow of the previous night, often obliterating the white spots. The snow was deep enough so that the path itself, usually discernible on bare ground, could not be seen at all in some places.

And now comes my tribute to the Trail Crews: "But The White Spots Were Always There." If they were obliterated by the snow on one side of the tree we could go ahead a ways where we thought the Trail should be, and then look back on the other side of the tree to check the white spot for the opposite direction. With all conditions as they were the one thing we didn't want to do was to lose the Trail or even have to use any extra time trying to follow it. But, due to the conscientious efforts of the Trail Crew, we had no trouble. The white spots were frequent, bright, and what a source of comfort and companionship each one came to be as we moved along through that day. We made it to Ritterbush and then to the highway by late afternoon, tired but without accident, but with our hearts full of gratitude to the boys in the Trail Crews for the excellent work they do in keeping the Trail in shape for the rest of us to follow.

BURLINGTON SECTION

Up The Trail Apiece—
   Since we last trekked along together, we have engaged in eight outings.
   On Oct. 2nd Skipper Guy and Jan Page turned their backs on their sail boat and piloted thirteen hikers up to the scenic Laraway Lookout.
   Oct. 10th found Kenneth Fisher and his group beating their way up the Barton Trail to Glen Ellen Lodge.
   Oct. 16th Connie Hall exhibited Vermont's autumn foliage from the top of Mt. Mansfield.
   Oct. 16-17th Harris Abbott and a group of 46ers candidates engaged in a "sweaty tramp" in the Adirondacks and logged off Dial and Nipple Top.
   Fred and Flossie Eaton again proved that grub gets the crowd out on Oct. 24th. Even though the day was a bit on the unpleasant side thirty-six hungry folks gathered at Mt. Philo for a Weiner Roast.
   Twenty-one followed Charlotte Philbrook in to Ranch Camp for an overnighter up in the Ranch Camp Valley on the weekend of Nov. 6-7.
   During the rest of Nov., when the Vermont woods barely had standing room for all the deer hunters, we lovers of the woods and trails stayed away. Mary Forrester did get a group out during the period to "beat the railroad tracks." The group tramped along the now abandoned Rutland Railroad north from Burlington. It was an interesting and scenic ramble winding along the shores of Lake Champlain and through the woodlands in some places.
   Rod Rice, one of our Section's trustees to the parent body of the GMC, and a group went to Taft Lodge to pack a stove replacement up and then made a day visit to the Mabel Taylor Butler Lodge the next day, weekend of Dec. 4-5. These two lodges, as most of you will know, are on the higher shelves of Mt. Mansfield. Taft Lodge, one of the oldest log cabins built by the Club, is tucked well up under the Chin of Mt. Mansfield on the east side. Butler is perched high up on the approach to the Forehead on the west side. Both have vast expanses of grandeur spread out before them. From Taft are the beautiful rolling Vermont hills out towards the distant higher peaks of the White Mts. . . . Butler offers the hiker the view of the great Champlain Valley and beyond to the Adirondacks' highest peaks.
   Here is where I wave a so-long to you all as I leave the Trail. . . .

LARRY DEAN, Reporter

NEW YORK SECTION

How the New York Section is Run
   Our section is run (as is also the Main Club and I believe the other Sections) by "government of the people, by the people and for the people." Our By-laws were drawn up first by a committee of members and adopted by the membership early in the life of the Section. From time to time these By-laws have been amended, always consistently with the originally avowed objectives of the Club.
   These By-laws consist of 13 small pages containing 16 Articles. They affirm (Article I) our Name, (Article II) our Objectives, (Article III) our Affiliation, (Article IV) who can be a Member, types of Membership, Rights of Members. In Article V is stated what Officers we shall have, their Terms, the Terms of Trustees, procedures of Installation. Article VI defines the Fiscal Year. Article VII sets up our rules for calling and running the Annual Meeting and any Special Meetings. Article VIII prescribes procedures for Election of Officers and in the case of Vacancies. Article IX defines the Duties of Officers and the Board of Directors. Article X defines the procedure with respect to Admittance to Membership. Article XI concerns the procedures and circumstances of Termination of Membership. Article XII is about Initiation Fee and Dues, no Assessments. Article XIII states what Standing Committees there shall be and defines in detail the membership and duties of the Membership Committee and Camp Committee. Article XIV defines the purposes for which the Club Funds shall be used, procedures in this regard, provides for an Auditing Committee, dispersion of assets in case of dissolution, use of Endowment Funds. Article XV describes the function of the Business Meetings of Club, the crucial Section being Section 3, "The final decision in any matter not provided for in the By-laws shall be made by the members at a business meeting." Order of Business, Duties of Chairman, Rules concerning Motions, Function of the Majority are defined in this Article. Article XVI concerns the procedure with respect to Amendments.
   The By-Laws of any Corporation, including this one, have legal standing and any departure from them could be a matter properly brought before the courts.
   These By-laws have worked very well in insuring to us that things go the way the Membership really wants them to.
                         EDNA M. ROHRBAUGH, Reporter

NEW INCENTIVE

   A great deal of interest was recently shown on a scheduled trip along the Appalachian Trail through Harriman Park. Our average attendance for Sunday walks during the past years has been between 11 and 12; however, the average attendance on the A. T. through the Park was 27. The enthusiastic hikers who came out on three successive Sundays to complete the trip received ribbons for their efforts. George and Althea Thornton, our Sunday-walk schedulers, deserve a lot of credit for setting up this series of walks which was so enthusiastically accepted by the Club members.
   Now that we have the ball rolling on these Sunday hikes, the New York Section has emblem sectors, similar to the End-to-End sector, which are available to all those who qualify. There are two separate sectors; one for the trails maintained by the New York Section in New York and New Jersey, and the other for those who have covered the portion of the Long Trail in Vermont that is maintained by the New York Section. The cost of each sector is fifty cents.
   In either case, certification forms which include information on the length and location of the trails maintained by the New York Section, must be signed by those qualifying for the sectors. These forms are available at Camp Thendara or they may also be obtained from the writer by sending a stamped, self-addressed envelope.
   Any Green Mountain Club member may request a certification form and we invite members of all sections as well as members-at-large to join us on our trails so that they, too, may qualify for the sectors.
   Enlarged scale models of the new sectors around the old GMC emblem with the evergreen trees and the new GMC emblem with the Long Trail sign are displayed at Camp Thendara. The sectors look good with either type of emblem and are worth working for.
   Let's go GMC'ers and see who will be the first to wear the full ring . . . N.Y.-N.J. Section, Vt. Section, End-to-End. Then let's see how many will follow.

TRAIL MAINTENANCE
RECOMMENDATIONS

With the trail clearing season soon upon us, Ben Rolston has prepared the following suggestions to assist those who will be responsible for the trail work this spring and as a handy reference for those who are just interested.

THE TRAIL SHOULD BE CLEARED SO THAT: A tall man, with full pack, can walk the trail after a rain without getting his body, his equipment, or his clothing wet.

THE TRAIL SHOULD BE BLAZED AND SIGNED SO THAT: A hiker can see at least one blaze ahead of him (and one blaze behind him) at all times, all abrupt turns are clearly marked, and all intersections are identified.

TOOLS, TRAIL CLEARING: Hand clippers for small growth. 21 inch pruning saw for growth over 1/2 inch diameter and for blowdowns and obstructions up to 5 inches in diameter. 30 inch bow saw for larger blowdowns and obstructions. Axe (single bit) for occasional assistance in removing large obstructions, but not necessary for routine trail work. Swizzle stick (heavy-duty weed cutter) for annual brushing of trail to remove small woody growth and perennial non-woody growth and weeds.

TOOLS, TRAIL BLAZING: White (titanium oxide base) paint for Long Trail; main supply in sealed cans, paint in use being carried in small glass jar for easy handling. Blue paint for side trails. Turpentine or other thinner for occasional thinning of paint and for cleaning brushes. Paint brushes. Scraper for removing rough bark before application of paint. Newspapers for cleaning and wrapping of brushes.

TOOLS, TRAIL SIGNING: Claw hammer for removing old signs and nails and for installation of new signs. Nails. 1/2 x 2 inch lattice wood and "magic marker" brush pen for use in making temporary signs as needed until permanent signs can be made and installed.

BLAZES: Blazes should be 2 x 6 inches in size, with square corners, per Appalachian Trail standard. The AT standard calls for double blazes (one above the other) at abrupt turns, altho arrows may be used to identify such turns.

FENCES: Where gateless fences are crossed, a sturdy ladder should be constructed so that the fence will not be damaged.

WET SPOTS: If drainage cannot be dug, lengths of down logs can be placed railroad-tie fashion in wet spots.

STANDARDIZATION: Should a complete standardization be needed, the three-man Long Trail Patrol system is the most effective method. Write to the GMC office for full details.

WOODS ROADS: Hikers become most easily lost in areas of old woods roads; easy walking causes them to overlook turnoffs from these roads. Therefore, give woods roads occasional "assurance" blazes, and apply more frequent blazes when approaching turnoffs. At turnoffs, use double blazing and/or conspicuous arrows.

A HANDY TIP: If the trail will be retraced to the starting point during a work trip, then clear LOWER obstructions and growth while CLIMBING (as you will be walking in a bent-over position and lower material will be more easily reached); and clear HIGHER obstructions and growth while DESCENDING (as you will be walking erect and the higher material can be more easily reached).

HERBERT WHEATON CONGDON

It is with regret that we report the passing of Herbert Wheaton Congdon in Arlington, Vt., on August 14, 1965. Our older members will recall that Mr. Congdon served the Club for many years as a Trustee and that he also made the first maps of the Long Trail.

Those who have read FOOTPATH IN THE WILDERNESS, published by the Middlebury College Press, Middlebury, Vermont, will recall with pleasure the chapter on "Wild Life" which was written by Mr. Congdon. From this we quote:

"... Praise be, one of the delights of our Trail is the absence of danger from anything but our own carelessness.

"Undoubtedly, the pleasure of a trip on the Long Trail may be increased by leisurely savoring of its many delightful elements.

"Leisureliness, taking time to see and hear thoughtfully and intelligently, studying to be quiet, opens new vistas that lead to serenity. All at once the quiet of wilderness comes alive, the silence is filled with undertones, the sounds of wild things unafraid and undisturbed. There is an exaltation to be felt on rocky peaks with far views of forest-clad hills and peaceful valleys that is emphasized by the busy chickadees hunting bugs on the balsams close by, the fragrance of the hot earth, the sound of a brook far below. Suddenly one feels the importance of the exquisite perfection of a tiny fern, the flash of a tanager or the contemplative assurance of a thrush's song.

"These things give us a sense of permanence, a glimpse into a serene world that has passed through the biting sculpture of Time and has learned to endure hardship, still knowing happiness and being able to transmit it to us."

The evening repast was a super special—a real Vermont Sugar-On-Snow Party. John Nuffort revived bicycling weekends with a tour of the Princeton, N.J., countryside. Mohonk Lake was a delightful place on the Hiker's Holiday weekend. Our annual canoe trips and camping trips are always fun.

Of course, we take for granted our regular weekends at Thendara where many hosts have shared their specialties: Ella Marie Van Dusen's Hawaiian weekend; Peggy Pons' St. Patrick's celebration; John and Clara Roddas' Bird Census weekend and Louise Kaiser's sauerbraten.

It's so nice to be together for the holidays, especially when Edna Rohrbaugh roasts a delicious turkey and has all the Thanksgiving trimmings to go with it. Walter and Grace Atwood with children and grandchildren were among the many who enjoyed it this year. John and Edna also invited Ben and Dot Rolston who showed us the famous 14 lb. end-to-end pack.

To share with others makes life more meaningful. And so it was when Larry Shaeffer invited Westchester Trails Association; Alice Burnett co-hosted the AMC August Camp Reunion; Doris and Ken Digby had the Philadelphia Trail Club up; and Elizabeth Levers and Jack Adelmann co-hosted the Appalachian Mt. Club weekend.

The wide variety of weekly Saturday outings and Sunday hikes we take for granted. But who could forget the fifty-two people in seventeen cars who showed up for Ray Darrenougue's mine-hopping tour. Guest Leader, James M. Ranson, who is currently authoring a book on the subject, led us to six of the old iron mines and furnaces in the area of Bear Mountain Park. That was just the beginning ...

Now we're looking forward to his illustrated lecture at one of our monthly dinner meetings in the City. And so it goes ... just wish there was more time to enjoy more outings.

SPECIAL REQUEST

When the Long Trail was originally laid out, the route passed through privately owned lands as well as the Green Mountain National Forest. In many cases the approach trails also were built on private property. On occasion when property changed hands and new owners were not sympathetic to our goals, the trails had to be relocated.

Bob Attenborough, Chairman of the Publicity and Membership Committee, would like to compile a list of all the current land owners over which our Long Trail and approach trails pass. Therefore will all readers who know of such land owners please send the information to Bob Attenborough, 330 King Street, East Hartford, Conn. Kindly include trail location with name and address of land owner.

OUT ON THE TRAIL
or
THE LONG TRAIL REVISITED

Let's pretend that in the fall of 1955 you walked the entire Long Trail. Then you moved west, or elsewhere, for ten years, and the Long Trail became only a pleasant memory.

But you have just returned to the Vermont area. Fired with enthusiasm, you are planning to revisit the Long Trail and once again walk its entire length, as soon as the snows recede in the spring of 1966.

You vividly remember the 58 shelters along the Trail. But that was 1955. How many of the older shelters have since been replaced? Have shelters been built at new sites?

We are sure that you will be pleased. The following south-to-north listing gives the new shelters you will see, the year of construction, the builders, and the shelters they replace or the new sites chosen:

Seth Warner Shelter, 1965. Carpenter trainees from Manpower Development Training Act, replacement of Seth Warner Camp (1938).

Deer View Shelter, 1958. Bennington Section, replacement of Thendara Shelter (1929).

Glastenbury Mountain Shelter, 1965. Long Trail Patrol, replacement of Glastenbury Mountain Camp (1929).

Story Spring Shelter, 1963. U.S. Forest Service, new site.

Bigelow Shelter, 1961. Worcester Section, replacement of Willis Ross Annex Shelter (1953).

Bourn Pond Shelter, 1957. Putney Summer Work Camp, reconstruction of two old fishing camps, new site.

Bourn Pond Shelter, 1965. U.S. Forest Service, new site.

Swezey Shelter, 1956. Brattleboro Section, new site.

Mad Tom Shelter, 1962. U.S. Forest Service, replacement of Mad Tom Notch Shelter (1934).

Lost Pond Shelter, 1965. Louis Stare of Bass River, Massachusetts, new site.

Big Branch Shelter, 1963. U.S. Forest Service, new site.

Lula Tye Shelter, 1962. U.S. Forest Service, new site.

Little Rock Pond Shelter, 1962. U.S. Forest Service, replacement of Little Rock Pond Shelter (1935).

Greenwall Shelter, 1962. U.S. Forest Service, replacement of Greenwall Shelter (1937).

Buffum Lodge, 1961. Killington Section, replacement of Buffum Shelter (1922).

In ten summers, 27 new shelters have been built—fifteen of these in the past four years. Seventeen of these are replacements, and ten are at new sites. This is progress!

Thus the Green Mountain Club continues to work toward "making the mountains of Vermont play a larger part in the lives of the people"—the aim of its charter members in 1910. And the U.S. Forest Service continues its fine multiple use management of the Green Mountain National Forest.

Have YOU walked on the Long Trail lately? The new shelters await your visit.

BEN ROLSTON

NEW SHELTER AT BOURN POND

Add another name to the lengthening list of Long Trail shelters!

In November the U.S. Forest Service constructed another one of their standard-design frame shelters. This one is located in a grove of spruce trees at the north end of Bourn Pond. From the shelter a path leads down to the pond, where a view of Stratton Mountain is offered. To reach the new shelter, utilize a 325-foot spur trail leading east from the Long Trail 4/10 mile north of the old Bourn Pond Shelter.

To Paul Newcomb, Forest Supervisor, and to Sidney Davis, District Ranger, we again express our thanks for another fine addition to the Long Trail System. Appreciation should also be extended at this time to District Ranger Evans Lutz for the new Cooley Glen Shelter, also constructed in 1965. The USFS score is now TEN new shelters on the Long Trail in the past four years!

1965 can go down in Long Trail history as THE new shelter year, with a total of five being built. In addition to the new Bourn Pond and Cooley Glen Shelters, 1965 saw the construction of Lost Pond Shelter (built by Louis Stare of Cape Cod), Glastenbury Mountain Shelter (built by the Long Trail Patrol), and Seth Warner Shelter (built by carpenter trainees from the Manpower Development Training Act). This, we hope, is successfully keeping pace with the increasing number of hikers using our Long Trail.

BEN ROLSTON
