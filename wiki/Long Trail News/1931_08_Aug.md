Gates Camp—This and the Patch Camp mentioned in the guide book are being abandoned and a new camp is being built at "West Settlement" about two miles south of the St. J. & L. C. R.R. crossing near Johnson. Through the kindness of Mr. Phillip French of Johnson, who owns this property, a beautiful site has been selected near a fine spring with a view of Whiteface to the south and Bowen and Larraway Mts. to the north. Mr. French also generously furnished lumber for this camp and probably by the time this issue is out, it will be completed.

Whiteface Lodge—In good shape.

Sterling Pond Lodge—Roof repaired and wire netting placed around outside as a "porkie" preventive.

Mad Tom Camp, Aeolus View Camp and Swezey Camp were in good repair when inspected by patrol.

The patrol workers have spent some time on the trails in the Sterling Pond region to make hikes from the Smuggler's Notch road more attractive to "short hike" enthusiasts.

As far as has been reported to us, the Sections are carrying on their good work and we believe the entire Trail is now well marked and easily travelled. Any criticisms will be most gratefully received by the Trails Committee.

Note our war on porcupines. We are trying two methods—creosote and wire netting. Has any one a suggestion? Write to Willis Ross at Rutland or Wallace Fay at Proctor.

Governor Wilson On The Long Trail.

Gov. Stanley C. Wilson of Vermont recently broadcast from Boston an address on "Vermont—A Vacation Land", and spoke as follows of the Long Trail:

For the hiker are countless wood and mountain trails, of which the Long Trail is the king of all. The Long Trail is a cleared and well-marked foot path through the wonderful forested and mountainous region that extends north and south from end to end of Vermont. Beginning at the Massachusetts line, it proceeds over mountains and down through valleys the whole length of the main range of the Green Mountains. While the scenery is rugged and beautiful, the Trail may be followed safely by the inexperienced tramper. Guides are not necessary. Many of the summits that the Trail crosses are open rock and present unobstructed panoramas covering hundreds of square miles. From the summits of the highest mountains, notably Mt. Mansfield and Camel's Hump, on a clear day, the view is simply beyond comprehension to a person who has not been initiated. Here one can see the length and breadth of Vermont, a good portion of New York and New Hampshire, and as far as Montreal in Canada. It seems like the top of the world. The valleys that the Trail dips into, with their splendid forests, are equally beautiful.

The Green Mountain Club and generous friends have provided shelters at convenient intervals, with bunks for over-night stay. The Trail crosses numerous highways and reaches several mountain hotels. While the Trail is in the wilds, it is never any great distance from habitations. It is said of the Green Mountain range that for the hiker it is six weeks long and six hours wide. Few have time to make the entire trip any summer, but thousands cover some portion each year.

Notes.

Slides of the Long Trail have been sent as a loan to St. Louis and shown there on several occasions.

Willis M. Ross attended the annual meeting of the New England Trail Conference at Boston in April as a delegate from the Club.

Capt. and Mrs. H. W. Congdon of Arlington, Vt., are absent on an extended motor trip to the western National Parks, going to the Pacific Coast.

An interesting, well illustrated quarterly magazine entitled High Spots is published by the Adirondack Mountain Club. From this we learn that they have 914 members.

At the annual meeting of the Proctor Section on May 27, Capt. Herbert W. Congdon talked to the members on the Long Trail, illustrating his remarks with colored slides.

The Appalachian Trail Conference has published a Trail Manual full of valuable information on the subject of trail making. Any persons locating or constructing trails should provide themselves with this manual.

Some additions and improvements have been made at the Lodge this season. A much needed new sleeping cabin has been built, also a new reservoir for the water system, and the old office has been tastefully converted into a gift shop.

First and second prizes are offered to persons seeing the mountain illumination on the evening of September 12 at the farthest distances east and west. Points of observation should be verified by time and compass reading, and reports sent to the secretary, Miss L. M. Tye, Mead Bldg., Rutland, Vt.

There is existing much interest in the question of the time to elapse between the start of the signal lights at the Lodge and their arrival at the north and south ends of the range, and how that will compare with the time consumed by the Indians in similar signalling. Telegrams from North Adams, Mass., and from some point in Canada on the evening of September 12 will give this information.

A new shelter is in process of construction for the use of hikers on the Long Trail. It is of the log cabin type, and is located between Stratton and Glastenbury mountains. It is being built by a party of boys from Camp Enagerog, Wilmington, Vt. Another party from the same Camp have built bridges over some of the wettest spots on the Trail. These boys are entitled to the thanks of the club for their good work, and we regret that lack of space prevents publishing their names, 19 in all.
