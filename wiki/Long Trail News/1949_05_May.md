MOUNTAIN SONANCE

We recently ran across the following article, under the above title, in "The Herb Grower Magazine," Falls Village, Conn., and we reprint it with their permission. It is by P. Stacey Sorrell, and we hope she will not mind.

"And now through the pantoscopic lenses of my days I view April all in the glory of herself. She moves across the country-earth with quiet, gentle barefeet, leaving behind her own epitaph. Inscribed in the soul of me are the delights she brings and leaves.

"I walk with April up the hills, up the mountains pale-green and clean. We do not hurry, April and I, but walk to the time of the slow, dulcet tone of a cowbell that comes up to us from the vale. We stroll the way of the old rail fence, winding and weaving, so we cannot miss the robin who sits in yonder white service tree, and so we do not fail to know that Hepaticas and Violets look out from the brown-leaf warmth. Our steps carry us on amid great drifts of April sound, we are led to the brook whose banks run full and sloshing with boyish, rollicking fun.

"To have mountains to climb and April by my side is all the wealth one person can hold. I must not lose one daylight minute of April, nor one hour of peepers calling up to me from down where the pussy willows grow and alder catkins bend low their golden locks. I must breathe great draughts of April and store her joys within the collar of my soul.

"But I do not need to panegyrize April: for she is her own eulogy!"

A letter to the club from the headquarters of the Appalachian Trail Conference brings the following information.

We have thought it advisable to write you in connection with the 1947 edition of the Long Trail Guide in order that certain references to The Appalachian Trail Conference may be corrected in any future edition:

Page 52: The address of The Appalachian Trail Conference is now 1916 Sunderland Place, N.W., Washington 6, D.C., not 808 Seventeenth Street, N.W., Washington, D.C.

Presumably you have both books available, since they were sent to the Club on their issue, but The Appalachian Trail data between the White and Green Mountains is contained in the Second Supplement to Guide to The Appalachian Trail in New England (Publication No. 13-A, July, 1942, 125 pp., 3 maps, price $1.00). The earlier Guide to The Appalachian Trail in New England (Second edition; Publication No. 13, 1939, 259 pp.) omits the White-Green Mountain data because the trails were then closed as a result of hurricane damage.

A.T. CONFERENCE REPORT

Dear Mr. Goddard:

I thought you might like to have a report on the A.T. Conference. Since I presume they send you some formal report, I thought I'd send this rather off the record and therefore record impressions rather than to summarize results.

To begin with, thank you for sending me the letter confirming me as a delegate. They never did ask for it, but the preliminary literature suggested that it might be required. Florence Ford appreciated having you include her also, but as she is no longer a member of the club we thought it best to register her as a guest only. Perhaps we were wrong; I noted with some interest that the official delegate of an all-male club was a woman. There were few New Englanders present, and I guess except for one member of the New York Section, G.M.C., who was there representing another club to which she also belongs, I was probably the only Green Mountain Club member there.

I found a good deal of interest in our section of the AT, especially in the prospective care of the section not coinciding with the Long Trail, and several expressed great pleasure that this section is to be put in shape as one of Prof. Buchanan's immediate projects. I found that our club, with its member sections, more nearly resembles a trail conference in its own right than any other club represented except perhaps the AMC. I was especially glad that I had been at our annual meeting so recently, so I could feel somewhat acquainted with club policies.

There was one matter of conference policy which was a matter of some personal concern to me, but I did not raise my voice in protest, especially after it was noted that the Green Mountain Club had gone on record as favoring their avowed policy. However, I wonder if the GMC has really given consideration to this problem as I can't conceive of any group of Yankees, either Vermont or otherwise, going on record as favoring government ownership such as the ATC advocates and stresses. It seems to be their policy to advocate that the government take over the lands through which hiking trails run, to preserve them from development or use by the present private owners. A copy of the Conference News cited the Green Mountain Club as approving the idea in theory, but not desiring it for Vermont. I am inclined to think it would be equally unwise for other parts of the trail. When the owners have been kind enough to cooperate with us and allow us to use their property, it does seem unfair to get the government to take this property from them. It reminded me of the man who, feeling sorry for his camel, allowed it to sleep with its head in his tent, only to find by morning that all of the camel had come in and he himself had been forced out. But perhaps this is something which the directors have considered more in detail, and on which they have further information. It does seem unfortunate, however, that so many of the governing body of the ATC should be from Washington, that hotbed of bureaucracy and government ownership, when such a question as this arises.

One of the nicest features of the trip was the opportunity to talk with members of so many other clubs whose interests are allied to ours. On the long hike were members of the Potomac Apple Club, the Mountain Club of Maryland, the Roanoke M.C., the Smoky Mountains H.C. of Knoxville, the Carolina Club of Ashville, the Philadelphia H.C., the New York Apple Club, Greylock Ski Club, Berkshire Appies, and others. I do not know how many clubs were represented at the Conference itself, but I think probably 30 or more; I hadn't realized there were so many of them. From Knoxville, since I have been home, has come a copy of one of their handbooks, a list of their outings and general information which they put out annually. I planned to send it to you with this letter, but we are finding it of such interest locally that I want to keep it a while longer. You will get it eventually, but not for a while. Jim wants to take it on the work party next week, to show John Vondell and some of the others. I suspect that when you are through with it, it might be of interest to Prof. Puffer for some gleanings for the LONG TRAIL NEWS; some of the descriptions are very amusing, and I believe there are ideas for some of us to use in our own programs. I'm sending her our Guidebook and other information.

I came home with great admiration for some of the clubs and the type of hikes they plan for their members. But I also came home feeling that our own club is one of the best, from the point of view of trails provided, methods of upkeep, and free use of shelters and provision of same at usable distances. Their country down there is more spectacular, I guess, but our own Green Mountains just about meet my specifications for a place to hike. If my feet recover from the Smokies, and I guess they have, I hope to re-do some of our hiking areas that I have especially enjoyed over the years.

Sincerely yours,
FRANCES GREEN

LONG TRAIL A-WINDING
By MARY PERKINS

Mountain climbing is becoming steadily more popular in the Green Mountain State, where "The Long Trail" is a "happy hunting ground" for hikers. Men and women from all parts of the country are enjoying a bird's-eye view of Vermont's numerous scenic charms.

The Long Trail has much to offer outdoors folk in the way of a healthful, happy, and inexpensive vacation. It covers 267 miles of well-blazed trail from the Massachusetts line to the Canadian border. More than fifty shelters have been provided along the way. They are spaced about six miles apart and vary in type from lean-tos to closed lodges of log cabin construction. They have bunks and are free to hikers, except where caretakers are established. There, a small fee is charged for staying overnight.

Trail markings are clear and easy to follow. Trees are blazed and painted white at head level. Approach trails are marked by blue blazes. At places such as highways and junctions, there are large Long Trail signs on posts or trees pointing to various places of interest and giving mileage.

The Long Trail has been so conveniently arranged that a hiker may approach it from many sections of the state and soon find himself in a beautiful wilderness. A guide is not needed, although "The Guide Book" published by the Green Mountain Club (Rutland, Ver-

mont) is helpful. This book gives detailed information on all sections of the trail. It also contains maps showing Long Trail, side trails, main highways, secondary roads, mileage, and altitudes of mountains.
   The vacationist may spend a day, a week, or a month on the mountains and trails cue off from civilization, yet near enough to get back to the highway in a few hours. The Rutland area makes an ideal starting place for your climb. But, if you prefer, you may step right out on a wilderness trail at the top of Sherburne Pass, on U.S. Route 4. Situated there at the very start of your climb is the Long Trail Lodge, where hikers are accommodated.
   Any story about The Long Trail would not be complete without a description of Mt. Killington. At an altitude of 4,241 feet, and being the second highest mountain in the state, Killington might seem to be a hard climb, but in reality it is one of the easiest. Starting at Sherburne Pass, at an altitude of 2,100 feet, with a gradual climb most of the way, the five and one half miles of trail pass through beautiful woods.
   Near the summit is Cooper Lodge. Bunks are available for twelve hikers, though many more have been known to pile in. There is a good view to the west from the windows of the lodge and a superb view from the top of Killington, which may be reached after a five-minute scramble over rocks.
   Rutland may be seen below, and views in every direction take on a certain ethereal beauty, especially when the stars come out and the lights in the valleys are lighted; and at dawn or sunset Killington is magnificent.

MOUNTAIN CLUB'S SUGAR PARTY SUCCESS

   Members of the Green Mountain Club have proclaimed their maple sugar party held last Saturday as one of the most successful events ever held by the Burlington unit.
   A group of 30 people headed by Kenneth Fisher, climbed up Cobble Hill Saturday afternoon, and then returned to Colchester Village shortly after 6 p.m. to join other clubsters and friends for the sugar party held in the Grange Hall. The supper was put on by the young people of the Colchester United Church. Topping the meal off was some delicious maple sugar, on snow which had to be brought from Stowe.
   Green Mountain Club President Harold Collins and his family led the whole group in square dancing during the evening.
   The entertainment and outing committees, headed by Emily and Rod Rice, made the arrangements for this event which had the largest crowd in club history.

WORK PARTY TO THE BLUE TRIANGLE
By Donald Peer

   On Saturday, January 22nd, Harold Valiquette organized a work party of Club members and friends with the aid of Craigue Perkins.
   The long put-off job of moving the Blue Triangle shelter to Spring Lake was the task that brought Ray Catozzi, Tom Pease, Don Peer, Michael Jennings, Joe Valiquette, Tony Fusco, and Bill Allard out into the snow to tear the camp to pieces. Besides the hammer and crowbar boys, were the spoon and coffee pot wielders. These were Vera Perkins and Eleanor Walker.
   Arriving at the camp later than expected, the crew broke the partially dissected shelter up into sections and waited quite a while for the truck to come, to haul the load to Spring Lake.
   Following this a generous supply of hot frankfurters and beans was done away with.

SPRING LAKE WORK PARTY

   On Sunday, Feb. 13, the following ten men put in a big day of hard work at Spring Lake: Harold Valiquette, Fred Field, Ray Catozzi, Craigue Perkins, Lee Wilson, John Clark, Jerry McGloughlin, Joe Valiquette, Michael Jennings, and Bill Allard.
   Six trips were made across the lake with toboggans loaded with sections and loose boards for the new Spring Lake Camp. A small amount of material was moved to the top of the hill where the new camp will be located.
   At 4:30 the men called it a day, built a fire and had a spaghetti supper along with the mince pies Maud Canty so generously contributed.

THE SPRING LAKE PROJECT
By Fred Field

   Ever hear of Shrewsbury Pond? That's Guide Book language. In Killington Section it is fondly known as Spring Lake. A working party (Harold Valiquette, Bob Rogers, Churchill Scott, Craigue Perkins, Gale Spaulding, Dud Bump and Fred Field) slogged its way through rain and mud Sunday, March 27th, up the winding road past Sarka's Ranch to put its back to the task of trucking parts of the shelter up to its new location.
   After a fine dinner of spaghetti, salad, bread and butter, coffee, doughnuts and oranges cooked by our special cooks, Mary and Vera Perkins, we commenced toting each section of the cabin up the hill.
   The method was simple, the execution complicated. Three poles were put under a section so six men could carry it—three to a side. Even though a path had been cut some of us had to climb over trees, bushes, rocks and down into holes. This way five sections were put

Some months ago we received the following from the correspondent of the New York Section:

Dear Professor Puffer—I am among the most backsliding of your correspondents, I fear—and what are the chances of getting material in to you in time to be of any use, if I wait until after our annual meeting on the 25th? I guess that's really a foolish question, since I haven't sent you anything all winter—what I really mean is, is it too late already to get anything into whatever the next issue is? CONSTANCE M. HALLOCK

Answer—You are not half so backsliding as the shiftless Editor—material is always in time to be of use—no questions are foolish—items are never too late for the next issue, whatever it is—and it's no use to get tough with correspondents when no one, including the editor, knows when the next NEWS is likely to appear.

FRED MOULD, HIS LETTER

Williamstown, Vt.                    April 21st, 1949

Prof. L. B. Puffer, Acting Editor of the G.M.C. L.T. NEWS, 176 Loomis St., Burlington, Vt.

Friend Professor:

Reading not long ago, in the August, 1948 issue of the LONG TRAIL NEWS, page 3, the reprint of a news item in the Burlington Free Press of June 3, 1948 impels me to write you something further about the circumstances which led to the excursion on the possibly lost Elephant's Head Trail over the 3-day week-end of May 29, 30 and 31, 1948.

In the first place, it was not I who feared that the E. H. Trail could not be followed but, when that fear was expressed to me, I did not promise to clear this trail single-handed—not at all! What I did say was that, if the effort to follow and clear the said trail could be made before the leaves should start to grow on the hard woods, I would volunteer to act as guide only to a party of workers who were willing to perform the hard labor involved and would permit me to take my time and rest when and as long as it might be necessary.

When the starting time arrived and I found that the "working gang" included sons Charlie Mould and Henry Mould and grandson Fred Hastings Mould (His name should not be omitted as he certainly was there filled with energy out of proportion to his size), I then knew that something was going to be accomplished and harmony would prevail. I should have stated earlier that, had I not been in the party at all, the boys would easily have followed the trail in spite of unavoidable neglect for several long years. In fact, they could find the old blue blazes in many cases where I could not see them.

It is to me a matter of regret that, when on May 31st, 1948 we worked from the notch road near Barnes Camp up the old wood-road which carries the trail 1.72 miles to the point where the hiker gets the glorious view over the notch to Mt. Mansfield, then turns north 2.74 miles to the east end of Sterling Pond; we did not have time to complete the work of cleaning out the numerous but light "blow-downs." All old and more recent blazes on this 3 and 3/4 mile loop of the E.H.T. should be painted blue this season. All the blazes on the main line between Sterling Pond Lodge and the Caves in Smugglers' Notch should be painted white this season. A fair sized "gang" could accomplish all this in two days.

The Elephant's Head Trail was first undertaken many years ago and, because of the distance between Morrisville and Smugglers' Notch, only an occasional day could be given to the work during a period of, perhaps, five years making progress rather slow. I want to give belated credit to Paul Cornell of Morrisville who, on three or more occasions, transported me in his faithful Buick to Smugglers' Notch and the scene of our labors; and for the very efficient personal work which he contributed to the building of the trail.

Charles E. Mould also gave time and effort to the cause as the opportunity was offered. If there were others who helped, I do not now remember; except that, when I offered the trail to Prof. R. O. Buchanan for the G.M.C. and we together went over the trail so he could judge whether or not it was worth while; and it was accepted as a link in a round trip loop between Barnes Camp and Sterling Pond: Following this decision, Professor Buchanan and his "gang" went over the route and added the finishing touches and some improvements.

If you are sufficiently hard up for copy for the next issue of the LONG TRAIL NEWS to want anything that is written on page (1), you have my permission to use it, any, all or nothing at all.

I hope no hiker will ever attempt to travel the E. H. Trail at night.

F. W. MOULD
