PRESIDENT'S MESSAGE
   A previous message mentioned an objective of encouragement of strong leadership for the future. A conscientious and knowledgable Nominations Committee, consisting of Chairman Dick Metcalf, Pioneer, Betty Weeks, Killington and Jack Harrington, Burlington has been considering the key nominations to be made at the May 25 Annual Meeting in North Bennington. They will welcome your suggestions and observations.
   We welcome the new Green Mountain Club, Inc. Jay Section at Newport, Vermont. 81 Charter members strong, this hustling bunch of trail enthusiasts will take over the northernmost segment of the Long Trail and shelters. AMC ANNUAL RANGE HIKE

The Annual Range Hike will provide an opportunity to hike approximately 50 miles of scenic trails between Franconia Notch and Pinkham Notch in the White Mountains of New Hampshire, including 35 miles of the Appalachian Trail, and a chance to reach the summit of a dozen 4000-footers. The AMC Hut System will be used for bed and board, thus pack weight can be kept to a minimum. The prospectus will include the schedule of the hike and a list of necessary equipment.

Two sections are planned for 1974, and each section will accommodate 36 persons (18 male and 18 female).

Section ABLE led by Anthony Francis and Cecil Jones, will traverse the Presidential Range—a hike over rough and often steep mountain trails which should be attempted only by those in good physical condition who are accustomed to strenuous exercise.

Section Charlie, with leaders Elaine Jones and GMC'ers Dick & Betty Church, will be based at Pinkham Notch Camp with the exception of three optional nights in easily-to-reach AMC Huts. This group will take short day trips arranged to be less demanding from a physical standpoint but giving all introduction to the White Mountain Trail including opportunities to view the area from above tree line.

The cost will be the same for each Section—$140 for members of AMC ADK, ATC, GMC, Bruce Trail Club and Sierra Club; and $146 for others. A registration fee of $25, deductible but not refundable, with a resume of applicant's hiking experience should be sent to the Coordinator by July 1, 1974.

For further information (a prospectus will be sent on request) call or write to Range Hike Coordinator: Cecil M. Jones, 72 Chester Street, Worcester, Mass. 01605.

PORCUPINES

My father-in-law, age 87 and a Vermont farmer all his
life, looked somewhat askance at me when I suggested
that porcupines did quite a lot of damage in the woods.
He promptly told me that the amount of damage they did
didn't amount to a row of fiddlesticks. On my property
here in Mendon there are three porcupine dens which I
have observed closely for over eleven years. This, to-
gether with my 65 years, much of which has been spent
in the woods of our beautiful state, brings me to the
same conclusion-the damage they do is negligible. One
observation I have made is that they gnaw mostly bent,
short, scrubby trees that aren't going to make it anyhow.
Rarely have I seen where they chewed on a good, tall,
healthy tree.

You will hear farmers say, "I wish they'd kill off every
last deer." They nibble on the crops. Bears kill a sheep
or a calf once in a while, hawks kill an occasional chicken.
Owls, bobcats, wolves, and foxes kill our small game. Of
course, the law says we can hoot raccoons, rabbit,
partridge, skunk, squirrels and other game so we do it-
do not the powers that be sanction it? Sure we're holier than
thou-we're within the law! Try to think of a species of
animal that somebody doesn't want to eradicate.

Now Mr. G.M.C.'er, don't you think it's about time
we changed our tune regarding "Porkie?" Sure, he's a
prickly old thing and he chews on our shelters but which
is more important a fellow mammal or our shelters?
Often he is the only wild creature of any size that we see
while hiking. So let's find some other way to protect our
shelters, and leave our spiked friend alone.

HAROLD HOWARD
Killington Section, G.M.C.

YOUNG END-TO-ENDERS

SNACK AT SUCKER POND

Two of the latest End-to-Enders are Alan and Barbara
Briggs, children of Newell and Ruth Briggs of the Bur-
lington Section. They completed the Long Trail at the
Massachusetts line on August 26, 1973, about a month
before Alan's 6th birthday. Barbara was eight.

They hiked the trail consecutively from north to south
over a three year period mostly on weekends, with their
mother and father. They learned of the joy of making
acquaintances with other hikers, of watching and feeding
scampering chipmunks and seeing the early morning sun
sift through the branches.

Barbara and Alan thoroughly enjoyed time spent at
shelters their favorite being Tamarack. They especially
enjoyed the companionship of a small, well organized
group of Boy Scouts from Rhode Island under the ex-
cellent leadership of two AMC members.

There were occasional rains but mostly fair weather
prevailed. Alan and Barbara learned the names of some
plants and trees and to steer clear of nettles. They became
very adept at crossing wet places and navigating rough
terrain, often surprising their parents.

GUIDE BOOK CHANGES

Per the notice on Page 142 of the 1971 Edition of the Guide Book, the following listing of changes involving trails and shelters in the Long Trail system is provided. This summary also includes many of the changes given in the February 1973 issue of the Long Trail News, where still applicable. A new printing of the 1971 Edition of the Guide Book, to be available soon, includes these and other notes of interest to the hiker.

Page 29: A primitive camping area has been established near Seth Warner Shelter, with tenting spaces, fireplaces, and toilet facilities available for overflow camping use.

Page 30: A primitive camping area, similar to that described for Seth Warner Shelter, has been established near Congdon Camp for overflow camping use. Another similar site is planned for a location north of the Dunville Hollow Trail crossing.

Pages 34-37: The planned relocation between Vermont 9 and Glastenbury Mt. Shelter, described in the first and second printings as the Alternate Route, has been officially abandoned. No effort should be made to follow the abandoned routing. Until a new routing, now in process, is completed, hikers should continue to use the present Main Route via Bolles Brook. Hikers may encounter temporary flagging for the new route north of Harmon Hill and near Glastenbury Mt. Shelter. To avoid great inconvenience and possible difficulties, this temporary flagging should not be followed.

Pages 34-36: Fay Fuller Camp has been removed, and its premises are closed to all public use, including overnight camping. No camping of any kind is permitted upstream along Bolles Brook. Parking along the gravel road between Vermont 9 and the chain at the end of the public road, north of the Fay Fuller site is prohibited. Violators may find their cars towed away. The Town of Woodford has provided an official parking area, located just north of the bridge and waterworks, on the east side of the road, one mile north of Vermont 9. Additional parking is available in the large field on the south side of Vermont 9, opposite the Woodford Hollow church.

Pages 42-43: The Long Trail routing to Stratton Pond via the Arlington-West Wardsboro Road and the East Branch has been abandoned, and its use should not be attempted. The new route begins just east of the bridge at Black Brook and follows a generally northerly direction on high ground 3.9 miles to the Pond. The Alternate Route via Stratton Mt. remains in use, but has been reblazed with blue paint and redesignated as the Stratton Mountain Trail. Due to the Long Trail relocation, the distance to the Pond via the Stratton Mountain Trail has increased by 1.2 miles. Also, for the same reason, 0.7 mile should be DEDUCTED from mileages given in the Guide Book for all points north of the Arlington-West Wardsboro Road.

Page 44: Willis Ross Camp was destroyed by fire in 1972, and plans for its replacement are indefinite. Primitive camping areas are located at two points reached via the North Shore Trail. It is requested that tent campers remain well away from shore areas of the Pond.

Pages 44-46: A trail relocation is under consideration between Stratton Pond and Swezey Camp. Hikers should use the present routing until further notice.

Pages 55-56: The two shelters at Little Rock Pond were moved in 1972 to new sites located short distances north and south of the Pond. The trail around the west side of the Pond has been closed, and the trail to Green Mountain now begins at the north end of the Pond. Relocation of the Long Trail further away from the Pond is planned for 1974.

Pages 60-61: The Clarendon Gorge suspension bridge was destroyed in the 1973 summer flood. Until a new bridge is completed at the same site later in the spring, hikers are urged to avoid the inconveniences and perils of fording the river by using the temporary alternate routing between Sunnyside Camp and Vermont 103.

Page 64: The Long Trail was relocated in 1973 for about 0.6 mile to avoid ski trails and lifts. The Trail now passes to the west of the Snowdon area.

Page 70: The Long Trail Lodge no longer accepts and holds mail or packages for hikers.

Page 116: Sterling Pond Lodge, destroyed by fire in 1972, has been replaced on the same site by an open-front shelter.

Page 133: The Trail between Ritterbush Camp and Ritterbush Lookout has been relocated to the west, away from the Pond. Camping near the Pond is not permitted.

Other Changes: Several very minor changes have been omitted from this list. In addition, other changes in joint Appalachian Trail-Long Trail routing may occur as a result of cooperative GMC-USFS efforts to obtain permanent trail rights of way under provisions of the National Trails System Act. Other unanticipated changes may occur at a time for reasons beyond the control of the Club. All hikers, especially those with limited experience, are reminded that the Guide Book does not attempt to provide detailed step by step descriptions of trail routing. This is the function of the blazing and other official trail markings. Despite unexpected changes, alertness on the trail will avoid confusion about proper routing.

Caretakers: Many heavily used sites, plus certain upper lift station facilities provided by cooperating ski areas, will be manned by GMC caretakers during the 1974 season. Nominal camping fees and/or voluntary contributions for overnight use will be in effect at these sites. Information about caretaker locations, fees, policy, and aspects of the Caretaker Program may be obtained from the GMC office.

Hiker Cooperation: Hikers are urged to comply with the "Carry In-Carry Out" policy of litter disposal, to carry shelter materials to alleviate crowded shelter conditions, and to carry pack stoves to conserve firewood at popular sites. Note, however, that state laws and U.S. Forest Service regulations, for all practical purposes, restrict fires and overnight camping to Long Trail shelter premises and officially designated primitive sites. Carrying of tents and stoves is not a license to camp wherever one pleases.

GEORGE F. PEARLSTEIN, Chairman
Trails and Shelters Committee

NEW YORK SECTION

Oct. 20 a small group cleaned up our woods south and west of the swimming area. They collected a park truck full to overflowing of city visitors' trash. If it had not been for the cooperation of Dan Rile and 9 of his Boy Scouts (who dug under the wet bushes like ferrets and worked like mad) Betty Silver, Bea VanOrden, Mildred Lubke, and Dorothy Dunkle (who came all the way from Staten Isl. to help) the area never could have been cleared in one day.

Also up on the hill the men put in a long day. With a power saw they cut up and got in a fantastic amount of wood from the logs cut by the Electric Co. for their line slash. They were Charlie Russell and son Peter, Sherman Atwood two new guest card holders, Neal Kirby and his son.

Marie Petrullo reports: On Nov. 4th, I joined a group of 9 hikers under the direction of Jack Coriell. We worked through some of the trails east of Arden and had a most successful day painting markers, snipping brush and overhead branches and sawing blowdowns to clear the trail. During the day about 12 various sized groups of hikers met us. We were amazed at their questions: What were we doing? Were we paid for clearing the trail? How did we get started doing this? Of course we had all the answers ready. What pleased us most was the fact that they were appreciative of the work of the trail-clearers. They actually thanked us for clearing the trails. We realized then that many hikers do not belong to organized groups. Since they were hiking trails in the Harriman State Park, they assumed it was the responsibility of Park employees to mark and clear the trails.

EDNA M. ROHRBAUGH, Reporter

PRESIDENT POINTS WITH PRIDE TO MAURI WINTTURI

(The GMCer Who Is Everywhere)

I first became acquainted with Mauri through a post card inquiry received shortly after I built the Baker Peak Trail in 1961. He heard about the new trail and wanted to hike it. This is half of the profile of Mauri Wintturi—if there is a trail, he wants to hike it.

Mauri hikes all over, from the Adirondacks to the White Mountains, from the Finger Lakes Trail to the Appalachian Trail in Georgia. But always, after wandering far afield, he's glad to get back to "God's Country," as he always calls his beloved Vermont.

Mauri is, of course, an End-to-Ender (No. 237 in 1966). He established a unique record in doing the Long Trail—by hiking mostly alone, and by backtracking and using side trail approaches, he did 420 miles of hiking just to cover the 269 miles of the Long Trail.

He is also a worker—and that's the other half of the profile of Mauri Wintturi. If someone needs help on the trail, he's ready and willing. Whether it is helping Worcester on the annual Stratton Pond work party, or helping Pioneer Valley in the Congdon area, or "adopting" a piece of the Appalachian Trail in the Chateaugay area, or helping to build or repair a shelter, old faithful Mauri is right there, ready to help. Few members have logged so many hours of trail/shelter work in so many places as Mauri.

And most everybody knows him too, because he never misses an Annual Meeting, an Intersectional, the annual meeting of the New England Trail Conference, or the triennial meeting of the Appalachian Trail Conference. And he participates too. He may sometimes be a controversial figure at a meeting, but I admire his courage in getting up and speaking his piece on what he honestly believes to be in the best interest of the Club.

So, dear reader, if you haven't yet met Mauri Wintturi, just get out on the Trail, or get to a meeting, and you'll find him. He'll be there, as one of our most dedicated members. He is an individualist, he is different, but he is THERE when needed.

BEN ROLSTON

END TO END ACTIVITIES 1973

In 1973, 86 people completed their End to End walks on the Long Trail. This was second only to last year, when 105 awards were made.
   This year, Massachusetts had more End to Enders than Vermont, 25 to 22. Eleven states and Canada were represented. There were 51 adult and 35 juniors, more juniors this year than last, but far fewer adults. This was surprising, since the weather was much better for hiking this year than last. Maybe the flood early in July kept people away.
   Even though we had fewer completions this year than last, there was a new record set, at least as far as I know. For the boys, Alan Briggs, who started hiking at age three, and finished this year at five, is the youngest End to End hiker that I know of. For the girls, Barbara Briggs and Susan Lanius share the honors at age eight. Susan started hiking seriously at six although she has been camping overnight for at least seven years. (Her brother Brian, also an End to Ender this year was camping at six weeks.) Susan was so determined to get her award this year that she hiked one fifty mile stretch with her father and a group of Boy Scouts.
   We also had two pioneer awards this year. These awards are made to those people who completed the trail before the End to End program was officially started in 1943. James G. Hindes of Swanton, Vermont, and John P. Eames, of Framingham, Mass. completed the trail in 1931, and may have been the first to do so, since the final section of the trail, from Jay Peak to the Canadian line, was not complete until earlier this year.
   Again I wish to thank all those who took the time to include comments on trails, shelters, caretakers, etc. in their reports. The information is appreciated particularly if it is current, since it helps the people in charge of these programs to plan their work. I would also appreciate it if all those sending me reports would include their membership status, adult or junior, and section affiliation if any. If someone requests an End to End patch, I assume he is a member, since that is a prerequisite. If he does not, I can not tell whether he is a member or not unless he tells me.

IRA T. ELLIS, JR., Chairman

ABOUT CAMPING AND FIRE BUILDING
ON THE TRAIL

With so much emphasis on tent camping, bivouacs, and
other forms of primitive overnight camping on the trail,
a review of the laws and regulations concerning over-
night camping and fire building is in order.

Private Lands: Most of the Long Trail is located on
private property. While the owners have generously per-
mitted the trail to cross their lands, this permission does
not include—and often specifically prohibits—overnight
camping and fire building. Failure to respect the rights
and concerns of private landowners could possibly result
in loss or impairment of our precious privilege of using
their properties for Long Trail purposes. The private
landowner also is protected by Vermont law, which pro-
hibits overnight camping or fire building on private prop-
erty without permission from the owner.

State Lands: Under Vermont law, fire building is pro-
hibited on State lands where officially designated fire-
places or charcoal grilles are provided. The law also pro-
hibits overnight camping on State lands except at sites
specifically designated or approved by the appropriate
state agency.

National Forest: A valid camping/campfire permit is
required before camping overnight or building fires at
other than designated sites. Permits are issued without
charge by the District Ranger in charge of the area in
which the requested permit is to be used. District Ranger
offices are located in Manchester Center, Rochester, and
Middlebury. The Supervisor's office in Rutland does not
issue permits.

The existence of a shelter or an officially maintained
primitive camping area means that the required permission
has been obtained for overnight camping and for building
fires in the official stoves or fireplaces provided on the
premises. Additional fireplaces, however, should not be
built.

In view of the increasing popularity of trail stoves, two
points should be kept in mind. First, both state and fed-
eral regulations technically classify trail stoves as open
fires. Thus their use on the trail is subject to the same
laws and regulations concerning conventional fires. Second,
and far more important, unless trail stoves and fueled
lanterns are properly used, they are potentially hazardous
to life and property. The destruction of Sterling Pond
Lodge and the recently reported tent fire near Montclair
Glen Lodge are but two examples of how carelessly
handled trail stoves and lanterns can cause possible ac-
cident or injury, great inconvenience to other hikers, and
major expense to the Club and its Sections.

Except for rare valid emergency situations, there is no
justification for building fires or camping on the trail ex-
cept at the official shelters and sites provided. With a
little foresight, the average hiker can easily plan his daily
itinerary to use only Long Trail shelters and designated
primitive sites. Those so infected by wilderness fever that
they consider solitude essential face a slightly more difficult
challenge. However, by avoiding weekends and holidays
in favor of "off peak" periods, by hiking less heavily
travelled sections of the trail, and by planning their sched-
ules to use the less popular shelters, they can satisfy most
of their desires within the framework of established camp-
ing policy.

Until now overnight camping policy on the Long Trail
has been governed largely by basic common sense and
courtesy, respect and concern for the rights and feelings
of public and private landowners, and observance of a
very few basic legal restrictions. If we all cooperate,
there is no reason why there should be any major changes
in this relatively unrestrained policy.

GEORGE F. PEARLSTEIN, Chairman
Trails and Shelters Committee
