MRS. FRANK BEANE
HANKSVILLE, VERMONT

The newer members of our Club are acquainted only with the name of Mrs. Frank Beane who, as our Guide Book tells us, lived in Hanksville and was known to Long Trail hikers for many years.

But Mrs. Beane had a great part in the early history of our Club and the Long Trail. Our first Guide Book, published in 1917 told hikers that from Birch Glen a lumber road would take them to the farm house of Frank Beane, where bread and simple supplies including maple sugar could be obtained, also lodging for two or three persons at reasonable rates.

Mrs. Beane later left the farm and operated the Hanksville store, selling food supplies to hikers, providing mail service, and offering meals and lodging. In later years, after the passing of Mr. Beane, Mrs. Beane had to give up the store, but continued to live in the adjacent house.

On one of our visits to Mrs. Beane, she told us that she often regretted not having kept a log book of the names of hundreds of hikers whom she had entertained over more than four decades. Such a book, she noted, would have made a valuable contribution to the recorded history of the Club.

An ailing foot that would not heal regretfully brought Mrs. Beane to our nursing home last year. But her spirits were high and she was going to get better and return to her beloved Hanksville home. A visit to her at the nursing home was an experience where the VISITOR would be cheered up! She possessed a strong religious faith which gave her courage in those dark days.

But as courageous as she was, the inevitable conquered. Mrs. Beane passed on, at the age of 88, just before Christmas last year. She was a true pioneer of our Club, and those of us who knew her so well miss her deeply. But we know that as she walks that Trail of Gold in the Great Beyond, she often looks down upon her beloved Camel's Hump, under whose shadows she spent so many happy years.

BEN ROLSTON


JOHN H. VONDELL

Suddenly on September 22, 1967 the Green Mountain Club lost one of its most beloved members.

A Past President of the Green Mountain Club; an honorary member of the Executive Committee of the New England Trail Conference; a retired Professor of the University of Massachusetts; a photographer possessing a God-given talent for capturing the beauty of Vermont on film.

But more than these, John was our friend. Whether an Annual Meeting, or an Intersectional, or a special outing, John was there—with his unique Vermont sense of humor and his camera—always ready to catch someone in a candid shot. His slide showings were always a special attraction which nobody wanted to miss. The Green Mountains always looked greener through the lens of John's camera.

We extend our sincere expression of sympathy to Esther Vondell. She has lost a devoted companion. And we have lost a special friend, whom we are going to miss more and more as the months pass by.

We are all richer, John, because you walked with us.

BURLINGTON SECTION

The long awaited trip to Mt. Katahdin finally took place Aug. 19th through 27th, and Burlington Section members were joined there by GMC President Bob Attenborough, and Peggy Pons and Dave Van Dusen of the N.Y. Section. The latter group was joined the following day by Dane Shortsleeve's day hikers on the top of Camel's Hump. Dane reported that there were between seventy-five and a hundred people on the Hump!

Newest Burlington Section End-to-Ender is Rod Rice, Jr., who finished during the Intersectional week. Congratulations, Rod.

MONTPELIER SECTION

Since our last report, we have encountered more foul than fair weather. July 9 we hiked from the summit of Mansfield to Taylor Lodge and down to Lake Mansfield. There was fog on the summit, and rain before the day ended. The Weatherman favored the annual picnic at Groton Pond, where we swam, canoed, water-skied and ATE. We also had fine weather for a climb up Wheeler Mt. in Barton followed by swimming in Crystal Lake.

This mountain offers fine views and plenty of blueberries. August 19 a group of 8 climbed Camel's Hump to Gorham Lodge in the rain. They found it already occupied by Boy Scouts. However, the scouts were cooperative and congenial. In the morning they came down by the Alpine Trail, picking blueberries as they came.

September 9 we had a short hike on some back roads, and were blessed with pleasant weather. The following day we had scheduled a long hike from Smugglers Notch to Route 15 and followed through with it in spite of cold wet weather (only 3 brave souls ventured out). September 16 four members camped at Passaconaway, N.H. and climbed Osceola the following day. September 24 we scheduled a "foliage hike" to Taylor Lodge. In spite of rain and little foliage, 8 adults and 3 children took part and enjoyed a spaghetti supper.

Future events are: weekend camping trip to the Adirondacks, climb up Camel's Hump and Jay Peak, combination long-and-short hikes, a moonlight (?) hike and a Christmas party.

FRANCES HOLMES, Reporter

PIONEER VALLEY SECTION

The gap in the LT shelter system was filled early this August by the construction of a fine new shelter, "Congdon Camp." This enclosed frame cabin was built by Jack Harrington and his LT Patrol and is ready for the fall season. There are built-in bunks and a table, and later on a stove will be added so we can enjoy overnight winter hikes. The camp is located in an attractive spot with a small brook nearby and a larger brook down the hill. It is about a mile east of the site of the burned down "Deer View Shelter" and can be reached by an alternate route marked by orange tape and signs. Hikers can get on the alternate route when traveling north by turning east at the sign which is 7.0 miles north of the Mass. line, and also just south of Sucker Pond. Hikers traveling south can pick it up on the route just below Harmon Hill at the sign 9.7 miles north of the Mass. Line. This route is being changed and improved weekly so please be patient with us if at times the trail is not up to the best of LT standards.

The many work parties necessary this summer and fall have to some extent curtailed our hiking activities, but now that we have a route through to the new shelter we hope to schedule more hikes. Much interest has been shown in snowshoe hikes so we will plan several this winter, with possibly an overnight for those who like it rugged. Our wish to open up contact with other clubs and sections is meeting with some success and already we have made several new friends and shared a few hikes together. Once again I wish to remind the Chairman of Activities that a card to our Chairman (Mrs. D. Knight, 152 Lucerne Rd., Springfield, Mass. 01109) will be answered with as much of our schedule as is ready at that time. She will also be happy to discuss any ideas for joint hikes, etc.

Some of us are attempting to hike the entire Metacomet-Monadnock Trail in Mass. and also the Appalachian Trail in Mass. We do a short section at a time and if this interests you, drop our Activities Chairman a line and join us. Several of our members are searching for second-hand low cost snowshoes and if you know where some can be found, please write to me and I will pass the information on (R. Metcalfe, 11 Lakevilla Ave., Springfield, Mass. 01109).

RICHARD METCALFE, Reporter

NEW MASS.-R.I. GUIDE

The Appalachian Mountain Club has published the second edition of the AMC Massachusetts and Rhode Island Trail Guide (1967). This guide is an extensive revision of its predecessor which first appeared in 1964. It contains 442 fact-filled pages, 134 more than the first edition. Two new maps have been added bringing the total number to eight. An important new section gives information on the many Massachusetts Wildlife Management Areas.

The removable maps in the cover pockets include Mt. Greylock and adjacent region; Mass. and R.I. with a key to the U.S.G.S. Topographic Maps; Blue Hills Reservation, Mass.; and R.I., Inter-Park Trails, Southwestern Section.

Sections list the Mass. and R.I. Parks and Forests, noting what facilities are available. Also listed are the Properties of The Trustees of Reservations (Mass.) with the particular interest of each; as well as the Audubon Sanctuaries and Refuges.

This new guide is a must for anyone interested in hiking, camping or just enjoying the out-of-doors in a natural setting in either Massachusetts or Rhode Island. It is available at a cost of $4.50 from the Appalachian Mountain Club, 5 Joy St., Boston, Mass. 02108.

LONG TRAIL PATROL

As predicted, the Long Trail Patrol had a busy summer. In August, the boys went in to Sterling Pond Lodge where lumber and supplies were floated across the Pond practically to the door of the Lodge. The roof and bunks were repaired, some vertical logs were replaced in front, and shutters were made for the front windows instead of glass. These are made so they swing in and are mounted vertically, each window having two, one on each side to eliminate the heaviness of one big shutter. The dump was moved and the cabin creosoted inside and out so we will see how the Lodge will survive another season.

From Sterling Pond, the Patrol went to Fay Fuller to set up headquarters for building the new Congdon Shelter for the Pioneer Valley Section. A site was decided upon by a committee, lumber was brought and for the first week we were lucky to have the valuable assistance of Roy and Chester Buchanan to start the actual building and framing. The Patrol finished Congdon the second week in August and moved back north to clear trail from Frying Pan Brook to Hazen's Notch, through the Jay Peak area to the Border. The Jay Peak area needs a relocation which was not accomplished because of work being done by the chair lift people. We haven't had any reports of anyone getting lost on that section of Trail, but relocation should be on the program for next season.

The Trail is standardized from Ithiel Falls to Parker and speed-cleared from Parker to the Canadian Border. Side trails in this area were also cleared.

The Patrol wound up their activities by Intersectional week and the Long Trail Patrol truck was used at the Intersectional for various duties.

Two of the boys are back at school. Dave Govatski, who graduated this year, has applied for hutmaster in Pinkham Notch and will work there until the closing of the huts.

The Patrol didn't finish everything they set out to do, but a new crew did a good job.

The last weekend in September, a delegation from Burlington headed by Roy Buchanan and Rod Rice went down to Bennington to put the finishing touches on Congdon. They installed a stove and almost finished a new outhouse except for putting on roofing and a door. The door is ready for hinges. This work party welcomed the assistance of a large group from the Pioneer Valley and Bennington Sections as well as various other assorted members including our President Bob Attenborough, who came in carrying much of the lumber and supplies and giving moral support.

And so ends the saga of the Long Trail Patrol for 1967.

JACK HARRINGTON, Chairman
Long Trail Patrol


NEW BATTELL SHELTER

Some shelter materials have arrived by truck. And by wagon. And by tractors. And on the backs of men.

But the materials for the new Battell Shelter came by air! In June, the United States Forest Service had the materials brought in by helicopter.

In July, work parties from Tamarack Farm, Plymouth Union, Vt., one of the camp units of Farm and Wilderness Camps, began the assembly of the shelter. This work, under the supervision of camp counselors, was done for the Forest Service as a service project. Subsequent work parties returned until the shelter was completed.

The new shelter replaces the old Battell Shelter which was built in 1938 by the Long Trail Patrol for the New York Section of the GMC.

Our thanks to the staff of the Green Mountain National Forest, and to the volunteers of Tamarack Farm, for another new shelter for our many Long Trail hikers.


CONGDON SHELTER

Congdon Shelter, which is really a nice closed camp, was a gift to the Green Mountain Club from the Congdon Family in memory of Herbert Wheaton Congdon (1876–1965). Mr. Congdon was an active member and trustee for many years. He made many of the early maps of the Long Trail and was a contributing author to the book "Footpath in the Wilderness," published by the Middlebury College Press in 1941. He wrote interestingly of the wild life on the trail. "Praise be, one of the delights of our Trail is the absence of danger from anything but our own carelessness."

His keen feeling for the Trail was unmistakeable.

"Leisureliness, taking time to see and hear thoughtfully and intelligently, studying to be quiet, opens new vistas that lead to serenity. All at once the quiet of the wilderness comes alive, the silence is filled with undertones, the sounds of wild things unafraid and undisturbed. There is an exaltation to be felt on rocky peaks with far views of forest-clad hills and peaceful valleys that is emphasized by the busy chickadees hunting bugs on the balsams close by, the fragrance of the hot earth, the sound of a brook far below. Suddenly one feels the importance of the exquisite perfection of a tiny fern, the flash of a tanager or the contemplative assurance of a thrush's song.

"These things give us a sense of permanence, a glimpse into a serene world that has passed through the biting sculpture of Time and has learned to endure hardship, still knowing happiness and being able to transmit it to us."

ATLAS PLYWOOD PROVIDES NEW SHELTER

Shortly before ten o'clock on September 9 a truck from the F.O and Phelps Sawmill of Morriville came to a stop at the Long Trail crossing of Route 242 south of Jay Peak. The load it carried was rather special in the eyes of the dozen or more people awaiting its arrival, and without any urging they quickly transferred the cargo to a carefully chosen spot located a few feet north of the road.

The next few minutes were spent in selecting and carefully placing several large rocks in a square. This done, a rugged platform of plywood roughly eight feet square was taken from the truck's cargo and expertly leveled up.

A Floor

Now the pace quickened a bit, and in rapid succession two side walls, each consisting of vertical maple plywood cores were set in place. Within minutes after this the rear wall had been fitted precisely to the side walls.

Walls

The one piece plywood roof was a bit heavier and more awkward to handle, but it too was quickly maneuvered into its proper location.

A Roof

Lo and behold! We were putting a lean-to together! With all pieces in place, the new structure was literally the center of a swarm of activity.

Busy Bees

Up on the roof a crew was busily laying down and nailing tar paper, while directly underneath other people were actively spiking and bolting everything down securely. Outside a couple of people were happily creosoting everything in sight, including the shelter; they showed so much talent with a paint brush, in fact, that they were allowed to do the inside as well. Meanwhile others were building a drywall to keep out the porcupines. And so it went until early afternoon.

A Shelter in a Day

The result is the new Atlas Valley Shelter, located on the site of the original Jay Camp and featuring a scenic view of the valley to the west from which the new lean-to takes its name. This rugged and attractive shelter is a gift from the Atlas Plywood Division, over whose lands much of the Long Trail passes between Jay Camp and the Ritterbush Pond area. The shelter was designed and prefabricated in the Company's Morrisville Plant under the direction of the Company Forester, Warren B. Drown. Mr. Drown was at the site to supervise the assembly of the shelter.

In providing this new facility, Mr. Drown pointed out, that the Atlas Plywood Division has taken another step to emphasize that recreational use of its extensive northern Vermont timberlands is an important aspect of the Company's policy of wise "multiple use" management. The new lean-to will serve both hikers and non-hikers who visit the area. Its handy roadside location provides a convenient and sheltered place for hikers awaiting their transportation home. Tourists and Long Trail visitors will find it an attractive picnic site. In addition, of course, it offers shelter for three or four hikers should nearby Jay Camp be fully occupied.

Atlas Valley Shelter is a fine addition to the growing system of Long Trail shelter facilities, and the GMC is very grateful to Atlas Plywood Division for providing this fine new lean-to. We are sure that Atlas Valley Shelter will receive many years of appreciative use.

GEORGE PEARLSTEIN

LAKE PLEIAD SHELTER REPAIRS

Eight Scouts and six adults from Troop 45, Granville, New York, undertook major repairs to Lake Pleiad Shelter on September 22-23, 1967. In addition to Scoutmaster George Pearlstein, the adults included the troop's Institutional Representative and his wife, Neighborhood Commissioner Ray Bogart and his wife, and Troop Committeeman Harry Bogart. Both Bogarts are professional carpenters and have been active in Granville area Scouting for several years.

The Unit arrived at Middlebury Gap shortly after dark on Friday evening. Due to darkness, fog and wet footing, it was deemed unwise and unsafe to attempt to get the material to the site that evening. Instead the material was removed from the truck and taken part way up the trail where it was left until morning. On Friday evening six people slept at Lake Pleiad Shelter, two slept in a tent in front of the shelter, and six Scouts elected to stay at Worth Mountain Lodge.

Following a 7:30 breakfast cooked by the women, work began without delay. Two Scouts assisted in meal cleanup and in setting up a cooking area behind the shelter, while six Scouts were detailed to transport the lumber to the site. Meanwhile the rest of the crew ripped up the old flooring and removed the outside sheathing from the rear wall of the shelter. This produced a large pile of lumber which was carefully sorted to yield some reusable material.

Next the front of the shelter was raised about three inches with hydraulic jacks and carefully leveled up. Three new stone piers were built to support the front in its new location. Then the rear of the shelter was jacked up to permit removal of the rotten rear sill. A new sill, consisting of two by sixes spliced together, was then spiked in place. Three stone piers were built in the rear, and the whole floor structure was placed in a level position. Also two intermediate stone piers were added under the side walls. The net effect of operations to this point was to insure that all portions of the sill structure were at least six inches above ground.

Following complete examination of all joists and timbers for structural soundness, the sills, joists, and other structural members were thoroughly creosoted on all surfaces. Next, new flooring of 5/4 spruce tongue and groove planking was nailed into place. All boards were creosoted on the underside prior to installation. Then the rear window was boarded in, and the rear wall completely sheathed with spruce shiplap.

With major repairs completed, all bunks were repaired and reinforced as required. In addition, most of the sag in the roof was eliminated by installing a center post consisting of two by fours spiked together. Other operations consisted of partial completing of a drywall under the shelter walls, creosoting of the front portion of the floor, and partial cleanup of the immediate premises. However, some work was left undone since it was not possible for the troop to remain overnight on Saturday.

WORK REMAINING TO BE DONE

Due to a shortage of nails, the rear sheathing was not fully nailed, although there is no danger of it coming loose in the near future. One row of nails is required at the top and at the bottom.

It was planned to board in the stove pipe hole and the two side windows, but time ran out. There is material at the site for this job, which can be done at any time.

Only the front portion of the floor was creosoted due to lack of time. Both the floor and the new rear wall should be thoroughly creosoted. One gallon of creosote is at the site; another will probably be required.

The dry wall should be completed to keep out porcupines. In addition, porky screening should be installed in the future. It is planned to complete the wall this fall.

There are several holes in the roof which should be repaired. Patching material is at the site.

A toilet is badly needed for this shelter. There should be enough material at the site to construct a crude facility; additional material will be required for a more elaborate toilet. Recommend that the Long Trail Patrol be given this assignment next summer.

A can dump should be dug and the premises spruced up. Also some additional brush should be removed from the immediate vicinity.

The water supply should be protected by digging out a large pool and installing a cover and a pipe.

GEORGE PEARLSTEIN

OUT ON THE TRAIL
(Or, We Hate Creosote)

By PORK U. PINE, Jr.

Two years ago, from our daytime perch in a big beech tree we watched as a large group of humans, led by a huge chugging tractor, invaded OUR territory. Behind that tractor was a wagon, and on that wagon appeared to be some sort of structure, somewhat dismantled.

Those humans put down some very indigestible cement blocks, and on those blocks they carefully assembled that structure. We heard them call it Lost Pond Shelter. Who cares about Lost Pond? We were hungry. Pass the salt, please.

We waited a week until some more humans returned to put on the shingles. After all, those same humans wouldn't eat cake until the icing had been added, would they?

Then, assured our privacy, we investigated this new delicacy. The brown stain didn't taste too bad, neither did the brush that the stain had been put on with. Then we sampled the floor joists and sills. Delicious! They must have soaked up some of that salt in the air at Cape Cod.

We were doing fine on the floor—even chewed through a couple of the floor boards—when someone discovered that we had been enjoying our meals. It was a sad day when two humans from some place called Schenectady came right into our dining room and nailed chicken wire around our dinner. Foiled!

But we were determined. How about going up into the shelter and sampling its interior? Great! Say now, those studs don't taste bad either. And those nice big fat center posts! This is real dining.

Tragedy struck again. We saw him again from our perch. That fellow from Schenectady with a big can of something and a big brush. Whatever it was, he spread it on thick. Then he departed. Perhaps we should investigate.

Ugh, what HORRIBLE tasting stuff. The label on the can called it creosote. Somehow, we weren't as hungry as we had been. Wherever we chewed, we tasted the stuff. Wait, let's go up the corner post. Yup, he forgot the rafters! They tasted as good as the floor used to. Hooray, back to our meal.

But we couldn't win. Back came the human, this time to nail tin right on the rafter where we had just dined. And to add more creosote. And to creosote the beech tree we had sampled. And to creosote the out-house. Creosote, creosote, bah!

But now we suspect trickery. We saw him return to put some salt around on some nearby stumps. S'funny, he's being nice to us for a change, giving us 100 percent pure salt. Must be a catch to it somewhere. Nobody has ever been THAT nice to us. Do you suppose he's trying to bait us, only to sneak up some night and assassinate us?

We'll fool him—we'll move down to Big Branch Shelter. We heard that the U.S. Forest Service doesn't use creosote.

Foiled Again!

ASCUTNEY MOUNTAIN

A second trail up Ascutney has now been reopened, blazed, measured and signed. It's the Weatherfield Trail, taking off from Route 131, from the end of a dirt road just a little to the west of the old Marsh Schoolhouse and before coming to the Paul Jarvis place (which is where this trail used to start). It's about 2½ miles long. Main point of interest is Crystal Cascade, where Ascutney Brook falls some 70 feet off a granite cliff. The Ascutney Trails Association is now working on a third trail which will follow part of the route of the old Brownsville Trail. It will be about three miles long and will pass up through the old Norcross (granite) quarry above Route 44.

Ascutney Guide Books are now available by mail from Ralph Bissell, 90 State St., Windsor, for $1.25 (please note correction in price).
