GOVERNOR'S CONFERENCE

The Governor's Conference on Natural Beauty was held on May 25-26, 1966 at Montpelier, Vermont. Miss Shirley Strong, President of the Burlington Section and Chairman of the Special Scenic Parkway Committee of our Main Club, represented the GMC and gave the following presentation at the panel entitled "Contributions of Citizen Organizations."

"Green Mountains—what an important asset they are to Vermont! The very name Vermont means 'Green Mountains.' Many organizations and business concerns use Green Mountain in their name to designate the state in which they are located. So, too, the Green Mountain Club, but here the name was selected primarily because the Club is concerned with the mountains themselves.

Back in 1910 men with vision had the foresight to conceive the idea of the Long Trail—a footpath running along the mountain ridges. They wanted to make the mountains play a larger part in the lives of the people. So the Green Mountain Club was formed to build and maintain a "Footpath in the Wilderness" the length of the state, offering people an opportunity to enjoy the mountains through hiking.

From that core of 23 people, membership grew rapidly and within 10 years the Long Trail had been blazed all the way from Massachusetts to Johnson; the first Guide Book to the Trail had already been published; and five sections of the Club had been established, including a New York Section. By 1931 the Trail had been completed from Massachusetts to Canada and numerous shelters had been constructed along it.

Today this trail system is extensive. The Long Trail itself extends 260 miles from Massachusetts to Canada. An additional 175 miles of approach trails make the grand total of some 435 miles in all. There are 67 shelters—located on an average of every 4 miles. Some of these are enclosed cabins, others three-sided leantos.

It's easy to reach the Trail within a short time via the crossroads through the mountains and by the various approach trails. Therefore, it is possible to spend a few hours or a day on the Trail—or, using the shelters, one may remain several days hiking in the mountains.

How is all this maintained? Primarily by the Green Mountain Club, which is comprised of so-called "Sections"—eight in all. Six in Vermont, one in New York, and one in Massachusetts. And just this spring an enthusiastic group in Springfield, Mass., has asked to become the ninth Section.

Each Section has responsibility for a certain part of the Long Trail, its approach trails and shelters, and, through trail clearing and maintenance trips, the members keep their section cleared and blazed. Where the Long Trail passes through the Green Mountain National Forest, we are indebted to the U.S. Forest Service which maintains it in this area. Also individuals often take it upon themselves to care for certain parts of the Trail—sometimes this means a member and his family; sometimes just an interested person anxious to do his bit.

The maintenance of the remaining part of the Trail is the responsibility of the GMC's Long Trail Patrol, organized in 1931 under Roy Buchanan. This Patrol also comes to the aid of Sections who need assistance with extensive shelter repairs and trail maintenance.

So the maintenance of the whole trail system, like its construction, is one big cooperative affair of interested individuals lending their time and energy just because they have a sincere love of the mountains and treasure the opportunity to enjoy their beauty. And because they want to share this with others. They ask nothing in return but that others enjoy them too and take similar pride in the Trail and shelters.

Appeal—Mountains have a "beckoning" power of their own, a certain magnetism for many people—through the need to explore, a desire to get to the top, an opportunity to get away from a hectic pace and breathe in the beauties of nature. In reading the registers in the shelters, and in talking with people one meets on the Trail (yes, somehow people just do speak as they pass on the Trail and sit together enjoying the frequent lookouts), one realizes just who find the mountains appealing—the real outdoor people; the professional person who wants to get away for relaxation, away from the phone (there is no phone service on the Trail or in the shelters!); teachers; UN officials; family groups; young people—full of energy, seeking an opportunity to be on their own, eager to experience their wilderness heritage, a chance for self reliance—Boy Scouts; Girl Scouts; church youth groups finding "trail camps" an inspirational experience; Vermont's summer camps planning trips for their campers.

Inquiries that are directed to the Club indicate the popularity of the Trail—inquiries from individuals and groups from Delaware, New Jersey, Washington, Minnesota, Arizona, California, in fact, all over the U.S. They ask about short hikes, bikes available from camping areas in the state, trips on the whole length of the Trail. More and more people, too, are meeting the challenge and becoming "end-to-enders," having hiked the whole 260 miles. Of the 42 who officially became "end-to-enders" last year, it is interesting to note that all but 10 were out-of-staters.

Yes, the mountains are important to Vermont—they offer wilderness character and scenic beauty, as well as recreational opportunities. Whether one hikes, one skis, one sits and looks, or one drives along our Vermont roads and looks up and over at them—they are an important asset of the "Beckoning Country"—but they are also important to Vermonters themselves. They stand out like a spinal column down the center part of the state. Vermont is a narrow state, and from most every part of it one can view some part of this range. Its profile line is so very familiar to all of us. For example—as we travel along the interstate between Burlington and Montpelier, as we ride the ferry across Lake Champlain toward Burlington, as we drive along the lengths of Rts. 100 and 7, over Orange Heights toward Barre.

With change, and there has been change and there will continue to be change—with pressures of increased population, of increased leisure, with pressures for more development,—can we be sure that the wilderness character of the mountains, this scenic profile of the Green Mountains, will last? (It's wonderful to know that at least for Camel's Hump it will!) Is there a long-range plan for preserving the scenic beauty of the mountains—their wilderness character?

Will more and more recreational developments encroach upon the mountain sides in a rather hit or miss fashion and yes, on the summits as at Jay, until overuse and overdevelopment do, indeed, destroy the very scenic quality which makes our present recreational facilities in the mountains so attractive and appealing?

Perhaps certain guidelines are needed so that as further pressures for development are exerted their advisibility may be more easily assessed in terms of the overall environmental quality.

The mountains are there, still relatively unspoiled. As we work to improve the scenic qualities of our roadsides and of our villages, which are important aspects of our state's scenic beauty, let's don't take the scenic grandeur and wilderness character of the mountains for granted. Careful thought needs to be given now so that Vermont will indeed remain the state of the beautiful Green Mountains."

JAY PEAK AREA CLOSED

We understand that the Jay Peak Ski Center is blasting on Jay Peak and that they close the area from 7:00 A.M. each Monday to 5:00 P.M. on Friday, and this is likely to be the case until Labor Day. One reason for this seems to be that transistor radios can set up enough feedback so that they may set off dynamite charges in the area.

With all the construction in the summit area of Jay Peak, hikers please watch carefully for the Long Trail signs.

BRATTLEBORO SECTION

Following is a report of the annual meeting of the Brattleboro Section, held on April 9, 1966:

(1) Seventeen attended—fourteen hiked up Mt. Wantastiquet and went from top to Mine Mt. looking for the lead mine. The group found the yellow ochre mine after a strenuous thru-the-woods scramble.

(2) Thirteen met at the warming hut at Brattleboro Memorial Park for the supper and business meeting. Trails and Shelter committee—Brandon Douglas and Rex Coleman reported Swezey Shelter and Bromley Camp in excellent condition. Swezey Camp had a leaky roof. These places had all been visited within the past month. The Forest Service has replaced the latrine at Bromley Camp with a "modern design job."

(3) Rexford and Elizabeth Coleman were congratulated on their accomplishment of hiking from the Mass. line to the Canadian border—earning their End-To-End Award.

(4) It was reported that four of our members had been active in organizing a new section of the GMC in Springfield, Mass.—all live in that area—Mr. and Mrs. Ralph Jay, Bill Hawkins and Jim Barrett.

(5) Discussion about the Intersectional being held in August at the Boy Scout Camp on Grout Pond near Stratton.

(6) A discussion of the proposed scenic highway along the tops of the Green Mts. was held after Rexford Coleman reported that he learned about it at the trustees' meeting.

(7) In addition to the officers listed in the Directory, we have Ann Davis, Program Chairman, and Rexford Coleman and Brandon Douglas, Co-Chairmen of Trails and Shelters.

(8) Ann Davis has worked up an interesting program of activities for our section and we welcome all those who would like to join us. For details please contact Louise Dunklee, 11 Chase St., Brattleboro, phone 802-354-4678.

LOUISE DUNKLEE,      Reporter

BURLINGTON SECTION

The Burlington Section had an active Spring, although snow in the mountains until the end of May forced postponement of one hike. Those of us who climbed Camel's Hump the weekend before the Annual Meeting were knee deep in snow from Gorham Lodge on up.

During June the emphasis was on work hikes, and rumor has it that Puffer Lodge is now the proud possessor of half an out house! At the moment the Properties Committee and several other Burlington GMCers are spending most of their spare time in the Adirondacks, trying to catch up with Elsie Washburn and Marion Holmes, who will probably be sewing on their 46er emblems about the time you're reading this article. We're happy to welcome back from her winter in Florida the third "Middlebury Puffer," Betty Myers, who will be living in Burlington this year.

According to Roy Buchanan, Rod Rice and Harris Abbott who were asked to consult with the Educational TV officials on the location of the new Mt. Mansfield tower, it will be situated about 75 feet east of the present tower, and should not hurt the profile of the mountain any further than is already the case.

We extend a hearty welcome to all GMCers, especially members-at-large, vacationing in our area, and invite you to come hike with us this summer. For details of our activities please contact our Outings Chairman, Miss Mary F. Forrester, 14 Germain St., Burlington, Vt. 05401.

JAN ABBOTT, Reporter

Event

The Fiftieth Anniversary Banquet-Luncheon of the New York Section was held at 12:30 P.M., April 16th, in a beautiful big room at Sterling Forest Gardens, Tuxedo, N.Y. The Committee, under Lillian Lodge, Chairman, did a perfect job and the event was completely unflawed. Present were 300 members, including people from Montana, Delaware, Massachusetts and Pennsylvania.

The room and tables were beautifully decorated in yellow and gold and white. Programs, name tags, place cards and posters had been made by some of the artists in the Club. At each place was a gift address book wrapped in gold and inscribed with the Club's name.

The President greeted the throng; then a delicious luncheon was served. There was a reminiscent speech, a club solo, a club song presented for a prize, group singing, and greetings from absent members. Two copies of the Club History were presented, beautifully bound, with a third copy for the author, Astrid Lier. A large 3-tiered birthday cake was cut and served to all.

A plaque was presented to Chairman Lillian Lodge, in appreciation of her efforts with the Luncheon. In her deeply moving remarks Lillian said in part: "We are not just another outdoor club. We are more than that ... we have found the secret of living together harmoniously at Thendara. We work well together on the trails and in building shelters. We have had a tremendous amount of fun in doing all this. The last 50 years are crowded with pleasant memories of the good times we have had together. ... We have given generously of our time, money and labor in building trails and shelters so that others might enjoy them. ... We have taken an active and vigorous part in conservation matters. ... We like to be together—witness this wonderful turnout today .... As we advance toward the century mark, I know you all will join with me in saying from the heart—Long Live the Club!"

If my figures are correct 104 luncheon tickets were sold and 68 dinner tickets. The coffee and doughnuts were courtesy of the Montpelier Section. Killington Section served a delicious picnic lunch and the buffet, also delicious, was served by the Long Trail Lodge. Assisting in the pre-planning for the meeting were Terry Gulick and Harold Valiquette. Doris Washburn and Fred Smith ably assisted with the registration and ticket selling.

Our after-dinner speaker was Mr. Carl Jester of the Northeast regional office of the Bureau of Outdoor Recreation. Mr. Jester, who wrote the regional report for the Nationwide System of Trails Study, gave a brief background on the development of the B.O.R. and described its functions. In relation to the Trails Study he quoted from President Johnson's Natural Beauty Message and Natural Heritage Message charging the Dept. of the Interior with developing a nationwide system of trails. He discussed Secretary Udall's bill providing for a nationwide system of trails under four classifications: (1) National scenic trails, with the Appalachian Trail being the first trail so designated; (2) Federal park and forest recreation trails; (3) State park and forest recreation trails, and (4) Metropolitan area trails. In relation to the study, Mr. Jester stated that the Northeast office had been assigned two aspects of the study: (1) State administered trails and (2) Metropolitan area trails. Interest varied in the latter. Concerning the former, it was found that there were more privately administrated trails than state-administered trails and in Vermont no state administered trails at all but rather the Long Trail, administered by the Green Mountain Club, and he stated, perhaps this is how it should be—a partnership. In answer to a question concerning inclusion of the Long Trail in Secretary Udall's Bill, Mr. Jester stated that it is not included except insofar as the Long Trail and the Appalachian Trail coincide.

Guests at the meeting included Mr. Jester's father; Commissioner of Forests and Parks, Perry Merrill, and Mrs. Merrill; and Coordinator of Natural Resources for the State of Vermont, David Walker, and Mrs. Walker. The persons coming the longest distance for the meeting were Frances Dickerson from Winona, Minnesota, and Mr. and Mrs. Dale Perkins attending from Tallmadge, Ohio. We had one "international" member attending from Montreal.

LONG TRAIL PATROL

The Patrol started work on June 1 with 2 men. The third man was in school for three weeks more. The first week they speed-cleared the Long Trail from Ithiel Falls to Frying Pan Brook. The second week was devoted to repair work on Montclair Glen where a new floor was back-packed in from the Monroe Farm approach, floor was laid, bunks repaired, table and seats, etc., done. The Patrol leader's grandfather died on Tues. of that week so, for the last three days, there was only one man working at Montclair Glen.

The third week the two men started work on Theron Dean Shelter. There is timber enough in the area so that this shelter can be built of logs. It is open front, smaller than the standard shelter and will sleep 6 or so if the sleepers are not packed in sardine fashion. Boards for roof, floor and bunks have been brought up on one of the Glen Ellen Ski Lifts as were the needed nails, roofing and creosote oil. Thanks are due to the Glen Ellen group for this great help. This material will have to be backpacked only a quarter of a mile to the site. At the end of the third week, the trail gang had cut and peeled 18 logs for the shelter and had hauled in more than half of them. They reported that they have fed innumerable black flies and no-see-ums.

Now, at near the end of the fourth week, no report is in concerning their accomplishments. However, there were three men on the job this week so the shelter is no doubt growing by leaps and bounds—or do I mean by slaps and bites?

ROY BUCHANAN, Chairman

OUT ON THE TRAIL

One year ago, on May 8 and 9, 1965, a Long Trail shelter was trucked from Cape Cod to Danby, hauled by tractor and wagon up to the Long Trail, and assembled on its permanent site. We named it Lost Pond Shelter.

This year, on May 7, an anniversary get-together was held at the shelter. Louis Stare, the shelter builder and donor, came from Cape Cod. The Rolstons came from Schenectady. George Pearlstein came from West Pawlet. Irving White and Mauri Wintturi came from Massachusetts. And Bob and Brian Attenborough came from Connecticut.

We came to the shelter to celebrate its first birthday. And to reminisce about the trials and tribulations of moving the shelter a year earlier. And to inspect the shelter and do some work on the foundation. And to eat lunch.

Following the consumption of the usual assortment of foods found at any trail gathering, out came a big cake, topped with one plumber's candle, and the strained voices of "Happy Birthday, Dear Shelter" echoed up toward Baker Peak and down toward Big Branch.

The ceremonies completed, we set out to find Lost Pond (which had eluded its original "discoverer" the previous fall). We found it! Someone suggested renaming the shelter "Found Pond Shelter," but the vote was NO.

Upon inspection of Lost Pond, we found it to be of great interest, for it consists of a small open pool surrounded by a quaking bog. Further inspection found the mat to contain pitcher plants and other bog growth.

Since this rediscovery of Lost Pond, serious consideration has been given to NOT building the Lost Pond Trail to the pond. We who have seen the pond feel that the unusual plant life there should be protected.

If, on your first or next visit to Lost Pond Shelter, you do not see a trail sign indicating the Lost Pond Trail, you will know that the decision has been made to let the pond remain inaccessible by trail. You may then make a notation on Page 51 of your 1966 Guide Book that the trail to Lost Pond will not be constructed. Thus the pond, in name AND reality, will remain LOST POND.

BEN ROLSTON
