Annual Meeting Considers "Lulu's;"
Elects Bristow GMC President

The 1983 meeting of the members of the Green Mountain Club was held for the second straight year in the congenial surroundings of the Northfield campus of Norwich University. The meeting started with a near disaster - a complete lack of coffee - but went straight uphill from there. It seems that the Norwich food service staff was under the impression that the meeting was to be held the NEXT day! No one was willing to take responsibility for so egregious an error, so members were obliged to survive without the benefit of caffeine.

In any case, the meeting was under way promptly at 9:45 A.M. on May 28th, and thereafter moved along with uncharacteristic celerity, driven by trail boss Jim Wilkinson. This despite the rather foggy condition of many attendees occasioned by the lack of coffee (or perhaps because of it.).

President Wilkinson began the initial phase of the meeting by introducing host Donald M. Wallace, a faculty member at Norwich. Don noted the bright side of the coffee problem by observing that the club saved $100 by not indulging in coffee and donuts. Don went on to describe the rest of the Annual Meeting activities that were scheduled, including a tour of some local slate mines and a work party at Montclair Glen Lodge, as well as the arrangements for lunch and dinner.

Bob Hagerman Remembered

Then, a somber occasion; President Wilkinson eulogized Robert L. Hagerman, who passed away in the year since the members last met at Norwich. Jim described Bob Hagerman as a man who distinguished himself as a "member, hiker, editor and officer" of the Green Mountain Club, and as "almost anything you can think of in the hiking community". A moment of silence in memory of this unique person was proposed, and the assembled members stood together in this deeply felt tribute.

Following rapid approval of the minutes of the previous annual meeting, Treasurer Joe Cook was called upon to give his report. He noted that he would give his Treasurer, Budget and Finance Committee and Brattleboro Section reports all at once so that he could get back to Brattleboro and work on a shelter-building project that was unavoidably scheduled for this day. In this respect he noted that the last thing he wanted to do was to "steal" people from Don Wallace's Montclair Glen project, but that if anyone was headed south, well... ......

The year ended April 30, 1983 showed a small surplus, reported Treasurer Cook. This happy result, amounting to $133.16, was after all the internal transfers and deferred income adjustments. As usual, this accounting esoterica left most members thoroughly confused, and as at previous meetings, it rapidly became apparent that the explanations made this confusion even worse. Let it be hoped that future Club presidents continue to hold the office of Treasurer first!

Joe again stated that the faith we place in Harry Peet's fiscal abilities is well rewarded; that Harry's stewardship of our funds is conscientious and astute.

With that thought in mind, it was gratifying to note that the proposed budget for the current fiscal year contains a raise for our erstwhile Executive Director! In addition, the new budget incorporates the staff realignment reported in previous issues of the LTN, the appointments of Northern and Southern Field Assistants. The budget continues spending at about the same levels as the fiscal year just ended. It calls for income and expenses of $112,035, indicative of considerable restraint in respect of spending. The continued support of all members will still be necessary in order to fund adequately the programs incorporated in the budget.

Joe Cook then put on his Brattleboro Section hat and described the shelter building project that the Section was embarked upon. The shelter is being constructed about a half-mile south of Spruce Peak, south of Route 11. Joe noted that the U.S. Forest Service had provided much assistance in this project. The site was cleared the week before the Annual Meeting and the logs were to be brought in today (May 28). With this heavy work falling on the rest of the Section members, Joe probably figured that he would never live it down if he pleaded "Annual Meeting business" kept him from hauling those logs, so after concluding his Brattleboro report, Treasurer Cook departed.

Bear Pond Trail Closed by Landslide

In the early morning hours of July 12th,
several tons of rock broke away from the west
side of Smugglers Notch and created a landslide which propelled several large boulders
to the opposite side of the notch road and
obliterated a portion of the Bear Pond Trail.
State officials who inspected the aftermath
were very much concerned about the stability
of the debris and other rock in the immediate
area, and as a result the decision was made
to prohibit hiking on the Bear Pond Trail.

The Trail
Maintenance
Workshop—A First
Person Report

By Bob Poole

About sixty Green Mountain Club
members attended the trail maintenance
workshop held in Danby on April 30-May 1
at the U.S. Forest Service supply depot. John
Schieman and I took Friday off and came up
from Middletown, CT, arriving in Danby in
the early evening. We were welcomed by Ray
Auger, Field Assistant, and took our places
for the night on folding cots set up in one of
several huge army tents.

Learning by Doing

An enthusiastic group of trail maintenance
experts greeted us after coffee and donuts on
Saturday. We were issued tools and
transported to various accessible work sites.
Some places were still closed due to snow.
John and I joined a group going to work on
the Long Trail going in toward Little Rocky
Pond. Our leaders were Debbie Dorsett and
Don Wallace. We advanced to Lula Tye
shelter, cutting fallen trees, clearing culverts
and repairing water bars.

On the trip back, following a trail lunch,
our intrepid leaders ranged the mountainside,
discovering a place where a massive deadfall
had diverted a stream from its normal course,
threatening erosion of the trail. Fortunately,
we were able to "persuade" the stream back
into its established course before much
damage could be done.

Pleasant and Unpleasant Surprises

We returned to camp and enjoyed a
delicious supper of beef stew prepared by
Herb Reed. Following this came a program
of information and demonstrations on the
proper sharpening of tools, sign routing and
use of propane-fired branding irons such as
those used by both the GMC and ATC.

In the middle of the night we were blasted
out of bed by an enormous crack of thunder
which came with no warning whatsoever. In
the ensuing rain, the tent leaked a bit less than
I thought it would!

Back to Work

After a pancake and sausage breakfast on
Sunday we were privileged to hear a lecture
on chainsaw safety given by Pete Norkeveck
of the Vermont Agency of Environmental
Conservation. I have been using a chainsaw
for 25 years but Pete showed me plenty! He
strongly recommends hard hat, hearing protection, face and eye shields, gloves, ballistic
nylon chaps and a saw with both a chain brake
and anti-kickback chain design.

After a quick demonstration of tree felling,
we were off to Forest Road 10 for more onthe-job training. Mort Phillips of the U.S.
Forest Service got that started with a
demonstration of puncheon construction on
a side trail.

Lunch was again served by Herb Reed,
after which our group reconstructed a couple
of water bars on the trail. Cleo Billings of the
Long Trail Patrol led a crew in building a
flight of logsteps up from the road. After this,
we returned to the depot, broke camp and said
our farewells. John and I reluctantly headed
back to the mundane existence of "real life"
in Connecticut.

Membership

Membership - Individuals can join the Green Mountain Club in either of two ways, At Large (membership in the main Club only) or Section (membership in one of the Club's 14 local and regional units known as sections; section membership automatically means membership in the main Club as well). Write HQ for dues schedules and other information about both forms of membership.

GMC Decal - Decal of the GMC emblem in green on white with water-soluble glue for affixing on car windshield or other see-through surface; order from HQ; 50 cents plus SASE.

Sections Report on Their Condition

Bennington—says "thanks" for the trail workshop to which eleven section members went. The contribution of women to section activities was noted—five of the eleven workshop attendees and all new members are women. There are two activities, minimum, every month. In spite of the rainy spring, only one was cancelled. Hikes draw ten to twenty-five hikers. The section is starting its own endowment. The family of Ted Goddard will contribute in excess of $1000 to the cause of rebuilding Gastenbury Shelter. With 72 members, Bennington is in excellent shape!

Burlington—the second largest section, reports that all is well. Much effort went into their section of the walk-through, but fortunately there was little damage to the trail during the rather mild winter. The new route near Bolton was successfully completed and opened with appropriate ceremonies. The reroute still needs work, however, and shelter and tent platform sites are under investigation. The Butler Lodge vandalism has been repaired, and the stove removed. Dues have been increased but membership is on the rise. At last count there were 370 members, which would move Burlington into 1st place in size if New York's last reported membership of 365 has not increased. There is at least one outing per weekend and frequently more, all scheduled far in advance and published on a convenient wallet sized card distributed with the Section's excellent newsletter "Ridge Lines".

Connecticut—was not officially represented, but someone in the know reported that the section was alive and well. This would certainly be the conclusion of anyone reading their well-edited newsletter, which invariably describes an active and varied schedule of work parties and outings.

Killington—membership is up, now 86 members, and they have an active outing schedule, which has recently been increased from one activity every other week to one every week. They have recently surveyed their entire section of trail and report that it is in generally good shape though several shelters are in need of refurbishing.

Montpelier—scheduled 54 events and only canned seven. There were six work parties, sixteen hikes and a wide variety of other things to do. Andy Nuquist was saluted as "most active" of the current 95 members. This year there were two end-to-enders.

Montreal—was not represented, a rather unusual occurrence for this usually active section. Still, no one present seemed to know what was going on up north of the border.

New York—made a brief report. Membership levels remain about the same, but the members seem to be younger. Activity level is still high and getting better all the time.

Ottauquechee—is really rolling—with 87 members they did their whole walk-through, schedule a trip every weekend and are seeing increased participation in all areas. The Gulf Shelter is their current big project, but they are in a quandary over how to move it.

Worcester—is alive and well with 161 members. They have a new shelter at Stratton Pond and an active schedule of activities.

The Board of Directors meeting held immediately after the Annual Meeting of the members went relatively quickly, with the only items inspiring any active debate being the issues of the Club's position on wilderness designation and the manner in which the NFS-Killington compromise was effected.

The minutes of the previous meeting, Presidents Report and Executive Director's Reports were incorporated by reference from the Annual Meeting. Harry did make note of the encouraging fact that not only was an excellent trail staff recruited without the problems of last year, but that entirely acceptable applicants had to be turned away this season.

LT System Management Plan

As promised at the earlier meeting, George Plumb presented the LT system management plan for the consideration of the Directors. Several questions arose regarding possible conflicts between this statement of general principles for the maintenance and operation of the LT system and the current Trails and Shelters procedures as well as Appalachian Trail Conference plans and procedures. Mr. Plumb patiently emphasized that this was the reason that the plan had been submitted as a draft, so that problems of this nature could be identified and resolved. The plan will be considered again at the September Board meeting.

Tents at Bolton?

Next, Don Wallace for Trails and Shelters Committee recommended that the Burlington section be authorized to erect tent platforms on the new route of the trail about a half-mile above the present Bolton Lodge, preliminary to the construction of a temporary shelter. Hopefully, beyond even that, a permanent shelter might someday be built that would have fewer vandalism problems than the highly accessible Bolton Lodge. Don observed that all of this would be subject to the approval of the landowner, the Bolton Valley Corporation, which subsequently was obtained.

Don also related that a Trail Acquisition fund had been started with a modest donation of $30 by the Connecticut section. While this is now the entire fund, Don can envision it growing to a substantial amount if the generosity of members in the past is any indication, or legacies become available, etc. In view of the LULU problems described by Jim Wilkinson earlier in the day, such a fund might be a good idea to acquire particularly desirable tracts that might be threatened.

High Finance and Farce

The always sensitive subject of money then caused a discussion that had to be the most bizarre of the day. A floor fight erupted over the disposition of the princely annual surplus of $133.16! Some directors wanted it in the endowment, one questioned whether or not a "surplus" account was even necessary, while others had various suggestions as to its disposition in all manner of worthy causes. Finally some sage director suggested it might actually amount to something if put towards a new truck, and it was added to the truck reserve. One speculates how long the meeting might have lasted if the surplus was $500, or preserve us, $1000!

A Sign on Greylock?

Neil Giroux suggested that the GMC might benefit from the public awareness to be generated by putting a sign of some kind up on top of Mt. Greylock in Massachusetts indicating the location of the LT and the GMC's role in its operation. Neil pointed out that there is a very large volume of car and hiker traffic on Greylock. All present seemed to think that this was an intriguing idea and it will be investigated as to feasibility.

Where Were They?

Next there was one of those episodes that make you wonder if people think about what they do while they are doing it. A couple of directors who voted to authorize the Executive Advisory Council to conduct negotiations on behalf of the Board as regards the Killington business (at the September, 1982 Board meeting) and who are now displeased with the outcome, are wondering why the EAC did what it was authorized to do. It was as if they completely forgot what words were adopted at that meeting. President Wilkinson read the resolution adopted by the Board at that time, which was quite explicit as to what the EAC was empowered to do. Director Neil Giroux correctly observed that negotiations are only feasible when conducted by representatives with the authority to enter into agreements and that the first step in any negotiation is ordinarily the establishment of the various parties' powers, as when diplomats present their credentials.

What is the use, he noted, of anyone negotiating with a negotiator who can not reasonably insure that any agreement entered into will stand up? This whole episode sorely tried the patience of all except those directors who initiated it.

What About Wilderness?

A long standing area of disagreement within the Club was again raised when the Directors were asked to vote on a resolution in support of the "wilderness" designation of certain tracts that were described at the members meeting by Dick Ackerman. Specifically, the resolution was in support of the bill, Senate #897 that would establish these areas.

The discussion ranged around two sections of the proposed resolution. The first referred to "within our own membership ... differing opinions with regard to the designation of certain areas as Wilderness, and the specific boundaries of those areas". The second phrase was the real substance of the resolution, the issue being whether it was strong enough. The debate over the second statement certainly confirmed the accuracy of the first! The resolution part read as follows: "The Green Mountain Club recognizes that designation as Wilderness of those areas identified in S.897 through which the Long Trail passes would be consistent with the Club's objective of preserving the scenic character of the Long Trail and its primitive environment."

Not surprisingly, there was sharp debate and very divided opinions on this controversial subject. Some sharp words were exchanged, with one proponent of a stronger statement characterizing the proposed Club position as "mealymouthed". On the other hand some directors felt we should say nothing at all on a subject we could not agree about ourselves. It was noted that the Killington situation points up the value of the undoubtedly very protective "wilderness" designation and that development will encroach anywhere it is not forcibly kept at bay by such constraints as wilderness status.

Beaver Meadow Lodge Gets a Face/Floor Lift

Beaver Meadow Lodge was the site of extensive reconstruction activity this past spring. Planning for the work was begun more than three years ago when it became obvious the floor was in sad shape, especially around the door and stove, from people splitting wood directly on the floor. Further study showed that the soil bank around the camp had crept downhill, burying three of the sill logs which completely rotted through as a result. Also rotted were the ends of several floor joists.

The Sterling Section, at its January 1982 Annual Meeting, decided to proceed with the necessary repairs, and decided on a plan whereby the replacement logs would be cut and peeled in the spring of 1982 and allowed to season a year before being used. The rough-cut boards for the new floor were also purchased and seasoned during the summer in the Section President's garage. In January 1983, members of the Section packed the almost three-mile trail to the camp so that the boards could be dragged there behind Carroll Lawrence's snow machine. The boards were then hidden under the camp until early June when the actual reconstruction began. Originally scheduled for two weekends, the job was completed in 1½ (rainy!) days.

On a Friday night, Bob Lindemann, Carroll Lawrence, and Smith Edwards (carrying 90-pound packs containing most of the tools, hydraulic jacks, a chainsaw, and a few beers) hiked in to Beaver Meadow to allow for an early start on Saturday. That morning other Section members arrived with the rest of the tools, food, and a few more beers. Ben Davis, Northern Field Assistant, also appeared and was pressed into service as technical expert. The old floor was quickly removed at the cost of two chains, the camp lifted up, and the new sills moved into position. By late afternoon the three sill logs were in place, the floor joists in position, the foundation rocked, and the first layer of floor boards laid. The stove was quickly reinstalled in time for supper. On Sunday, everything was leveled out and the new floor nailed down (except for a small portion where we ran out of boards). Not bad for 1½ days of work!

The job was completed in June when a Morrisville Job Service work crew under GMC supervision carried in the remaining boards, built a bridge across the stream, relocated the outhouse, and worked on the rock "patio" in front of the lodge.

If there was one lesson learned from this reconstruction it was the importance of keeping sill logs high and dry.

Bob Lindemann, Sterling Section President
