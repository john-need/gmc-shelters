GMC "ADOPT A SHELTER PROGRAM"

   The Green Mountain Club and the National Forest
Service maintain about 66 shelters on the Long Trail
System. For over two years the GMC has had an Adopt-
a-Shelter Program designed to try and get more people
involved in shelter care and maintenance. At the present
time 20 of these shelters are "adopted." Since the start
of the program a number of dedicated individuals have
done a fine job helping the GMC to maintain the shelters
and remove many of the can dumps.
   Basically an "adopter" agrees to make periodic inspec-
tion trips, doing routine housekeeping chores, such as
sweeping out the floor; if needed replacing the log book;
keeping the foundation porcupine proof; packing out any
rubbish; and reporting on a regular inspection form any
other repairs that may be necessary, such as windows,
doors, stove repair, etc. This is most important since
regular maintenance time is often wasted finding out what
needs to be done. A copy of the inspection report is
forwarded to the Section Shelter Chairman. The Section
still has the primary responsibility for the shelter. All
shelters should be inspected monthly during the busy sea-
son, ie: May thru October.
   Would you like to "adopt" a shelter in your own Sec-
tion? If you are an "at large" member and would like to
take a more active part in helping the GMC maintain its
shelters, there are many still available. If you are interested
and would like more information on the Adopt-a-Shelter
Program, please contact: Peter C. Palen, Box 163, Hur-
ley, New York, 12443.

NEW GMC SLIDE PROGRAM AVAILABLE

   One of the major problems of the Green Mountain Club
is how to handle the increasing number of youth and
other groups hiking the Long Trail. Often these groups
are poorly equipped, poorly trained, and have little knowl-
edge of the practices that are so important if the Long
Trail is to be continued as a footpath in the wilderness.
   The new slide program, "Backpacking On The Long
Trail," is aimed specifically at groups that plan on using
the LT. Prepared by experienced Scoutmaster Ronald
Dean of Southboro, Mass. the new program is an excel-
lent training tool for Scout troops, school groups, summer
camps and other organizations that are planning trips
during the coming summer.
   Subjects covered in the program include clothing and
equipment needed, food selection and packaging, schedul-
ing, hiking tips, what to look for on the trail, Green
Mountain hiker etiquette, use of portable stoves, fire-
building, use of shelters, carry in-carry out a little
ecology, and much on the general appreciation of the
LT and what it provides.
   The program has 89 slides and is accompanied by a
taped narration recorded on a cassette tape for easy
handling. The slides come in a carousel reel all ready
to be shown. Cost for rental of the program is one dollar.
   Arrangements for a showing can be made from the
following locations: Green Mountain Club, P.O. Box 94,
Rutland, Vt. 05701; Richard Church, 66 Elmwood St.,
Millbury, Mass. 01527.

After a great deal of work with the Environmental Commissions throughout the state, we were pleased with our input in the original Land Capability plan as presented to the Governor in November and sent to every resident in the state. The following is the portion of the plan which directly concerns the GMC:

3. Recreational Trails—

The Long Trail and the Vermont portion of the Appalachian Trail are historic recreational resources that continue to provide high quality recreational and educational experience for Vermonters and visitors alike. These trails, together with the associated access and side trails, are valuable because of the hiking experience they afford through largely undeveloped terrain, much of it along the ridges of the Green Mountains. The essential wilderness or wilderness-like qualities of these trail systems have in recent years been jeopardized or even destroyed altogether by development activity.

It is the policy of the State of Vermont to encourage uses of land containing important trail corridors that will most contribute to the essential scenic and wilderness-like qualities of these corridors.

(a) It is the policy of the State of Vermont to encourage the Green Mountain Club, recognized as the founder, sponsor, defender, and protector of the Long Trail System, to continue to develop satisfactory understandings and arrangements with landowners to insure the continued value of the trail system.

It is recognized by the State of Vermont that the Long Trail and Appalachian Trail Systems are increasingly threatened by damage through overuse. Further it is recognized that there exist other foot trails, less well known outside Vermont, that provide recreational experience similar to the Long Trail itself.

(b) It is the policy of the State of Vermont to encourage and help assure the protection of hiking trails of high recreational value and popularity or potential popularity so that the expanding need for places for hiking in scenic or wilderness-like settings may be met without resorting to manipulation or alteration of established trails or existing physical conditions.

However, after much editing and amending by the legislature, it appears that no plan will be approved this year. As the Land Capability Plan was being revised, it was recommended by one of the legislators that a new resolution be introduced to the legislature which would read somewhat as follows:

Suggested Joint Senate Resolution

Whereas the Senate and House of Representatives in their resolution of 1971 recognized the Green Mountain Club as the Founder, sponsor, defender, and protector of the Long Trail system for now and future generations.

Whereas there is a need to support the Green Mountain Club in its action to protect the Long Trail System.

It is hereby resolved:

1. That the GMC coordinate its effort with those of the state and Federal offices covering sections of the Trail traversing state and federal lands.

2. That the GMC take action through voluntary agreements to protect the Trail corridor on private lands.

3. That the GMC work closely with the State Regional and local planning commissions on protection of the Trail through Vermont environmental laws.

There was not sufficient time to clear this important obligation with the club membership, therefore it was decided that we should wait until the next year when we hope we would have the club's full support for such action.

Do we want the responsibility of seeking voluntary agreements with the 75 private landowners? Should we seek protection of the Trail above 2500' under Act 250? Do we visit the environmental commissions and local town planning groups and seek protection for the Trail from this source? The handling of the Trail on state and federal land is another issue for consideration.

In a recent two day meeting with State and Federal Officials, we were asked if we would like to assume all responsibility for the Long Trail on Government land. Apparently reduced budgets make this a logical move.

Are we ready to plan for maintenance, reconstruction, control and education on the Trail to meet State and Federal Standards. Do we have the staff, and, of course, can we finance this project?

The entire length of the Appalachian Trail can be funded without federal assistance other than that of the Land and Water Conservation Fund, except Vermont. An exchange of correspondence between Governor Salmon and Mr. Gray, Chairman of the Appalachian National Scenic Trail Committee, raises the following interesting questions concerning the protection of the 39.2 miles of trail on private land in Vermont:

"Will the proposed Vermont Land Control Bill offer sufficient protection—this year—to the 39.2 miles of unprotected trail on private lands by its restrictions? Will the local trail club assist state personnel in obtaining agreements? In the event funds must be sought, has the actual value been determined? How likely is it that Congress would provide the funds for Vermont, especially when 13 other states are protecting the Appalachian Trail in their state without federal aid?"

The Green Mountain Club has been the symbol of hiking in Vermont for the past 59 years. With the growing number of people on the Trail, we must ask ourselves if we are carrying out our responsibility to the hiking public in communications, guide book, shelters education, so that the hikers can have a good experience and yet not destroy our natural resources.

More and more pressure is being placed on the club to cooperate with other groups. We are also being asked for our position on many issues from snow mobile regulation, to the Bottle Ban and Senator Aiken's Wild Area Bill.

Are we ready to discuss major issues and reach a decision as to the club's position? Do we want the Vermont Natural Resources Council or the Sierra Club speaking for us? Do we cooperate with the AMC, ATC, Ski Touring Association, and others in their programs?

Last but not least is the financial position of the Club. If we decide to do more things we will need more money. We should have a tax exempt status reasonably soon, to enhance the club's ability to raise money thru gifts and special programs and provide the necessary funds to carry out well organized plans.

I believe we are capable of preparing, financing, and more important carrying out management plans for the Long Trail System to cover protection of the land, maintenance of the Trail, control of the people, and provide the hiking public with a good experience without changing materially the unspoiled, unsophisticated and unrestricted traditions of the Green Mountain Club.

BRATTLEBORO SECTION

About 30 people attended the Annual Meeting of the Brattleboro Section, held March 23, 1973 in the Meeting Room at the Brattleboro Library.

Frank Dearborn, president, presided at the short business meeting where it was voted to raise the dues and not to become involved in the "Adopt a Shelter Program" at the present time. A member who had inspected Swezey Camp reported that someone had maliciously removed the stove from the cabin and broken it up by heaving rocks on it.

The following officers were elected: President—Frank Dearborn; Vice President—Russell Southworth; Secretary—Louise Dunklee; Treasurer—Roger Davis; Trails and Shelters Chairman—Brandon Douglas; Program Chairman—Robert Southworth.

Slides were shown of the trail from the parent club collection and the Dearborn collection. Sugar-on-snow, home made doughnuts, pickles and coffee were served by Louise Dunklee and committee.

BURLINGTON SECTION NEWS

The Burlington Section Annual Meeting featured an extremely interesting slide program, "Journey of a Lifetime," narrated by Everett Skinner. Mr. and Mrs. Skinner described their seven month hike of the Appalachian Trail. The meeting, which was attended by 145 people, was arranged by Hollie Johnson and held at the Old Lantern in Charlotte. A highlight during the social hour prior to the dinner was an automatic slide show, compiled by Betty Myers and Madeline Miles, which displayed slides of GMCers trips to the Grand Canyon, Greece and the End-to-End journey of the Spears and Pirkanens. New officers elected are: President—Kenn Boyd, Vice-President—Madeline Miles, Secretary—Coralie Magoon, Treasurer—John Findholt, Trails Committee Chairmen—John Harrington, Frank Mack, Bill Tennent, Properties—Howard van Benthuysen, Recreation—Hollie Johnson, Membership—Charlene Whitcomb, Publicity—Jan Lange, Outings—Pirk and Dot Pirkanen, and Trustees—Marion Holmes, Harris Abbott, Joe Frank.

Members of the Burlington Section were saddened by the death of Anne Clark, former membership chairman. In her memory, members have established a memorial and have made individual contributions to the Green Mountain Audubon Society's Nature Center, "so that those things which she appreciated most can be shared by all."

KILLINGTON SECTION

We added a "first" to our Hike Schedule this year when Dave Govatski suggested ski touring on March 3 from Mt. Tabor to Little Rock Pond. The snow cover was thin, but participants were enthusiastic about scheduling more ski tours another year.

The Section annual meeting, held Jan. 21, brought out more people than in the memory of anyone present. The 1973 Intersectional will be hosted by our Section, under the direction of Wilbur Weston, and held at Tamarack Notch—Aitken Forest Camp, in Mendon, from Aug. 25 to Sept. 3.

                        MIGRATION

Upon a jutting crag with mosses fringed,
Where stunted trees from many gales have cringed,
Day dreaming in the sun, we idly gaze
At distant mountains almost lost in haze.
Then in the air there swells a weird, wild song,
Like muted trumpets or a clamoring throng.
With awe and wonder our eyes search the sky,
And high above, in vast vees winging by,
Great northland geese like arrows pierce through space.
Long necks outstretch, wings beat in rhythmic grace,
As by sky paths that give no guiding clue,
They fly beyond, to their far rendezvous.
                                         MARIAN WEST

Dear Editor:

    I am writing to comment on the letter by Stephen Moore in the Long Trail News, February 1973. I fully share Moore's concern of the basic incompatibility of mines and mineral exploration with wilderness areas, and of the slow pace whereby areas are being added to the National Wilderness System, especially seeing that less than two years remain before the 10-year limit for the classification of land under the Wilderness Act of 1964 will be reached. In the latter regard, we should all write to our Senators and Congressmen and urge them to work harder to get areas under the Act; we should also write to the heads of various Federal bureaus to submit candidate areas to Congress without further delay.
   I am concerned about Moore's repeated use of the expression "virgin lands" as if that is a requirement for protection under the Wilderness Act. This is not so. I quote from the Act: a wilderness is "an area where the earth and its community of life are untrammeled by man, where man himself is a visitor . . ." an area "of undeveloped Federal land retaining its primeval character and influence, without permanent improvements or human habitation . . . generally [appearing] to have been affected primarily by the force of nature, with the imprint of man's work substantially unnoticeable." The concept of purity is not intrinsic to a wilderness area as here defined. The concept of purity is being seized upon, for instance by the U.S. Forest Service to deny eastern United States (anywhere east of the Mississippi) of ANY additional wilderness area within the National Forests (the so-called Wild Areas East Act and its equivalent now in the 93rd Congress, being cosponsored by our own Senator Aiken yet!). The Forest Service's position is being vigorously contested by three bills in Congress: S. 316 of Senators Jackson (Washington) and Buckley (New York), and H.R. 1758 (Haley of Florida) and H.R. 2420 (Saylor of Pennsylvania), where it is pointed out that Congress, not the Forest Service, has the authority to determine what areas qualify for wilderness protection.
   I write at length because this misconception seems very prevalent. It is also a double-edged sword now being used against wilderness protection. It would not take much to extend the doctrine of purity, as formulated by the Forest Service, to say much of the areas in western United States do not qualify for wilderness designation either. So all of us should oppose the Wild Areas East bill because it undermines the entire basis for wilderness protection under the Act of 1964, on a national scale.

                     Sincerely yours,

                                             E-AN ZEN

BACKPACKER MAGAZINE

   Backpacker is a new quarterly publication for backpackers which is edited, written and photographed by backpackers. In addition to articles on backpacking itself—the how, who, where, when, and why of it—Backpacker will carry a wide variety of fascinating features on equipment evaluation; nature photography; bird watching; rock climbing; mountaineering; fern, flower, tree, track and moss study; painting and sketching; and mushrooming.
   According to the advertisement, Backpacker will be offered to known backpackers only and is not intended to encourage more people to go out on the trails. Their mission is to discuss ways that the backpackers who love the wilderness, don't love it to death. Some back country or wilderness areas have been so seriously threatened that sections have already been closed or are being rationed. Backpackers themselves are endangering the delicate ecological balance in areas where just too many are going. Litter and waste disposal is another big problem. (We GMCers are well aware of these problems on our own Long Trail.)
   If Backpacker can succeed in its mission to instil a new back country ethic, it will be well worth our support. Not available at newstands or bookstores, Backpacker will be offered at select equipment stores at $2.50 for a single issue. The charter subscription rates are $6.00 for 1 year; $10.00 for 2 years; and $12.00 for 3 years. Add $1.50 to charter price if you do not send money with your order but request billing after the first issue (March 1973).
