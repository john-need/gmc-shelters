PRESIDENT'S MESSAGE

G.M.C. Members:
   Mark Whalon, (another rural male carrier) states in his book "They say the best thing about a fellow who takes a cold bath every morning before breakfast is that he don't do it." It's 30 below as I write this to you all, and that's why I'm not hiking.
   But I was reading the Merit Badge HIKING requirements yesterday and found these statements, in the Boy Scout Manual.
   1. Take five hikes of ten continuous miles each on five separate days.
   2. After sufficient training, take one hike of twenty continuous miles in one day.
   3. Within a month of the final hike submit a short report of each of the six hikes containing dates, routes traveled, weather conditions and interesting things observed.
   There are more tests, but I have been wondering what a wonderful chance for these scouts to make hiking Merit Badges on our Long Trail. I wonder if we could let the Scouts know they might happily use our Trail and maybe some of us could read their written reports.
   I wonder too how many Scouts know about our "End-to-Enders" and what fun they all have had doing the trip from Canada to Massachusetts. Seems as if maybe we could help the Scouts out.

LEONE E. SMITH, President

NOVEMBER TRUSTEES MEETING

   At a Board of Trustees Meeting held November 3rd in Rutland, Vermont the following trustees were present: President Leone Smith, Messrs. Kent, Goddard, Talbot, Koerber, Paulson, Gormly, Vondell, Mitchell, Perkins; Miss Goeres, Miss Hinchey and Mrs. Lodge. Also present were Prof. Roy Buchanan, Chairman of Trails and Shelters, and Mrs. Mary Perkins, Editor of the LONG TRAIL NEWS.
   Here are some of the highlights of business acted upon and reports and suggestions made:

Up to November 3rd of last year, 1060 Guide Books had been sold.
   Total Members-at-large number 221 plus 52 life members. New members this year (1956) total 29.
   It was suggested that we post a topographic map of the territory around each shelter inside each shelter to encourage more use of the trails. Signs are needed for camps and shelters, telling the Club's name, membership dues, and the fact that the Guide Book is available at $1.00. Ted Talbot was appointed to work on this suggestion and have some facts and figures ready to present at the April Trustees meeting.
   The President read Eugene Bamforth's letter of resignation from the Board. It was moved and passed that this resignation be accepted with regret.
   Prof. Buchanan reported for Trails and Shelters Committee that the net cost for the summer's work was about $1670. A detailed report is found elsewhere in this issue.
   Mr. Paulson reported that new roofs had been put on Thendara and Fay Fuller Shelters.
   There was a discussion about determining responsibility for specific parts of the Trail and whether Sections can and will do the work. Mr. Talbot felt that a definite fixing should be done and that we should work with the Green Mountain National Forest to determine what they are responsible for and what the Club is responsible for.
   Mr. Koerber made a motion that the Board of Trustees, at the spring meeting, review the present assignments to determine whether all Sections take care of their particular assignments, and if the Sections are unable to, the trustees will make a reassignment. This motion was passed.
   Miss Rena Gusmai of Barre, Vermont was elected a trustee to fill the vacant place of Eugene Bamforth.
   It was voted that the place for the 1957 Intersectional would be in the Camel's Hump region.

TRAILS AND SHELTERS COMMITTEE
REPORTS WORK OF LONG TRAIL PATROL

The Patrol started work on June 11 with two full time workers and a third one for the last five weeks. They worked on Killington Section's trails for one week and on Burlington Section's trails for two and one-half weeks with payment from those Sections. This eased the Patrol's budget by about $400. and got the more popular trails opened early. Then beginning July 4th the Trail was cleared from Smuggler's Notch to Canada. Camps in this region were repaired as needed.

TRAILS—Cleared of blowdowns all the way and bad places debushed. Rerouted near Ritterbush on account of beaver work. Very bad bushes were cut from Beaver Meadow to Chilcoat Pass and from Beaver Meadow to Whiteface Shelter. There were lumber jobs at Prospect Rock, Parker Camp, and Belvidere Basin.

CAMPS—Floor and ground frame at Hazen's Notch Camp repaired; roof and window at Beaver Meadow; stove pipes and work on stoves at Barrows and French Camps; creosote for nearly all camps north of Johnson.

LAURA WOODWARD CAMP—This camp was rebuilt on the same site. A lumbering road gave access on the east side. This reduced the cost somewhat. It is an Adirondack type shelter, built of logs, with metal roofing protected with a coat of tar. The floor is of boards back packed in from the above mentioned lumber road. The cost of the camp is $386.14 complete with one wall of mountain air on the Jay Peak side.

APPALACHIAN TRAIL—Cleared from Gifford Woods to the Barnard Gulf road at Prosper. Relocation of trail across the marsh east of Gifford Woods where beaver had flooded the trail.

We had the use of Ted Goddard's truck once more which saved the Patrol much money.

Our liability insurance cost $75.71.

Next year we shall have to relocate Jay Camp for the new road to connect Montgomery Center to the Jay Peak ski area goes past the camp. Whiteface Shelter should be rebuilt. It offers shelter and nothing else unless one considers the shelter for porcupines that it affords. Possibly Glastonbury should be rebuilt or else repaired. And of course Mother Nature is doing her usual winter's work.

ROY BUCHANAN,
Chairman of Trails and Shelters

NEW ENGLAND TRAIL CONFERENCE

The date for the Annual Meeting of the New England Trail Conference is April 6th, 1957. This is the old Annual Recreation Conference with the date pushed up into April so that weather conditions may be better than what was endured last year in March by those attending.

The place of the New England Trail Conference meeting is to be Skinner Hall, University of Massachusetts, Amherst, Mass. Green Mountain Club members are cordially invited to attend. For further information write to Prof. John Vondell, University of Massachusetts, Amherst, Mass.

We quote from Carl O. Chauncey's Message to Constituent Organizations in the May issue of New England Trails: "The New England Trail Conference is not only a clearing house for trail information for constituent organizations. It also performs an important public service on your behalf, that of disseminating information about trails to the people who use or might like to use the trails which you so laboriously maintain. Your reports to the New England Trail Conference reach not only the persons who attend the annual meeting, but also the many readers of "New England Trails," "Appalachia," published by the Appalachian Mountain Club, and "Appalachian Trailway News" published by the Appalachian Trail Conference, all of which carry accounts of the annual meeting of the New England Trail Conference.

"More than that, your reports are a source of valuable data for the secretary of the New England Trail Conference who, all year long, receives and answers many requests for trail information from people in various parts of the United States and sometimes in foreign countries.

"If you can't send a representative to the annual meeting to deliver your report in person, PLEASE, send in a written report. You go to a lot of hard work to maintain your trails. Hikers want to know what you have done."

Let's hope The Green Mountain Club will have a good representation and deliver a splendid report at the April 6th New England Trail Conference.

NOTES FROM THE NEW YORK SECTION

With the holiday season pleasantly tucked away in memory, let us roll up the curtain on the New Year and see what New York Section members are planning for the coming weeks.

Winter sports figure prominently, for we find a ski trip to Southern Vermont, a snowshoe trip on the Long Trail and two ice skating parties mentioned.

Camp Thendara will be open for members and guests on five weekends between now and the end of March.

The Sunday hikes for the same period number nine and run from easy to difficult, and from straight hiking to exploring old mines or going on a guided tour of Harvard Black Rock Forest.

The Saturday outing fans have the skating mentioned above, plus a trip to view medieval art at the Cloisters on one Saturday, and a walk along Brooklyn's interesting waterfront on another Saturday.

And last, but by no means least, on February 1st our always popular dinner-dance will be held at Hotel Shelburne in New York.

That about winds up our activities until the end of March.

LILLIAN P. LODGE

UP THE TRAIL A PIECE--
BURLINGTON SECTION

Although Outing Chairman, Dave Otis, has steered through a schedule of twice a month outings for the Burlington Section we'd like to highlight a few for the readers of the LONG TRAIL NEWS.

The trek to Spruce Mountain, southeast of Plainfield brought out 20. Because of its advantageous position the little mountain offers an uninterrupted view of the major peaks of the Green Mts. to the westward and a broad view of the crowning peaks of the White Mts. We class it as an "A-1" trip and recommend it to other hikers. The trip was made in the Color Season and because the location of the peak is the center of a hardwood belt the area was a painted picture to behold.

The Halloween Party with its family picnic and spirited square dance at the Colchester Grange Hall brought out a good attendance.

The climb of "Potater Hill" (generally known as Mt. Abraham) was climaxed by a "House Warming" for the Otis family. About 40 invaded the Otis wigwam and gave their new little house a real work-out, and ate the cupboard bare.

Then on Nov. 28th. the Montpelier Section invited the Burlington Section to "eat on them" at Willey lodge (in the New York Section of the Trail). Yum! Yum! Was that beef stew ever good.--Bet they didn't realize that the Burlington Section had at least two BIG eaters and maybe they'll think twice before they repeat such a dinner date to the lake shore crowd.

On Dec. 6. the schedule pointed to Sterling Pond lodge. Even though the weather was a bit rugged 14 hauled out their snowshoes and made their way up into the summit of Smuggler's Notch road and up the Long Trail. The ice over the pond offered an easy short cut to the lodge.

Although the connection of the Spruce Peak and the new Sterling Mt. ski developments are close at hand, at the moment, it is hoped that the skiers will not make their way to the lodge and missuse it. There are no ski trails approaching the structure even though the trails are only approximately a quarter mile's distance away.

The Burlington Sectioners observed their now traditional New Year's trip to the Bolton area again this year. Nine of the most adventuresome men and women spent New Year's Eve and night at Bolton lodge. Two others came up to watch the New Year in and then returned home. On New Year's Day five others came snowshoeing up to the lodge in the zero chilled weather to join the group of overnighters.

By the time you have read this the Burlington Section followers will have had their Annual Meeting and will have kicked out or re-elected a slate of officers for the ensuing year.

All eyes, after the election up this way, will be focused on the annual Oyster Stew supper at the James P. Taylor Lodge, in Nebraska Notch on the eve of Jan. 27th. Man! Can that young Remick couple mix up quite a stew with oysters and beaver-pond water-.

LARRY DEAN

SPECIALTIES IN THE NEWS
The "Big Surprise"

Thirty-two Burlington Section members gathered on the "spur of the moment," gathered up some special kind of vitals and pushed into the Buchanan homestead on the occasion of Prof. Roy O. Buchanan's birthday on Dec. 15th. It was hoped it would be a complete surprise but the honored person got the scent of the movement before the party broke.

How old is Roy? Ask him yourself--this we can tell you: He's young enough to walk many of you off your feet on any trail you wish to challenge him on.

The group "hashed over trail ventures," heard Roy read a story or two from the writings of the late Rowland E. Robinson and then helped him take care of a bunch of cake and ice-cream.

Just to have spent an hour or two with this Champion among Long Trail hikers and genial philosopher of the outdoors can only be an uplift to those who shared the occasion.

Two members of the Mountain Club of Maryland swung in on the Burlington Section's last trip into the challenging Adirondacks. They reported having a grand time with the group and are looking forward to another such a trek.

L. D.

WORCESTER SECTION REPORTING

At a special meeting called in regard to purchasing property on Cedar Lake in Leicester, Massachusetts, for a new Camp, it was decided to purchase same providing the finances could be raised. A gift of $100. was turned over to the Treasurer after the meeting, so we are on our way.

On October 21st and 28th a group of workers went in to the Harold Daniels School of Forestry in Rutland, Massachusetts where they helped build new cabins for the school.

The Annual Dinner and election of officers took place October 25th at the White Cliffs in Northboro. The new officers elected are: Robert W. Humes, president; Forrest H. Thomson, vice president; Charles E. Stewart, executive committee member-at-large; Jessie Phillimore, secretary; Phyllis P. Ojerholm, treasurer.

A joint hike with the Appalachian Club over A.M.C. trails, and a hike around the West Boylston Reservoir were trips enjoyed in November. A Military Whist Party took place on November 28th to raise funds for the new camp.

The Christmas Party at Salter Secretarial School was well attended, and another hike on A.M.C. trails with supper at the Appalachian Club Lodge was enjoyed in December. We are very grateful to the "Appies" for their generosity in the use of their Camp.

So we close another year, and after two years of reporting for the Worcester Section, this reporter's job is done. The new reporter, Mrs. Forrest H. Thomson, takes over. May she enjoy it as much as I have. Happy New Year to all.

VICTORIA M. McNIGLE

NEWS FROM BRATTLEBORO

The Brattleboro Section of the Green Mountain Club has not had a scheduled program through November or December. However, our president, Don Kent, went into Old Swezey Camp by jeep on Thanksgiving day and put three rolls of tar paper on three sides of the cabin, and weather-proofed the cabin further by covering the windows with plastic window material.

On December 30th, Don took three of our junior members—Keith Harrington, Dick Payne, and Lynn Kent into Bromley Camp. They hiked without snowshoes or skis but felt they needed one or the other for they found more snow in the mountains than in Newfane. Breaking trail was quite a chore. They found the cabin in good shape. The wood supply was low and they left more than they found, so some cold traveller should benefit. A member of the Norwich University Ski team stopped in while they were making coffee.

Brattleboro is having a big celebration on Wednesday, January 16th when they receive the All-American City award. Our Section plans to have a Green Mountain Club float in the parade.

Our annual meeting will be held January 21st at the office of the Holden and Martin Lumber Company. The program will include talks with emphasis put on hiking equipment and foods of minimum weight which are good for carrying on long treks.

LOUISE H. DUNKLEE

SUSPENSION BRIDGE
AT CLARENDON GORGE
by
EMILE AND CECILE BOSELLI

It seems a while ago we promised the editor of LONG TRAIL NEWS an article on how we built the Clarendon Gorge Bridge. Well, I guess we'll just have to sit ourselves down and write it now. I don't know how we ever got started on this project. The old bridge across the Gorge had reached such a stage of decay that it was necessary to destroy it several years ago to prevent anyone being injured on it. We, of the Killington Section had not done anything about replacing this bridge; perhaps we were just trying to avoid a difficult job. Craigue Perkins did attempt several times to put small log bridges down in the Gorge itself but these never lasted more than a few days as the water in the Gorge rises after any rainstorm. The river narrows going through the Gorge and personal observations have shown that the water will rise as much as ten feet in spring run-off. However, it was getting more and more difficult to ignore the issue. Hikers on the Trail were finding it a great inconvenience and were writing to tell us so. Truthfully, we ourselves were finding it difficult also.

I guess Cecile and I figured the only way to solve the problem was to take the matter into our own hands. We went down and examined the old bridge site carefully. The piers from the old bridge were still good and sound. We certainly could not build a bridge of the type that had been there before as this was a timber carriage bridge and its construction would be much too heavy for our amateur labor; besides all we needed was a foot bridge. So we got the drafting board and pencil to work, and designed ourselves a steel suspension bridge. Now we had a plan but didn't know if GMC was interested in such an idea.

On August 8, 1955 a special meeting of the Killington Section Executive Board was held at the home of Craigue Perkins to examine our plans of the proposed project. The Killington Section was definitely in favor.

Work was started almost immediately. I hired some outside help in putting in the main anchors. We started out very optimistically but our attitude soon changed. Before we had dug one foot we hit rock. It seems the builders of the old bridge had backfilled the road bed with boulders and we had to go down six feet through this. Fortunately, Bill Whitman was a stubborn enough fellow to just grin and say, "Let's go!" Those were some of the most miserable weekends I spent. While the temperature soared to 103 we swung the sledge all day making small boulders out of big ones. When we were ready to install our first set of main anchors we had a trench in the rock six feet deep, six feet wide, and twelve feet long. The anchor rods were eight feet long and terminated in a pair of specially treated railroad ties. These were installed at approximately a 45° angle. When the hole was backfilled only two steel eyes showed at the surface of the ground. These were the anchors for one end of our bridge. Installing the anchors for the other end of the bridge proved to be just as miserable if not more so because there we hit portions of ledge along with the boulders. Fortunately, I hired Bill on the "job basis" for helping to put in the anchors. If he had been hired by the hour, GMC would probably have gone bankrupt.

Then winter set in. We tried to figure out just how we were going to construct this bridge then we shopped for our materials. We needed so many wire rope clips that we cleaned out Montgomery Ward's Mail Order Supply House in Albany.

Getting our quantities of materials proved to be

difficult. Figuring out how to build this bridge was even worse. After much thought, we decided building the bridge entirely in place seemed to have its difficulties. We thought of building the entire bridge on dry land and then moving it into place but some more head scratching showed us this was not too practical. It was finally decided to pre-fabricate the bridge and then assemble the individual parts at the bridge site. This seemed a good idea since we had the whole winter in which to make up our parts. This called for some careful measuring and calculating but this is the kind of things engineers are made of.

The towers for the bridge were ten feet high. They were made from four inch galvanized steel eye beams donated by C.V.P.S. Corp. All joints were welded making each tower a solid piece weighing approximately 250 lbs. The six main cables were to be 7/16" special high strength 7 strand galvanized guy wire. Our vertical drop cables were 3/8" galvanized guy wire. It may be of interest to note that most of the hardware on the bridge is galvanized. This was done in the interest of longer life and ease of future maintenance. The floor cross timbers are 4 x 4 Hemlock and the floor itself is composed of 2" Hemlock planks running lengthwise to the bridge.

Starting work at the bridge was greatly delayed in the spring due to the height and turbulence of the water going through the Gorge. This made it impossible to cross to the other side. When the water did subside sufficiently to get across I was able to get the help of Ray Catozzi to assist me in building the forms for the cement footings of my towers. During this period of the work "Cele" kept a diary of our daily progress. I will take the liberty of calling upon it here:

June 22nd—With the appreciated help of Ray Catozzi the forms for the tower footings were put together on the far side of the Gorge.

June 23rd—Eight bags of cement were picked up at the Clarendon Building Supply and materials were collected from various sources. All this material was transported to the bridge site in a wheelbarrow via the Long Trail.

June 24th—A tramway was put in order to haul cement and all necessary equipment across Mill River Gorge. Water and stone for the cement was hauled up by bucket from the river below. Then the mixing and pouring of cement began. One half of the form on the other side was completed by Sunday evening.

June 25th—The concrete had been wet down. Two planks were installed and anchored in order to get across the stream down below. (P.S. They didn't last. They were victims of the stream's mad waters).

June 26th—More concrete was pulled across, more pails filled with water or stones and then began the pouring of the second half of the form. During the evening a group of young fellows came through from Camp Plymouth. Their first remark was, "What did you do with the bridge?" (The new Guide Book had optimistically stated that our bridge was already in service—Gene works faster than I do.)

June 28th—The form was taken away from the concrete and measurements were taken with Ray's assistance. It was discovered that the pier on the near side would have to be built up two feet to make it level with the pier on the opposite side (the old timers goofed when they made this one.)

June 29th—Lumber was picked up on the way down to the Gorge and lunch was eaten at the site after which Emile and Ray put the form together for levelling this side. Later in the evening two visitors from Brattleboro Section strolled down to look things over. Work was finished by dark.

June 30th, Sat.—In the morning Emile and Ray picked up twenty bags of cement and brought them down to the site. They couldn't be left there as no one was at the neighboring house so they returned with them again in the afternoon. Then Ray left and the concrete was brought down to the Gorge two bags at a time. The bridge pier on this side had to be built up twenty inches plus six inches for the block the tower sat on. Lunch was eaten and then more concrete was put into the form. There were about eight fishermen and visitors during the course of the day. Part of the Trail was cleared in the afternoon, also. In all, there were twelve bags of concrete poured today. (Mixed by hand.)

July 1st, Sun.—Three bags of cement were wheeled down from the house and cement was poured. Then lunch was eaten and two more bags of cement were poured.

July 2nd—We came down a little later tonight and took more measurements to get the right height for the tower footing blocks on this side. A bonfire was started to burn up the twelve empty cement bags left over from yesterday.

July 3rd—The wooden forms were ripped off the cement footings on the far side of the Gorge.

July 4th, Wed.—We came down around 11:00 AM and poured the remainder of the concrete—six bags. Then we had dinner after which we finished pouring and then covered the forms.

July 31st—One side of the second tower was painted and then a few of the drop cables were made up.

Aug. 1st—About ten more drop cables were made up tonight. There are forty-six to be made. They have to be pre-fabricated in the cellar at home.

Aug. 2nd—After going down to the bridge site for a last minute check we finished painting the second tower, then set to work again making drop cables.

Aug. 3rd—Tonight also was set aside for drop cables.

Aug. 4th, Sat.—Most of today was used up for hunting up more rope clips and getting roofing tar for the tops of the tower footings.

Aug. 5th, Sun.—Bill Whitman, Percy Davis, Jerry LaMoria, and Clayton Kelley, all members of the Blue Ridge Bowmen Archery Club, helped Emile take the towers from the shop to the bridge site in Will Shackett's pick-up truck. The boys had quite a struggle getting one of the towers over to the other side of the Gorge.
