LONG TRAIL PATROL

Except for the activity of Vermont's winter, there has been very little doing on the Long Trail since our last report. Of course the vandals are keeping up their good work. They have developed a new idea recently. They now cut off the projecting logs at the corners of log cabins to use for fire wood. This leaves the cabin sort of half naked, which must please the vandals. However, the Patrol built many new out-houses last fall and so we hope that the vandals will put more time on their outhouse destruction project than they do on the cabin-corner logs.

The Patrol will do the usual trail and shelter work from Ithiel Falls to Canada, with two-thirds of that trail speed cleared and one-third of it standardized. The standardized part will be from Hazens Notch to Canada since that work was done from Parker Camp to Hazens Notch in 1964 and from Ithiel Falls to Parker Camp in 1965.

No doubt there will be trail problems at Jay Peak where an $8,000,000 ski area development is to go in during the next three years. Also, the Bolton area is to have $5,000,000 spent on its year-round recreation project. And a new lift is planned to go through or very near Taft Lodge. We are waiting to see what will have to be done as the skiers take over.

It seems possible that Noyes Pond Camp may have to be moved, for it is pretty near a new summer home.

The distance between Wiley Lodge and Bolton Lodge, over the trail relocation, is too great, so there should be a new camp located somewhere near Duck Brook. This location may be influenced by what happens in the Bolton valley.

ROY BUCHANAN

PLANNING NEEDED TO PRESERVE WILDERNESS AREAS

At the present time it is not anticipated that any action will be taken, on either the national or state level, to implement the plan for a Vermont Scenic Parkway along the Green Mountains before 1967, perhaps later. The Committee has been keeping in as close contact as possible with officials. It has drawn up recommendations for routes which could be used as alternates to the proposed route should these be requested and needed.

Right now it seems most important, and perhaps more urgent, that efforts be focused on planning carefully to preserve some of our fast disappearing wilderness areas for future generations to enjoy. Vermont has had an abundance of wilderness. However, the situation is changing rapidly and these areas can no longer be taken for granted. More and more of the state and national forest lands are being and will be developed for camping, picnicking and skiing. Lumber company holdings are being developed for recreation, as evidenced most recently by plans for the whole Bolton Valley, and the north basin and summit of Jay Peak. Private lands are being sold for developments and posted against public use.

Wilderness is a very vital asset to Vermont as a place to live and a place to visit. It plays a very important role in outdoor recreation in the state. To be sure it is difficult to evaluate its value in dollars and actual usage, but it, like many other important things in life, is priceless. Without it, would Vermont still be Vermont to most people?

Until recently the wilderness as an aspect of outdoor recreation has been given little recognition in long-range state planning. However, the committee feels that it should be an integral part of the planning for Vermont, and it has been in contact with state planners regarding this.

Vermont's wilderness, which might more appropriately be called wildlands, would include some natural areas and other undeveloped areas set aside under good timber management. It is encouraging to learn that the very summit of Camel's Hump has been designated as a natural area—this is a step in the right direction!

As GMC'ers, you can:
1. Let other people, including state and local officials, know the real value and need for undeveloped wildlands, and the role they play in recreation.
2. Send the committee ideas of areas in the Green Mountains and along the Trail which you feel should definitely be preserved.
3. Sign trail registers and encourage others to do so. Statistics on trail use are important in our planning.
4. Forward to the committee any information on activities which might be helpful in compiling utilization data of the mountains and the Trail.
5. If you have specific suggestions, forward them to the Central Planning Office in Montpelier, or to the U.S. Forest Service in Rutland. In their planning, these agencies are open to suggestions and ideas.

For years the Green Mountain Club has offered Vermonters and visitors alike the opportunity of enjoying mountain wilderness via the Long Trail. It has been a real recreational asset to the state. Let's plan so that it will remain as much a "Footpath in the Wilderness" as possible.

SHIRLEY J. STRONG, Chairman

MOUNT GREYLOCK CAN BE SAVED

We reported to you in an earlier News that Mount Greylock, the highest mountain in Massachusetts, is scheduled to have a massive ski and summer resort development plastered on its east face. The grandiose published plans include an aerial tramway, several chair lifts, many wide ski trails, a shopping center, summit restaurant, lodges, etc. We think this is of concern to you because the southern approach trail to the Long Trail traverses the summit ridge of Mount Greylock less than 10 miles south of the Long Trail. This trail is also known as the Appalachian Trail.

My message to you this time is one of cautious but optimistic hope. We have a very good chance of saving Greylock just like we saved Mt. Mansfield a few years ago, if we all pull together. Our hopes are up for several reasons: the Mount Greylock Protective Association's suit against the Tramway Authority was upheld in the Massachusetts Supreme Court; the hearing before the Committee on State Administration in Boston was decisively in opposition to the Tramway; the Committee has acted promptly and released the bills to the floor of the Legislature for debate; and the City Council and Mayor of Pittsfield (largest city in The Berkshire Hills) and the Association of Business and Commerce of Central Berkshire County have gone on record as being opposed to the Tramway.

However, in spite of these optimistic signs, the battle is not yet won, for the Tramway Authority is still a legally constituted body and the Mount Greylock Commission has not publicly denounced its pro-tramway leaning. Therefore, with the opposition now very definitely on the defensive, the time for concerted action on our part is now.

Here is how you can help if you want to save Mount Greylock. If letter writing does not come easily to you, your feelings can be effectively conveyed at this point by a simple written statement such as "Please save Greylock by abolishing the Tramway and placing the Reservation under the Department of Natural Resources." But if you don't mind writing letters for a good cause, then add a paragraph or two in your own words explaining why it is important to save our remaining mountain heritage from massive developments.

Send your comments to Governor John A. Volpe; Senator James F. Burke, Chairman, Ways and Means Committee; Senator Maurice A. Donahue, President of the Senate; and Senator Philip A. Quinn, Chairman, Committee on Natural Resources, State House, Boston, Massachusetts.

To be most effective, your message should be sent as soon as possible. If you don't have time to write a letter, send a postal card or telegram.

ART KOERBER

A BILL IN HOUSE

As previously reported in the NEWS, Senator Gaylord Nelson of Wisconsin introduced in the 89th Congress bill S. 622—"to facilitate the management, use, and public benefits from The Appalachian Trail"... and "to facilitate and promote Federal, State, local, and private cooperation and assistance for the promotion of the Trail." At the hearing on Sept. 16, 1965, this was favorably received by the Subcommittee on Parks and Recreation of the Senate Committee on Interior and Insular Affairs.

In January a bill identical to S. 622 and three revised versions of S. 622 were introduced in the House of Representatives by Congressmen John G. Dow of New York, Roy A. Taylor of North Carolina, Phillip J. Philbin of Massachusetts, and Nathaniel Craley, Jr., of Pennsylvania....

No doubt members residing in these States will want to thank and congratulate their Congressmen for introducing these bills since they will assist in the preservation of the Appalachian Trail, which coincides with the Long Trail from the Massachusetts line to Sherburne Pass, some 94.72 miles.

All members are urged to write to their Congressmen expressing their interest and support for this bill and to encourage an early hearing in the Committee of Interior and Insular Affairs. The House bill H.R. 12393, introduced January 27, 1966, by Congressman Roy A. Taylor of North Carolina is a revised version of S. 622 and provides for an Appalachian Trail Advisory Council, and other minor changes. This deserves our support—please write a letter or post card to your Congressman today!

KILLINGTON SECTION

The most important news from the Killington Section is that a major lumber operation at Clarendon has removed most of the wonderful white pine stand of trees that has made this spot so beautiful for years. Yet we feel that with the coming of spring, a few work parties and lots of help, the area can be cleaned up and with the large amount of new growth, it will be only a short time until Clarendon will look itself again.

The Lodge is still popular for every weekend there are many, many visitors. At the time of our moonlight hike, in spite of the -10-degree cold, there were scouts from South Londonderry, a group of Middlebury College Outing Club students, both overnighting and our group totaling 47 persons. The following day Terry Gulick led a group of Castleton College Outing Club students there for a cook-out supper.

A number of work parties have been held to get ready for the summer hiking season. Tucker and Gov. Clement will have to have major work done on them, including roof, walls and bunks, in addition to toilet houses. Sunnyside has had the roof repaired, windows replaced, new stove, outside creosoted, toilet house, dump and water supply improved. Clarendon has had the roof patched, windows replaced, the dump moved and toilet house built and the outside creosoted.

WORCESTER SECTION

Sunday afternoons during the winter months found us hiking around the nearby areas, and we must say we looked forward to these times of relaxation and fresh air. The winter went by all too quickly.

There was that super snowshoe hike in Millbury, led by our new members and end-to-enders, Dr. and Mrs. Richard Church. I am afraid some of us were not in quite as good condition as our leaders, but we learned as we went along not only how to bushwhack on snowshoes but how to clamber over fences, stone walls and fallen trees. We were gone over 3 hours and it was dark when we returned, but it had been a perfectly beautiful afternoon right after a big snowstorm and there was plenty of that soft fluffy stuff. Cocoa and crackers at the Churchs' refreshed us before we returned home. I am sure we all slept better than usual that night.

January 16 was a real cold and windy day, but nevertheless 13 of us hiked through the woods around a nearby reservoir. The snow was deep, but only 3 of us thought to bring snowshoes so there were some mighty envious people on that hike, especially one particular person who had brand new snowshoes back in the car.

Plans were made for a winter weekend at Northfield on January 22-23 with the other outing clubs, but it turned out that "there was no room for us in the Inn." The event was postponed until the following weekend, and the 7 that went reported a good time was had by all and the food was better than ever.

The Long Trail News

For those of you readers who are not familiar with our State reservations, I hasten to add I am not speaking of that other place.

February 20 was a real chiller, but 17 of us dared the elements to hike around Ramshorn Pond. Our leaders, Bob and Helen Vickstrom, warmed us with hot corn chowder and coffee at their home and treated us to an added bonus of colored slides.

A beautiful Sunday afternoon was February 27 as 7 of us snowshoed at Straw Hollow in Shrewsbury. Jim Green was our leader and he took us over new trails in the area and later invited us back to the Green homestead where Fran Green had delicious corn chowder and coffee and cake waiting for us. We eat well on these trips, as you can see!

An educational evening at the Worcester Science Museum brought out 24 people where we not only saw the planetarium show but were allowed to pet (if we wished) a big six-foot-long black snake. We also had the dubious privilege of seeing at close-hand a snapping turtle. I am afraid it was only the boys in the group who felt rewarded by this experience.

The weekend of March 12-13 found 10 of us at the Temple Inn in Temple, New Hampshire, where some of us went skiing and others climbed South Pack Monadnock in a sleet storm. The signs at the top said there was a beautiful view. Some of us visited a sugar house and saw the sap being cooked down into that delicious substance called maple syrup which, through the generosity of "Woody" Woodbury, we enjoyed the following morning on French toast. The weather was not too cooperative, but in spite of it we took some good hikes around the country roads.

Our sugaring-off was on March 19 with Jim Green as our Sugar Master. 85 people attended this event. The syrup was done to perfection, as is always the case when Jim is tending the big pots. The art of cooking seems to come natural to both Fran and Jim Green, and those of us lucky enough to be in their presence enjoy the art of eating. Following the sugar party, we enjoyed slides of the Long Trail by Richard and Betty Church and relived with them their interesting experiences when they became End-to-Enders!

LINNEA ELDRIDGE, Reporter


OUT ON THE TRAIL
A NEAR TRAGEDY, AND A WARNING

Although spring is here, and our Trail is returning to an appearance more familiar to most hikers, your reporter is going back to Thanksgiving weekend and a somber "Out on the Trail" theme.

A group of four boys, ages 15 and 16, ventured south from the Stratton Road. With parents' permission, they had no adult leader. Mistake No. 1. They were not prepared for the snow already on the ground. Mistake No. 2. As conditions worsened, they did not turn back. Mistake No. 3, and one that could have resulted in tragedy.

Briefly, here's what happened. That night sleet and snow fell. The next day the boys left the shelter and continued for 3½ miles until they lost the Trail. Then, in an exhausted state, and almost too late, they decided to turn back. One boy became extremely tired, and his buddies could barely keep him going. Returning to the shelter, they packed just a few items and left all the rest behind.

The rain and sleet continued, and it was getting late. The boys were soaked to the skin, and the one boy became physically and mentally exhausted to the point of hysterics. Here the group divided. Two boys went on ahead, and the third boy stayed with the sick boy.

The first group reached the road. They met a hunter who arranged for some hunters to search for the other two boys. They were found, about an hour and a half behind the first group. They had lost the Trail about an hour after the sun had gone down, and, fortunately, had bedded down in the Trail where they could be found.

A couple of these boys had been to the rugged Philmont Scout Ranch in New Mexico. But Vermont can be more rugged. They had summer hiking experience. But winter comes early in the Green Mountains.

The intent of this article is not to criticize. It is to emphasize the need for education on the basic rules of hiking a mountain trail.

Some quotes from our Guide Book: "The Trail is not dangerous . . . on the other hand the Trail is no garden path . . . the presence of snow at higher elevations early in the fall ... the hazards of winter outings must be considered . . . extreme weather conditions, icy firewood, the need for heavier packing ... winter trips should be done only in groups, with experienced leaders, and over familiar portions of the Trail."

Fortunately, this story had a safe ending. But it could have resulted in tragedy.

Each year, thousands of young people walk the Long Trail, safely. We have always encouraged young people to use the Trail. But here we may best conclude this edition of "Out on the Trail" with the famous motto of the Boy Scouts—BE PREPARED—plus my own philosophy—if conditions worsen, turn back; it is better to be a live coward than a dead hero!

BEN ROLSTON
