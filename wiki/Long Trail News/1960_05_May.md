OTHER PROBLEMS.

Beaver population. Reported that numbers of beaver have become excessive in many parts of the National Forest. Have become pests, not controlled by trapping, are flooding recreational and potential recreational areas including parts of Long Trail System and are generally detrimental to many fishing waters. The Forest Service in cooperation with Vermont Fish & Game Service proposes to proceed with a cautious program of control or removal of beaver in some areas where depredations cannot otherwise be controlled.
    After Mr. Maslack's report, the trustees passed the following resolution: "The Trustees of the Green Mountain Club are in whole accord with the report of the U.S. Forest Service with respect to the damage to the Long Trail and its shelters, by beavers particularly, and the proposed controls which they outlined."

GUIDE BOOK COMMITTEE REPORT

1960 purchasers of our 50th Anniversary Guide Book will receive with each Guide Book a small insert listing several changes in our Trails and Shelters which have occurred since the Guide Book was printed last year. In order that LONG TRAIL NEWS readers, who already have Guide Books, may receive this information, we are reprinting the text of the insert here:
    The Mt. Mansfield Summit House will not be open this season.
    Additional ski trails on the south side of Bromley Mt. intersect the Long Trail, so hikers should follow arrows carefully.
    A new chair lift will take skiers and summer tourists to the summit of Killington Peak, and additional facilities will be built nearby.
    A new chair lift of the Sugarbush Valley Ski Area reaches the ridge of Lincoln Mt. near Nancy Hanks Peak, and new facilities and trails will be found nearby.
    Worth Mountain Lodge has been built near Voter-White Lodge. Serving the Snow Bowl, it will be open to hikers during the hiking season, and will accommodate 30 campers.
    Sunrise Camp has been repaired, and now has bunks for ten persons.
    The new Pico Lodge is a closed cabin, has bunks for 12, and replaces the old Pico Shelter at the same scenic location.
    The new Frank Post Trail begins at Tillotson Camp and descends two miles to the road between Eden Mills and Route 58 west of Lowell.
    The Laura Golden Cowles Trail on Mt. Mansfield (formerly the Cowles Cutoff) parallels the Sunset Ridge Trail, beginning and ending on that trail, and provides a more sheltered route to the mountain.
    Should beaver flooding continue at Stratton Pond, the use of the east end of the North Shore Trail to Willis Ross Annex Shelter is not recommended.

KILLINGTON SECTION NEWS

Mention of the annual section meeting always
seems to be a good way to start off this report for
the Spring issue of the LONG TRAIL NEWS. About
20 attended the supper-meeting Jan. 27, and en-
joyed three color movies on conservation shown by
Mr. Paul Newcomb, Supervisor of the U.S. Forest
Service in Rutland. The following officers were
elected for the coming year: Bennett Bell, reelected
president; Ray Catozzi, vice president; and Eleanor
Walker, reelected secretary-treasurer for her 15th
straight year.

Some of the better attended events this past winter
were trips to Clarendon Lodge in November when
a dozen or so members of the Green Mt. College
Outing Club joined with us; Buffum Shelter in
December when again several out-of-town guests
hiked in with us; and the Winter Weekend at
Craigue Perkins camp in February. This last event
was one of the most successful in recent years, both
in numbers present and good times had. Fifty-two
in all showed up to partake of the family-styled
meals and good fellowship, and 45 stayed over-
night one night or more. Once again a large delega-
tion from the Green Mt. College Outing Club en-
joyed the weekend with us.

Graigue Perkins reports Pico and Clarendon
Lodges are in excellent condition; Sunnyside, Gov.
Clement and Tucker Lodge in good condition; and
Buffum in fair condition.

Pico Lodge is a new shelter, built last summer by
the Patrol to replace the very tired open-front Pico
Shelter. This lodge is closed in, and is located on the
same spot as the old one was, giving the same
beautiful view of Ascutney to the east and Killington
to the southeast. A housewarming on last Sept. 27
attracted about 30 members and friends to this
spot, and they enjoyed a beautiful fall day on Pico.
Just before winter settled in an old-fashioned work
party of six men (we're accustomed to one or 2 or 3)
packed in a new stove in all its 63 disassembled
parts and 200 pounds, making the lodge livable for
any winter hikers passing that way.

Buffum Shelter, another of the real old veterans
on the Long Trail, is, we fear, in its last days, and
we have hopes of rebuilding this one, too, in the near
future. How near depends on the 3 m's: money,
manpower and manageability of the old shelter to
remain standing until we find the first two.

Craigue Perkins also reports that maintenance of
Cooper Lodge, which is owned by the Vermont
Forest Service, has been turned over to the main
club by the Killington Section, due to its proximity
to the Killington Basin Ski Area and the anticipation
of excessive yearly repairs from the increased use
of the lodge. Cooper Lodge was built by the CCC
for the Vermont Forest Service in 1938 and has been
maintained, by agreement, by the Killington Sec-
tion, GMC, ever since. It needs extensive repairs,
including a completely rebuilt roof, and it is hoped
that the main club may be better able to convince
the Forest Service that these repairs, as well as
repairs in the future, should be taken care of by
the service.

Throughout the past year the shelters of the
Killington Section have been for the most part main-
tained by the untiring efforts of Craigue Perkins
and W. Churchill Scott who have attended nearly
every outing-work party scheduled during that time.
Rarely had an outing gone by that these two weren't
fixing something, big or small, at one of the shelters.
We also have a one-man work party in the name of
Ben Rolston of Schenectady whose pet project is
keeping Clarendon Lodge in excellent condition and
well supplied with firewood. We suspect Ben may
some day retire permanently to this camp, he loves
it so much.

Work lined up for the summer includes repairs on
the swinging bridge over Mill River at Clarendon
Gorge, where a few vertical cables have broken (the
bridge is still perfectly safe, please note); window
repairs at Tucker Lodge, where a new stone founda-
tion is also needed; and some changes in the swinging
window arrangement at Pico Lodge to prevent the
wind from damaging them if they should ever be
left open.

RAY CATOZZI

HEAR YE! HEAR YE!
YOU DON'T WANT TO MISS THE
BIGGEST INTERSECTIONAL YET!

The Green Mountain Club followers never had such a chance to get to hike around the northern hills of the State together, or just to enjoy a week's vacation at little cost, as is now offered by our next Intersectional get-together. We hope the word gets around early enough so that you can plan to take advantage of the BIG 50th ANNIVERSARY event now being "souped-up" by your Intersectional week's get-together committee and scheduled from August 27th through Labor Day, Sept. 5th.

The camping area for the BIG event is the Mt. Norris Scout Reservation of the Long Trail Council BSA, on the north shore of Lake Eden, Eden Mills, Vermont. The Camp has made the camp and its facilities available to the Green Mt. Club. This offer includes a large recreation hall with fireplace, boats and canoes, cots and mattresses and a limited number of tents. Volley ball and badminton courts will be available for the sport of the campers. Use of canoes and boats are restricted to the area of Eden Pond only. The camp has a phone and electric power hook up. It is close to stores and not far from churches of all faiths. It is located only a half mile from hard surfaced Route 100 to Newport, Vt.

All the Green Mountain Club Sections and members-at-large are invited to take part in this 50th anniversary event. Invitations are also being extended to the ADKers and other northeastern hiking clubs to join in the event. A special invitation is being extended to all End-To-Enders to attend a Recognition Camp Fire event scheduled for Saturday night, September 3rd.

Each Section will be asked to put on one evening's entertainment at Camp. It may be a square dance, stunts, songfest, story telling, speaker or what-have-you. We hope each section will name the night they will take and advise Co-chairman Don Havens, their choice of nights and type of event. He would like to know in advance, so let's all make those so called "campfires" the best ever and ones long to be remembered as worthwhile.

The Intersectional Week Camp opens Aug. 27th (Sat.) and Cochairman Larry Dean will be on hand to be of assistance to the arrivals, registering and assigning camp sites. It is requested, since it will add to the success of the camp, that the members of each section register with their Section president so that he can send a list to Larry by August 5th. However we want everyone to come whether they register in advance or not so don't let anything stand in your way.

All people will register on arrival at camp and a charge of 50c per person or $1.00 per family per day will be charged at that time. Name plates will be furnished and each is urged to wear them during the stay so everyone will know everyone. Shoulder patches commemorating the 50th Anniversary will be available to all attending the camp.

The Intersectional Week Committee does not provide for meals or supply cooking or eating gear. Each camper, family group or organized group must bring his own food, camping equipment and be prepared to take care of themselves during the stay.

There are seven cook shacks with wood stoves in the camp and plenty of area for developing your own campsites. Groups could team-up at the regular seven cook areas with a little planning. We will supply the wood for cooking. The Committee requests that all bring their own canvas as tentage in camp is very limited and can only be supplied to those who definitely cannot supply their own tentage for their stay. In those cases several will have to share the pyramid type tents together. There are a number of tent platforms available. Most of the campsites look out onto Eden Pond and the Chin of Mt. Mansfield can be seen from some spots and of course Mt. Norris stands close by small but real sporty-like.

FIFTY YEARS OF
NOTABLE ACHIEVEMENTS

In its first fifty years what have been the notable achievements of the Green Mountain Club?

First, the Long Trail, laid on the skyline of the main range of the Green Mountains, from Massachusetts to Canada, 255 miles, has been completed. There are also 170 miles of side trails. This makes a grand total of 425 miles of trails, enough to reach from Burlington to Boston and back, by the highway.

There are 61 shelters on the Long Trail, at attractive spots, at intervals of about 5 miles.

The first grand special mission of the Green Mountain Club has been splendidly accomplished.

Where else is there, where else can there be a comparable "Footpath in the Wilderness," a woodland and mountain trail the entire length of the State; a trail so easily reached; so hikable, not over too lofty, rugged mountains; in an almost unbroken wilderness; and in such safe, friendly and delectable mountains?

With its vast system of trails and shelters, the Green Mountains of Vermont are now the hiker's paradise. The Green Mountain Club has presented to the people of the State of Vermont, and their visiting tourists, a unique, priceless possession. A notable achievement.

The Green Mountain Club has made other notable achievements in its endeavors "to make the mountains play a larger part in the life of the people." There were no ladies among the charter members of the Green Mountain Club. In 1910 ladies with their long skirts were rarely mountain climbers or hikers. The members of the Green Mountain Club are now about equally divided between ladies and men. What has granted ladies freedom to enjoy healthful, delightful recreation in the Green Mountains equally with men? It has been the Green Mountain Club, in large part, as we all know.

A club of a thousand or more members about equally divided between men and women, is not that rather unusual? Is there any test for admission to the Club except to pay dues?

Another notable achievement of the Green Mountain Club in its efforts "to make the mountains play a larger part in the life of the people," is this:

Vermont has become a great, healthful, colorful winter-sports resort, with a multi-million dollar annual intake. How has that come about?

Some of us remember those Washington's Birthday outings of the Green Mountain Club, inspired by Jim Taylor-snowshoers thronging our mountains-Smuggler's Notch, Couching Lion, Nebraska Notch. Those winter outings were the forerunners of the present crowds of skiers thronging the slopes of our Green Mountains, from end to end.

It was certainly the Green Mountain Club that first disclosed the healthful, delightful winter recreation hidden in our Green Mountains.

The ski has stolen the show from the snowshoe. Skiing is a thrilling sport-for youngsters. Snowshoeing is pleasant, healthful recreation for everybody. In its winter outings the Green Mountain Club is retaining and increasing snowshoeing in the Green Mountains, as healthful, pleasurable recreation. There is no comparison between skiing and snowshoeing. Each has its place.

Mention should also be made of the activities of the Green Mountain Club in publishing its guide book and the LONG TRAIL NEWS; and the present outings organized by the various Sections of the Club. These activities have been very effective "to make the mountains play a larger part in the life of the people!"

What would the Green Mountains be without the Green Mountain Club? Give that a thought.

Now let us gratefully honor three men, who have done most for the Green Mountain Club, during the Club's first 50 years.

First, our highest honor goes to James P. Taylor, founder of the Green Mountain Club, and father of the Long Trail.

Then comes Prof. Will S. Monroe. Prof. Monroe taught us to place the Long Trail on the sky line. And Prof. Monroe built 38 miles of the Long Trail, the "Monroe Skyline Trail," from the Winooski River to the Brandon Gap, and three shelters,-Mountclair Glen, Cowles Cove and Birch Glen.

And now comes our good friend and great benefactor of the Green Mountain Club, Prof. Roy O. Buchanan. For 32 years Prof. Buchanan has been the devoted and herculean builder and repairer of trails and shelters. Prof. Buchanan's labors for the Green Mountain Club are beyond words of praise and gratitude.

Finally,-"To make the mountains play a larger part in the life of the people" is a glorious ideal. The strength of the hills is His also. To bring the strength of the hills to people is indeed a glorious ideal. In its first fifty years has not the Green Mountain Club made notable achievements toward attaining its glorious ideal?

The future of the Green Mountain Club is bright with promise. We will continue to lift up our eyes to the hills, renew our strength, and the Green Mountain Club in its next fifty years will accomplish even greater achievements "to make the mountains play a larger part in the life of the people."

C. P. COWLES, Charter Member
Burlington Section of Green Mt. Club, Life Member

NEW ENGLAND TRAIL CONFERENCE

April 2nd was a day of pleasant spring weather in Amherst as a bigger-than-ever group assembled for the 24th annual Trail Conference. It was most fortunate that the Public Health Auditorium could be used for the meetings, as the former meeting room, Skinner Hall, would never have been able to accommodate the crowd that attended.

The excellent reports of the constituent organizations were eagerly absorbed by those seeking information of trails and shelter conditions in their favorite hiking areas. Our Green Mountain Club report told of the work done by the 1959 Long Trail Patrol, and of plans of work to be done this year. Our reporter also showed the group our new 50th Anniversary Guide Book.

The afternoon program began with an informal talk on light weight packing, with emphasis on the use of dried foods. This was presented by Ben Rolston of the GMC, who took the audience on an illustrated End-to-End tour of the Long Trail, showing summer, foliage and winter views to the Trail, of the Trail, and from the Trail.

This was followed by a magnificent showing of nature slides by John A. Collis of Belchertown, Massachusetts. Many of these were greatly magnified views, and of especial interest were those showing the delicate beauty of the opening of buds and those showing the life cycle of caterpillars and moths.

After a fine dinner at the Methodist Church for which reservations were completely sold out, William L. Putnam of the AMC took us by movies in color on a hike over Mt. Washington, beginning at Pinkham Notch, passing over the summit, staying at Lake of the Clouds Hut, and descending to Crawford Notch. This AMC film was especially designed to emphasize the importance of mountain safety.

We were then led on a breath-taking trip to Alaska's Mt. McKinley by the noted explorer, Bradford Washburn. We saw North America's highest peak from distant views to close-ups with exceptional views of the Muldrow Glacier taken from a helicopter. Then we were shown the magnificence of Europe's Matterhorn, with final views from a plane circling the mountain at sunset.

John Hitchcock of Worcester did an excellent job as chairman (he was reelected for another year), and John Vondell of the GMC did his usual fine job of engaging the speakers for the program.

The GMC attendance was not as large as that of last year, but all those from our Club who attended agreed that this Conference was the best ever.

BEN ROLSTON

END-TO-END REPORT
OF STEPHEN L. CONGDON

I have been hearing of the Long Trail for years. A number of times I have operated the slide projector while my father, Herbert Congdon lectured about the Trail. But I had never taken the opportunity of going on it before last summer.

Having planned an almost unobstructed summer I made the length of the state in six trips with a total hiking time of twenty-one days. I felt I had to hurry; since I might not get an opportunity like this again.

In my first, or shakedown cruise, I went from Deerview shelter to the Arlington-Wardsboro road. As I was quite green I had to spend both nights without shelter. The first one, on Harmon hill was one I will never regret. Dinner was fresh, tender, fern fiddleheads followed by a beautiful sunset over Bennington. A soft bed of moss and a cloudless moonless night with brilliant stars capped the day.

On the second trip early in July I tested my rain gear in two thundershowers while going north to Manchester.

The third trip from Manchester's Prospect Rock to Appalachian Gap occurred in mid July and was the longest in time and second in distance. That time I saw my first people on the trail in 80 miles at Cooper Lodge and spent another night out (on purpose) since it was over 4000 feet on Mt. Abraham).

The fourth and fifth trips in August completed the Appalachian Gap-Canadian border sections included some very memorable days. Camel's Hump (fringed gentian!) in shifting thunderstorms was one. A fogbound day in Mable Taylor Butler Lodge was another, followed by climbing the length of Mansfield, from Butler to Taft, in pea-soup fog and a 40 mile wind. Going up the forehead and down the chin, imagining what could not be seen (anything over 45 feet) was the most exciting part. On the other hand, an absolutely still night with a full moon lighting Jay Peak, from Laura Woodward while a family of three porkies played in front the whole night through was perhaps the most beautiful although Glastenbury's view and Devil's Gulch after a rain run close second.

The last section from Deerview to the Mass., border fell over Labor Day. Pine Cobble, by the way, should be renamed blueberry hill. Next day I dragged my long suffering mother up there and we picked 13 quarts of berries in 3½ hours!

For the statisticians, in 20 nights on the trail I had human company on 6 and was alone on 14. Greatest porkie concentration is Glastenbury Mt. where I saw 16 in one day. In 21 hiking days 12 were spent without seeing another hiker while on 9 I met hikers.

Had a wonderful time!

WILDLIFE TO BE STUDIED
IN GREEN MT. FOREST

Wildlife study plots on a limited basis are being established at the northern district of the Green Mountain National Forest. Forest Supervisor Paul S. Newcomb, said several plots of one to several acres in size are receiving special treatment to promote habitat and seeding areas for game.

Wildlife is one of the several important considerations in managing the Forest for multiple use.
