"To Make the Vermont Mountains Play a Larger Part in the Life of the People":
The Founding of The Green Mountain Club, 1910


Stratton Mountain; where James P. Taylor waited for the mist to clear and thought about a "long trail."


On March 11, 1910, at 2:00 p.m. twenty-three prominent Vermonters met in the Sample Room of the Van Ness House in Burlington to found a hiking club. The name they chose for the infant organization was The Green Mountain Club. Its objective according to the new constitution was "to make the Vermont Mountains play a larger part in the life of the people." This purpose remains unchanged in 1985, the 75th anniversary year of the Green Mountain Club.

Vermont was ready in 1910 for an organization which saw itself as the defender and promoter of the state's major scenic asset, the Green Mountains. In the latter part of the nineteenth century Vermont had been neglected by tourists. Those who had the leisure, the inclination and the money to take a mountain vacation, traveled to New Hampshire and New York. The higher and wilder peaks of the White Mountains and the Adirondacks were better suited to the Victorian quest for "the picturesque and the sublime."

The new century brought changes which gradually played in Vermont's favor as a vacation destination. An emerging middle class in the industrial Northeast gave more Americans the time and means to travel. The automobile and new or improved roads made Vermont accessible to people in the more populated states to the south. Vermonters were waking up to the fact that tourism was good business. As the first chairman of the GMC State Membership Committee pointed out, "with this prophecy already proven by the energetic grasping of opportunity with unmeasured success by our neighbors, the White and Adirondack mountain groups," Vermont ought to enter the race for the tourist dollar. Summer visitors were said to leave behind eleven million dollars annually in New Hampshire alone.

There was also a young "back to nature" movement in the years following 1900. Instead of seeking the ultimate esthetic experience in the way of dramatic, cragged peaks against torn clouds, the traveler now saw in nature a comforting haven away from the ever changing city. Vermont was unchanging, pastoral, and safe. Her mountains were not isolated and removed from the population centers, as in New Hampshire and New York. The Green Mountains ran the length of the state, they were everywhere present, and their scale was less intimidating.

The state's mountain range was such an integral part of people's lives that it took outsiders to make Vermonters aware of their rocky, but valuable asset. In 1909 the state celebrated the 350th anniversary of the discovery of Lake Champlain by Samuel de Champlain. The occasion was important enough to warrant the presence of President William H. Taft and the Honorable James Bryce, the British ambassador.

On July 8 Bryce addressed the citizens of Burlington and invited dignitaries. Bryce who was an active mountain climber and a popular writer of travelogues, including his classic The American Commonwealth (1888), told Vermonters that their state would increase in popularity as a sanctuary for city dwellers. He continued:

One who foresees such a future, and who himself loved Nature would counsel you to bear this in mind, and to suffer none of the finest gifts of Nature to be lost by any want of foresight. To spare the woods whenever they are an element of beauty, to prevent unsightly buildings from destroying some exquisite project, to keep open the mountains, and allow no one to debar pedestrians from climbing to their tops and wandering along their slopes,

— as was often the case in Europe.

His remarks were heard and read by many, undoubtedly also by the twenty-three who met the following year in the same city to found the Green Mountain Club. But it took an outsider, a Yorker, to act as a catalyst for the renewed interest in Vermont's mountains.

James P. Taylor (1872-1949) is the founding father of the Green Mountain Club. He was born in Hamilton, New York, was educated at Colgate Academy and Colgate University (1895), and did graduate work at Harvard and Columbia universities. He returned to Colgate Academy to teach for seven years before he came to Vermont Academy as assistant principal in 1908. From then on he was an adopted Vermonter, one of the most avid promoters the state has had. Much of his energy was channeled into the Greater Vermont Association (to become the State Chamber of Commerce in 1922) where he worked for thirty-seven years until he drowned in Lake Champlain in 1949.

Two present-day historians call James Taylor "this slightly shabby, slightly disreputable chamber of commerce huckster, this amiable schemer, this compulsive promoter, this mercurial and most unvermonterish grand champion of Vermont." Whatever adjective one chooses to describe him, there is no doubt that Taylor was dynamic and strongly in favor of outdoor activities. At Vermont Academy in Saxtons River, a private preparatory school which paid "Special attention to life in the open," Taylor founded the Vermont Academy Mountain Club. Some of the requirements for membership were: "No man shall be eligible ... unless he has climbed at least once Crystal Rock, Signal Rock, Hartley Hill, Jones Mountain, and either Monadnock or Ascutney Mountain ...." Later Taylor would say that no young Vermonter should be allowed to vote unless he had hiked the length of the Long Trail.

Like Alden Partridge before him, Taylor led his students on many climbing expeditions. He was constantly bothered by the lack of trails and shelters in the Green Mountains. He began to envision a hiking club which would help make the mountains more accessible. Taylor got an unexpected opportunity to air his idea when he had to fill in as speaker for the Vermont Academy principal, George B. Lawson, at a banquet for Vermont schoolmasters at the Van Ness House in Burlington. Five minutes were at his disposal, and he talked about his dream of a hiking club.

The response must have been positive. Not long after invitations went out for the organizational meeting on March 11, 1910. The meeting was held in the same city at the same hotel, but in a smaller adjoining room. Of the twenty-three men who were present, eight were school superintendents or principals. There were three lawyers and one supreme court judge, three editors or journalists, one minister, one college professor, one business man, and one YMCA secretary. Present were also the State Superintendent of Education, the State Highway Commissioner, and the Fish and Game Commissioner. Letters of regret were received from Governor George H. Prouty, Joseph A. DeBoer, president of National Life Insurance Company, and Theodore N. Vail, president of the American Telephone & Telegraph Company. Prouty and DeBoer soon joined

The list of the founding fathers of the Green
Mountain Club is impressive. With so many
experienced and capable men present, it is not
surprising that they gave the club a sound constitution and a workable organizational structure which has remained mostly unchanged
for 75 years.

Any "Persons of legal age" could become
members. They were to be organized in local
chapters or sections. The oldest section is Burlington which was founded on August 8, 1910,
as the Mountain Man field section. Today there
are fourteen sections located in Vermont, New
York, Massachusetts, Connecticut, and
Canada. The total membership has grown
from twenty-three to over 4,000.

James Taylor was, of course, elected the
first president of the Green Mountain Club.
Always one to think big, Taylor wanted a
"long trail" along the spine of the Green Mountains from the Massachusetts border to
Canada. Thus the Long Trail, the country's
first long distance footpath, was born. It had
a model in Der Hoheweg in the Black Forest
in Germany. The Burlington Section began
the construction of the Long Trail in 1910.
Clarence Cowles, one of the club's founders,
and his law partner Sherman R. Moulton
were first to take axe in hand. The twenty-
five miles between Mount Mansfield and
Camel's Hump one of the most scenic sections of the Long Trail, were completed first.
During the summer of 1910 more than 1900
people climbed Mount Mansfield.

The fact that a scenic part of the Long Trail
was built first, was no coincidence. Taylor
understood the value of publicity. With the
cooperation of Charles R. Cummings, editor
of The Vermonter, the club prepared a slick
special issue of the magazine which described
the Green Mountain Club, its progress on the
Long Trail, and its plans for the future. This
May 1911 issue carried seventeen half-tone
cuts showing the finest mountain views. The
club, with some financial help from the newly founded State Department of Publicity,
bought 5,000 copies of the issue, over and
above the regular subscription, and mailed
3,000 out-of-state.

In 1912 the Forestry Department offered
to help with the trail building which was not
progressing very rapidly. The department
believed that the Long Trail could be combined with a fire patrol trail through the Green
Mountains. In just a few months the trail was
completed from Camel's hump to Killington,
a stretch of about 100 miles. Almost immediately there was dissatisfaction with the
foresters' trail. It did not follow the mountain
tops and thus did not have enough views. It
was partly built across burnt-over mountain
side, ideal conditions for fast growing brush.

Two years later when Will S. Monroe first
hiked in the Green Mountains, the trail was
virtually obliterated. Monroe (1863-1939),
one of the Green Mountain Club's pre-
eminent trail builders, decided to relocate the
Long Trail south of Camel's Hump. The next year,
1915, he returned to the University of Vermont to teach summer school. He combined
lecturing with trail work.

For eighteen days he and three other men
camped on the south side of Couching Lion,
Monroe's preferred name for Camel's Hump.
(He called his tent site Montclair Glen after
Montclair Normal School in New Jersey
where he was head of the department of
psychology; today Montclair Glen is the site
of a popular shelter on the Long Trail.) For
fourteen of the eighteen days it rained. In
Monroe's own words, "no one ... will ever
know what we suffered from the torments of
the Canadian black-fly and the midgets - nosee-ums, as called by the natives." In all
Monroe spent fifty-six days in the mountains
that summer, built 13.75 miles of trail, and
paid $432 out of his own pocket for expenses.

He continued for several years. He completed the famous Monroe Skyline section of
the Long Trail and exerted himself so much
that he had to take a year off from teaching.
Nevertheless, the native Pennsylvanian had
become just as devoted a Vermonter as James
P. Taylor. When Monroe retired in 1925 he
settled on the 100 acre Callahan farm in Duxbury, 1420 feet above the sea, on the side of
Camel's Hump. Here the world traveler who
had hiked in the Alps, studied in Paris, Grenoble, Jena and Leipzig, been a member of the
US Peace Inquiry Commission, and was a
friend of Balkan princes, settled with his books
and his dogs. He is buried near the entrance
to the Forestry Trail, one of the paths to the
summit of Couching Lion.

The completion of the Long Trail took not
five years as the club first anticipated, but
twenty years. In 1930 Professor Roy O.
Buchanan of the University of Vermont and
his son Bruce marked the last ten miles of the
trail to the Canadian border. As Bruce said,
"We better get rid of the almost." The following year, in 1931, Charles G. Doll and Phillips
D. Carleton cut the final link.

Shelters were gradually added along the
trail to accommodate overnight hikers. There
were never any serious plans to build large
staffed huts like those of the Appalachian
Mountain Club operated in the White Mountains. The structures were small and intentionally primitive. Hiking in the Green
Mountains was to remain a wilderness experience. Even so, early hikers may have
sighed upon reading the first Guide Book of the
Long Trail which appeared in 1917. "Best
telephone Mrs. C.S. Brush at Bolton in advance, and arrange transportation as boat is
usually on south bank of river." At Randall
lumber camp between Bolton and Camel's
Hump "Buildings were in poor conditions.
Stove in ox stable, which affords fair protection from rain and cold. No water." Today
there are some seventy shelters or lean-to's
along the trail.

In spite of, or because of, the primitiveness
of the Long Trail over 1,000 hikers have
become End-to-End'ers. That is, they have
walked the entire 265 mile "footpath in the
wilderness." Some have made it in one continuous trip. Others have taken as long as fifty-
two years. To receive the coveted certificate
and shoulder patch, hikers must submit a
report to a club committee. If the committee

SECTION NEWS

THE STERLING SECTION NEWS

The first outing of the Sterling Section on the winter schedule was held on December 15 and 16, an overniter at the Edwards camp. It was small, but successful. The people were almost outnumbered by Cubby, Hudson and Jessica, the newest member of the Section. She is a Pekinese, she loves to be out with the hikers and she can run circles around Cubby. However, when Andy and Smith took a walk over to Belding Pond, 125 lb Cubby did not fall through the ice, but little 10 lb Jessica did! They thought she was a goner for a moment, but she got out okay. The menu that night was spaghetti and beer. Bob arrived in time for chow, and Tony skied in the next morning in time for breakfast, however, Carroll was walking in when everyone else was leaving, so I guess he was in time to be counted.

The annual sledding party was held on January 6 at the Lawrences. There was just enough snow to slide down the driveway, however there were no snowbanks to save your neck if the sled got away from you. Seventeen members and guests attended and the sledding was really great. I myself made at least a dozen runs. For those of you who have never had a chance to slide down Carrolls driveway, it seems like it is a quarter mile long, twisting and turning down the hill. Reminds me a little of a bobsled run. Everyone had a great time, and we ended the day with a potluck supper. This outing was supposed to held in conjunction with a cross-country ski trip at the Little River Reservoir, but due to lack of snow, that was cancelled. The skiers decided to climb Mt. Hunger instead. It was nice on the top of Mt. Hunger, clear and crisp, but where were the blueberries?

On January 17, the Sterling Section Annual meeting was held at Lamoille Union HS. The following officers were elected for the coming year: President Robert Lindemann, 1st Vice President in charge of Trails & Shelters - John Lepinski, 2nd Vice President in charge of Outings - Jan Edwards, Secretary - Herb Fay, and Treasurer - Tony Smith. Smith Edwards is Director for the Main Club, with Robert Lindeman the alternate director. Section reporter is Jan Edwards. In the president's report, one of the clubs goals will be to expand the section membership this year by focusing on the 75th anniversary of the GMC. It was also suggested to offer new members a welcome package from the president, which could consist of a personal letter from the president, a section outing schedule and the latest issue of the LTN, along with the membership card. The main project for the Sterling Section for 1985 is to put a new roof on Beaver Meadow Lodge. We have the money in the treasury, and it should be done. Carroll Lawrence has put in a request for runner sleds - if anyone happens to have any to donate, or sees them at lawn sales, etc., he will gladly reimburse. This is probably because his sledding party has become so successful that he is running out of sleds. Let's see if we can help him out by being on the lookout for runner sleds.

On January 20, seven hearty skiers and two dogs (Cubby and Hudson) journeyed over to Elmore on what was supposed to be a very cold day to ski Little Elmore. Well, the day and the skiing both turned out to be beautiful - we skied approximately five miles across fields, down crooked snowmobile paths through the woods, up hills and down hills breaking trail through a foot of new snow, and ended up skiing down an old logging road back to the spotted cars. We continued the outing at Lindemann's chateau, where Smith, Tony, Bob, Andy and Carlene skied for a couple of hours more, but Jana and I opted for spiced cider and the Boston Celtics. Later on Sharon & Carroll Lawrence joined the group for pot luck and the Super Bowl.

The cross country ski outing at the Edwards Camp in February is always well attended, and we were at capacity again on the 2nd and 3rd. Paul & Jo Woodward from Fitchville, CT, had planned on attending again this year, but while practicing Jo injured her knee so they decided not to come. But Andy, Carlene, Ginny, Bob, Frankie, Carroll, Sharon, Tony, John, Judy, Don, Smith & Jan all skied or snowshoed the mile and 3/10 (uphill) into camp. Some arrived early and skied over to where the Long Trail goes down to French Camp, what is known as the Bucket. Because of individual levels of ability, small groups either skied or snowshoed here and there until it was time to consume the chili. After a gathering of the minds, some Trivia Pursuit and the Don Wallace Story Hour (it was a fantastic evening) it was time to crawl into the sleeping bags, leaving Carroll to tend the fires and let the dogs (there were four of them) in and out all night. After a chili breakfast, supplemented by bacon, eggs and pancakes, John led a group of skiers on a loop up the ridge crossing into the head of West Settlement Basin down to French Camp back by Cranberry Bog to Edwards Camp, approximately ten miles. Bob & Smith took the rest of us up behind camp, down the long trail to the Bucket and back to Camp via the Cranberry Bog, a little over two miles. The weather was great and there was plenty of snow. When we returned to camp for lunch, two other Sterling Section members had skied in to greet us, Dave Sanborn and Herb Fay. One of the Johnson residents, who lives near camp, was skiing in the area and she was pleased to learn of our group, she liked to ski but had seen nothing but snowmobilers - hopefully a future member of the club!

On Sunday, February 17, John Lepinski led a ski into the "Diggins", an area located on the upper end of the Green River Reservoir. Smith, Bob, Carroll and Tony (and of course Cubby and Hudson) trekked approximately 5 miles down from the Albany Road to the upper end of Green River down to the Reservoir then out through Devil's Gap to South Pond where they had spotted a car. It was a relatively level ski, a fun trip that took around three hours.

ST. ALBANS SECTION

While Smith & I were in Hawaii, the Section, consisting of Bob, Carroll, Tony and John hauled roofing material into Beaver Meadow in expectation of fixing the roof this summer. Carroll used the snow machine to haul in the 7 rolls of roofing paper, the others carried in stovepipe, nails and tar. We will definitely have a work weekend at Beaver Meadow on the Spring/Summer outing schedule!

The last scheduled Sterling Section outing was a ski into Belvidere Basin on the 17th of March, led by Smith Edwards and followed by five other brave souls, Bob, Carroll, Tony, John & Bill. There was fairly heavy new snow. They had a little trouble getting up to the Davis Neighborhood, but after arriving, they skied up the approach trail, which is an old log road, to Parker Camp. After a rest break, they followed skid trails and bushwhacked into the head of Belvidere Basin. They turned at the height of the land, which had taken over two hours to reach, and went back to the cars in less than half an hour. This is what happens when you have to break trail all the way in. As this was St. Patrick's Day, some of the Irish in the group felt they deserved a green brew or two, so they retired to the Charlmont.

The most popular winter outing was the sledding party at the Lawrences with seventeen attending, followed by the February outing at Edwards Camp with sixteen participating.

We are looking forward to another great summer. Some of the outings scheduled are overnights to the Adirondacks, Whites, canoe trips on the Winooski and Lamoille, plus several work trips to maintain our trail and camps.

Jan Edwards

THE HUNGRY HIKER
Winnie Shambo

Well, folks, after being cooped up in a bowling alley as co-director of a tournament for nine week-ends and another to help with the Youth Coca Cola Scholarship Bowling Tournament, we were more than anxious to be back in the forest. The weather held nicely as Honey Bear, my puppy, and I packed into Clarendon Shelter for an over-night. No snow. Lots of mud. And lots of relaxation for me just roaming the woods while Honey Bear enjoyed her freedom from the leash. She's a great companion. We loved every minute of being out there.

We want to thank those of you who responded to our appeal for recipe and food ideas for possible use in this column. Keep them coming.

Sorry to disappoint anyone looking for a recipe for Macaroni and Cheese. You will not find it in this column. We can hear the "oh's" now. That old stand-by is consumed up to four days a week by some backpackers simply because they lack trail meal-planning imagination. While a Caretaker at Stratton Pond we heard of an AT hiker who had consumed about 57 boxes of Kraft Macaroni and Cheese Dinner by the time he had reached Killington Peak.

In the first place, we can't imagine a more disagreeable meal and never have it while on the trail if it can be avoided. Secondly, there are so many other menus to choose from, such as Beef Stroganoff and Lemon Cottage Pudding.

Beef Stroganoff

1 pkg (4.5 oz) Lipton Deluxe Noodles & Sauce Stroganoff (sour cream & mushroom sauce)
2 1/2 Tbsp instant non-fat dry milk
2 Tbsp butter-flavored popcorn oil
2 Tbsp Mrs. Grass Homestyle Vegetable Dip & Soup Mix
1 pkg (2 oz) West Land Beef bar

To Prepare:
Break up beef bar and saute briefly in 1 Tbsp of the oil. Add 2 cups water, the pkg noodles, the remaining 1 Tbsp oil, dry milk, and veg. Bring to boil, then simmer about 8 minutes. Stir occasionally. If mixture becomes dry due to high altitude, add more water. Whole wheat pita (pocket bread) would go well with this dish.

Your elegant dessert treat is nothing more than Lemon Pudding served along with or over vanilla pound cake or angelfood cake, sliced and prepackaged for your trip. During fall or winter camping, pound cake will last longer than other cake types because of its high fat content. Spring or summer you'll want something with less fat otherwise it will spoil quickly in warm temperatures. Prepare instant lemon pudding as directed in the February 1985 Issue of LTN.

Unfortunately, not being a certified nutritionist, we are unable to supply the hiker with food value information regarding our menus. However, we have always been concerned with proper nutrition in preparing "home-cooked" meals, and would not knowingly recommend foods considered unhealthful.

There are a couple of ways to packaging food for the trail. In the first instance, all basics can be bagged together while all breakfast items are bagged together as are lunch, snack and dinners. Meals are prepared from the "pantry". We find this works fine for an "over-nighter". However, on an extended trip where every ounce counts and every inch of space is needed in the pack, we find it much more convenient to prepackage each meal as it is to be prepared, then bag each day's 3 meals in one larger bag and mark "Day 1", etc. This isn't to say you can't have "Day 2" on "Day 3" or whatever. We find this system of packaging is much easier on us at the end of a long backpacking day. It does entail a bit of pre-trip planning and packaging. You can almost tire of looking at all the food you have to eat in the near future. That is why we usually take a "real meal" for the first night out. Not only do you give yourself a treat but also, by the end of the next day, after you've done 16-20 miles that prepackaged food is going to taste like ambrosia.

"Let us not be upset and overwhelmed in that terrible rapid whirlpool called a dinner ... Weather this danger and you are safe, for the rest of the way is down hill."
— Henry Thoreau, Walden

NEW EDITION OF LONG TRAIL GUIDE

At the end of this month, the twenty-third edition of the Guide Book of the Long Trail will be ready for sale. While not radically different from previous guide books, this new edition does have several changes and improvements.

The first noticeable change is the new cover design. The green and yellow cover features the GMC logo and a diagonal banner that proclaims "75th Anniversary Edition". This departure from previous designs was made to emphasize the association between the Long Trail and the GMC, and to highlight the fact that this is our 75th year.

The new design isn't the only change to the cover. A heavier and more durable cover material was chosen that should hold up longer when subjected to the abuse the guide book often gets on the trail.

Inside the cover, one notices two fold-out maps instead of the single one expected. The Vermont state map has been moved to the front of the book, and brought up to date with the addition of the Interstate highways. In the back, there are detailed maps of Camel's Hump and Mt. Mansfield, and they're in color. These new maps were drafted by UVM student Jane Dosdall while she was working for the Club on an internship project.

The text and maps at first seem very much like prior editions, but a closer look shows that both have been updated to reflect the most current information available. The information on recent trail changes came from many sources: End-to-End reports, letters from hikers, Section Trails and Shelters Chairmen, U.S. Forest Service and Vt. Department of Forests, Parks and Recreation personnel, and most of all from the GMC's Field Assistants Ray Auger and Ben Davis.

There is a new section in the text that contains a map, summary and description of the Appalachian Trail between Route 12 and the Connecticut River. President Preston Bristow suggested this and obtained approval from the Dartmouth Outing Club to include it in our publication.

One other not so obvious change is that this edition is being produced by Northlight Studio Press of Barre. Northlight has done other work for the Club, including printing The Long Trail News, but this is their first GMC guide book.

A final note is that the twenty-third edition is dedicated to Bob Hagerman, a GMC'er who exemplified the volunteer spirit that makes it possible for the Club to undertake projects like publishing the Guide Book of the Long Trail.

The new guide book will make its debut at the Club's Annual Meeting on May 25th. The GMC Bookstore order form on page 10 has been updated to reflect the new edition and advance orders from GMC members are being accepted.

B.T. Fitzgerald

BOARD OF DIRECTORS

On Saturday, March 16, 1985, the Club's Board of Directors met in the Red Pines Restaurant at Quechee Gorge Motor Inn. Traditionally, the March Board meeting, when the Club's operating budget for next fiscal year is adopted, is a long one. And this year promised to be no exception.

FISCAL ANALYSIS

After the minutes of the January meeting were accepted, with minor corrections, Executive Director Harry Peet presented a Fiscal Analysis which projected a deficit of approximately $1,300 for the year ending April 30th.

He explained that expenses have been essentially as planned, but that income from several sources has been less than expected. At-Large Membership dues, sales of the Guide Book of the Long Trail, and the Club's annual solicitation of contributions did not quite meet expectations. In addition, much potential income was lost because of the twice-delayed publication of the new Mt. Mansfield and Camel's Hump trail maps. It was noted, however, that thirteen hundred dollars is actually less that 1% of the Club's total operating budget of more than $140,000, and should be no cause for alarm.

ADDENDA TO LONG TRAIL SYSTEM MANAGEMENT PLAN
Adopted by Board of Directors - 3/16/85

Under "Trail Standards" - add:

Vistas or vegetative openings along the trail provide opportunities for scenic enjoyments, which would not normally be available. They can range from the cutting of a few branches to the removal of several trees and, in some instances, includes the maintenance of hilltop pastures through brushing, prescribed burning, or grazing. With most of the Long Trail lying below timberline, man-made vistas can greatly enhance the hiking experience. New openings will be considered on a case-by-case basis by the group responsible for that section of trail maintenance. Permission of the landowner must be secured before cutting an opening. Openings will only be made large enough to provide a reasonable view and will be made in a manner that is as natural and unobtrusive as possible.

IN THE GREEN MOUNTAINS

FOCUS ON
WATER
QUALITY AND
GIARDIA

Along the many hiking trails of the Green Mountains pure drinking water is not as common as it once was. With the growing popularity of hiking, hundreds of thousands have ventured into remote and pristine areas, criss-crossing watersheds and coming into contact with dependable drinking water sources. Fewer and fewer surface waters remain pure.

The range of contaminants affecting water supplies along trails is wide, from food wastes improperly disposed of, to toothpaste and soap (even the biodegradable varieties), to fecal coliform bacteria and protozoan parasites.

For years the Green Mountain Club has been advising hikers that any water supply can become contaminated at any time. The mechanisms of water supply contamination can be as simple as one hiker washing dishes in a spring, or as complex as the introduction of a human parasite into the wild animal population.

Contamination by human pathogens may result from human defecation on the ground surface, or on the snow in winter, allowing runoff to reach surface water. Dogs can easily pollute water supplies by defecating in or near water sources, or on hiking trails - feces are then tracked or washed into the water.

One contaminant, a protozoan called Giardia lamblia, has received increasing attention in the Northeast during the past few years. G. lamblia is the world's most prevalent pathogenic gut protozoan, living in the small intestines of humans and animals.

Giardia has two stages in its life cycle: a highly mobile form which attaches to the intestinal wall by a small suction plate, and a slightly smaller cyst, formed when the mobile stage passes into the large intestine. The cysts are passed from the host via the feces, enter fresh water, are consumed by a new host, develop into the mobile form, and the cycle continues.

The number of cysts needed to infect an individual and cause discomfort is very low. Contaminated drinking water is the usual source of cysts. Symptoms of giardiases, or infection with Giardia, vary with the individual, from almost no symptoms (asymptomatic), to mild discomfort, to acute disease with severe diarrhea and weight loss. In some areas of the world, large segments of the population are infected. It is seldom fatal.

Over the past ten years, drinking water sources in the northeastern states have been invaded by Giardia lamblia, including areas of Vermont. Once Giardia is established in the wild animal population of a watershed, eradication is impossible, and risk of disease transmission is high. Dozens of mammals, including dogs, serve as transient hosts, though the mechanism is poorly understood. Most mammals are asymptomatic.

The cysts can survive for up to weeks in the guts of transient hosts, and live through extremes of winter cold. Only time, and careful precautionary measures, now slow the spread of Giardia to uncontaminated areas.

Treatment of water containing Giardia cysts is achieved by standard sterilization techniques. Bringing water to a boil destroys almost all micro-organisms. Boiling for twenty minutes kills viruses, and assures complete sterility.

Chlorine and iodine are used for chemical purification of water. Iodine is more effective than chlorine as it is not inactivated by organic matter and does not lose its potency quickly. The most widely adopted method of iodine treatment is to add 12.5 cc of saturated iodine solution to one liter of water, and let stand for twenty to forty minutes; water must be at least 70 degrees F.

Micropore filtration is used in many areas of the world. All micro-organisms except viruses are removed from the water. Where viruses such as those causing hepatitis are found boiling is still required. In areas enriched with Giardia, micropore filters are seeing wider use.

An authority on micropore filters is Jack Byrne, co-founder of Hydro-Pure of Vermont. Hydro-Pure was set up to help people resolve drinking water quality problems, and, as part of this effort, markets and distributes water purification devices (see ad elsewhere in this issue). Jack has done considerable research into what is available in the water purification market. Very few water filtration or purification units will effectively remove Giardia cysts for the full stated capacity of units, if at all. For that reason, Hydro-Pure markets micropore filters - structured matrix microfilters - made by General Ecology, Inc. They are completely effective for Giardia and bacterial removal. They are also the lowest priced units for what they do, and have seen widespread home, camping, travel, and commercial use. These filters have been used for years specifically for Giardia. The 0.4 micron pore size (compared to the 8 to 12 micron Giardia cyst) also eliminates bacterial, chemical, and physical contaminants.

Hydro-Pure has donated three portable micropore filters to the Green Mountain Club. These filters, under the trade name FIRST-NEED, are being assigned to the Club's Caretaker program for field testing over the 1985 season. Caretakers at high use sites, where the risk of water supply contamination is also high, will be evaluating them for ease of use, durability, and filtration capacity. A report on the results of these tests will appear in a later issue of The Long Trail News.

Prevention of water supply contamination along hiking trails in Vermont remains a goal of the Green Mountain Club. Through a variety of on- and off-the-trail educational efforts, the Club recommends and encourages sound guidelines for using the Long Trail System.

With the rapid and irrevocable influx of Giardia into many areas, including the Green Mountains, and with more than 100,000 people setting foot somewhere on the Long Trail system each year, the Green Mountain Club must continue its efforts to safeguard water supplies.

RELATING TO THE LONG TRAIL.

Whereas, the Green Mountain Club, recognized by this General Assembly as the founder, sponsor, defender and protector of the Long Trail system (R-44, 1971 Session), is this year celebrating its 75th Anniversary, and

Whereas, Vermont's famous Long Trail, a primitive footpath spanning the crest of our beloved Green Mountains from Massachusetts to Canada, is the state's premier hiking trail, and widely considered to be the first long-distance hiking trail established in the nation, and

Whereas, the Long Trail and its extensive system of side trails is both an inspiration and a source of enjoyment for the people of Vermont and her visitors, and

Whereas, the continued existence of the Long Trail system and the preservation of its usefulness, beauty and natural character is threatened by the encroachment of residential, commercial and business activity, now therefore be it

RESOLVED BY THE SENATE AND
HOUSE OF REPRESENTATIVES:

That this General Assembly considers the Long Trail system to be a unique, historic and irreplaceable resource, whose protection is of great benefit to the people of the State of Vermont, and be it further

Resolved: That this General Assembly supports the efforts of the Green Mountain Club to protect the Long Trail system through the voluntary transfer of perpetual rights-of-way by private property owners to the Green Mountain Club, private land trusts, and public agencies, and be it further

Resolved: That this General Assembly encourages voluntary federal, state, local and private cooperation in this effort to preserve the continuity and scenic qualities of the Long Trail and its side trails.

The Club appreciates both the continued support of the General Assembly, as evidenced by J.R.S. 18., and the confidence invested in the Club by this representative body.

To Senator Robert T. Gannett and Representative Henry H. Carse, the resolution's sponsors in the Legislature, the Club extends its thanks and appreciation.
