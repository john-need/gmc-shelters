PRESIDENT'S MESSAGE                       ANNUAL MEETING

Most of you were exposed to the radio and TV coverage of the situation involving 22 very ill-advised hikers who were forced to spend a bad night in an open front lean-to near Griffith Lake and then had the good fortune to be rescued relatively unharmed. The fact that we are not organized to offer any great amount of help in emergencies such as this should be of some concern. That we, as creators of this Trail, are not well-enough known to have been asked for advice, either by these hikers before starting or by the rescue groups during the emergency, might be of even greater concern. Hopefully, the wide coverage given this event will help prevent a reoccurrence. Your thoughtful suggestions for the Club's future activities, if any, in the search and rescue area will be appreciated.

Now that the National Trail Bill has been passed the Government is starting to move quickly. They have already put into action a plan to map the entire Appalachian Trail by aerial photography. The section from the Massachusetts line south to Georgia is well along. Maine, New Hampshire and Vermont were scheduled to be done first but early snow prevented this. Best conditions are when both leaves and snow are gone. During this relatively short period we must get many 20-foot plastic X's out on the Trail. Volunteers should contact your section or the Special Projects Committee.

The Club now has a Cooperative Agreement with the State of Vermont as it has had with the U.S. Forest Service for several years. The first joint action involving all three groups under these agreements is the purchase of "Leave Nothing But Footprints" signs. Certainly this cooperation will continue to our mutual benefit in the future.

This issue of the News always has the preliminary announcements of several important events. Look for them and make plans to be with us.

ROBERT W. ATTENBOROUGH, President.

PROPOSED BY-LAW CHANGES

The following proposed By-Law Changes will be voted on at the Annual Meeting on May 31. The paragraph or section is printed in full as revised followed by the reason for the change.

Article II, Section 4, Paragraph 2

Each section shall elect its trustees in the manner and at the time used to elect its officers. Each section shall notify the President of the Green Mountain Club, Inc. by April 15 of the election of its trustees. If such notification is not received by the President he shall consider the offices open and instruct the nominating committee accordingly.

(This paragraph is changed to give the Sections an additional two weeks to elect their trustees and makes it clear that the offices will be considered open unless the President has been notified of the election.)

Article II, Section 4, Paragraph 3

A nominating committee, appointed by the President at least four months previously, shall, at the Annual Meeting, present the name of one member for each vacancy of section trustees and of trustees representing members-at-large. The name of one member shall also be presented for clerk, and one for corresponding secretary. Additional nominations for all vacancies may be made from the floor. Voting shall be by paper ballot whenever there is more than one nominee for a vacancy. Election shall be by plurality of members present and voting at the Annual Meeting.

(This paragraph is changed to require the nominating committee be appointed at least 4 months prior to the Annual Meeting and also to clarify their duties and the voting procedure.)

GOVERNOR'S CONFERENCE ON RECREATION

The Annual Governor's Conference on Recreation was held in Montpelier, November 19, the theme this year being "The Challenge of Change." The conference featured guest speakers, the Governor's Luncheon, exhibits and discussion groups. The GMC was represented by an informative and interesting display arranged for by Dons Washburn, Montpelier Section.
One of the afternoon discussions sessions was devoted to "The Challenge of the Changing Demands for Trails." Commissioner of Forests & Parks, Robert Williams, keynoted the discussion, posing various problems and ideas for consideration. This was followed by viewpoints of special interest groups: Bicycling-John Meloney, American Youth Hostels; Cross-country skiing-John Caldwell, U.S. Ski Education Foundation; Snow-mobiling-Amos Colby, Vt. Representative of District 63; Hiking and Snowshoeing-Shirley Strong Green Mountain Club; Horseback riding-Herbert England, England Farms (Montpelier). Needs, problems and possible areas of cooperation were discussed. In concluding the session, it was felt that future meetings of the panel or designated representatives would be fruitful in planning for trails and trail use in the State.
The first meeting has been scheduled for January 28 by Mr. Ben Hoffman, State Forester in charge of trails. Hopefully, this will be an opportunity to exchange ideas, recognize each other's problems and needs, consider the use compatibility of trails and plan for a state-wide inventory of trails. Such coordination seems essential as demands for trails in the State increase.

SHIRLEY STRONG, Vice President

"TREES OF THE ADIRONDACK HIGH PEAK REGION"
A Review

This pocket-size book of 100 pages is one of the most practical tree-identification guides that your reviewer has ever seen.

This hiker's guide to trees of the Adirondacks was written by E. H. Ketchledge, Department of Forest Botany, State College of Forestry, Syracuse, N.Y. It is published by the Adirondack Mountain Club, and may be obtained for $1.50 from their headquarters at Gabriels, N.Y. 12939.

Many tree identification books deal with trees which the hiker will never find along his favorite woodland trails. But this book is unique-it describes only 34 trees. These are the 34 trees that can be found along the trails in the high peak region of the Adirondacks.

But what makes this book of great value to Long Trail hikers is that these same 34 trees are the ones which can also be found on our trail system in Vermont!

The book contains a concise description of each of these 34 trees. Accompanying each description is a leaf photograph, with grid squares representing one-inch spaces to indicate the size of the leaf.

In addition, there is a fool-proof leaf identification key whose 33 steps, by a process of elimination, makes identification certain. At the end of the book is a personal check list, where you may record each species you have identified and the location of your findings.

Add the study of trees to your hiking program. Obtain this book and start looking. A new adventure in Nature may be yours!

BEN ROLSTON

LONG TRAIL PATROL REPORT

This Long Trail Patrol Report will be the usual winter one, with nothing to report on Patrol work as we stopped in September. The various members are at their winter occupations. Two of them are in school. One has gone into the Air Force where he is in basic training in Kentucky and glad, no doubt, to be out of this weather. We are in a deep freeze up here and have winter weather since the first of November.

On December 31 two of us went up Mt. Mansfield, to check on Taft Lodge and found it mostly buried in snow. In fact, we could walk up to the ridge pole on the back side with no trouble. The stove pipe had to be cleaned out before the stove could be used. There have been practically no reports regarding possible vandalism of trails and shelters.

Sterling Pond Shelter seems to have suffered some damage again this fall; also Buchanan Lodge was shot up quite badly by hunters during the deer season. It would seem that they didn't have any luck so took it out on the cabin. I expect they have been busy elsewhere too.

This season the Patrol covered the northern part of the trail, starting at Whiteface Mtn. and working toward the Canadian border, doing necessary repairing of all shelters on the way. Blazing and relocating of trails was completed around Jay Peak where ski trails were still being built. This section of trail is now practically standardized all the way.

Roy Buchanan is recovering from an eye operation which he underwent for the removal of a cataract. He is at home now and seems to be his same chipper self. We hope he will be out on the trails again next summer.

I expect next summer to be practically free from black flies. If this weather holds for the rest of the winter it will take them all summer to thaw out.

I hope people are getting out on the trails and taking advantage of this fine snowshoe weather. Ski touring seems to be gaining as a good sport also. If one is dressed for the weather, winter hiking can be very rewarding.

This is a fairly accurate report as far as I know. Maybe we will have more next time.

JACK HARRINGTON, Chairman Long Trail Patrol

OUR 300TH END-TO-ENDER

In a violent snowstorm, on October 31, Dick Goldin, of Forest Hills, New York, found his way over Jay Peak. A few miles beyond, at Route 105, he became our 300th End-to-Ender. Let's hear him tell part of his story:

"I started hiking at Route 242 at 7:30 A.M. The weather was miserable, with between 2 and 4 inches of fresh snow on the Trail all the way up Jay. The violent snowstorm increased in intensity as I reached the rock ridges on the Jay summit ridge. It was a true winter day and there was a veritable blizzard on the summit. I had to take refuge in a doorway of the summit house, as icicles were forming on my nose tip and ear lobes.

"There were about five inches of powder on and around the summit, and the trees (everything) were solid white. I couldn't spot a blaze to save my life. That's where Ben's letter was a lifesaver. I followed his instructions to the letter and when I got about 500 feet below the summit the white blazes once again reappeared and I was on my way. I really started moving, with impending success so close at hand. Arrived at Route 105 at 12:35 P.M., official completion of my End-to-End. I was jubilant!"

Our End-to-End award was first given out in 1943. As many hikers had completed the Trail and were waiting for the certificate to be prepared, there were thirty-two End-to-Enders who came forward to claim the award that year.

Seventeen years later, in 1960, we awarded our 100th certificate. The recipient was Ernest Goss, one of the four members of the Goss family to receive our award (including Rene Goss, our youngest End-to-Ender at the age of nine).

It took only five years for the next 100 End-to-Enders. In 1965, Sylvia Koerber became our 200th End-to-Ender. But the pace has quickened! Only three years, and another 100 End-to-Enders have walked up the Trail to claim our coveted certificate.

ALL of our End-to-Enders have worked hard for their awards, and we congratulate them all. But we thought that Dick Goldin deserves a special recognition for being our 300th. Congratulations, Dick!

BRATTLEBORO SECTION

The Brattleboro Section has been active in small groups during the past year.

The Roger Davies have visited Branch Pond during the fall with one group of friends and then at another time they went into Little Rock Pond with a canoe and had a great time paddling around the pond.

Rex Coleman went with the Pioneer Valley group on an overnight hike over Killington and Pico. And at another time he went with some GMCers over Camel's Hump.

Everett Dunklee and a friend combined a canoeing and hiking trip into Baxter Lake Park where he climbed Mt. Katahdin and explored some of the nearby lakes by canoe.

Laurie Brown has just returned from a trip to Africa. He went on many hiking trips in the upland hills of Kenya and visited the Rift Valley in East Africa several times exploring a geological rock fault. He has interesting tales to tell of walking among giraffes and pulling a big python sunning itself on a log.

Repair to Swezey Camp

The problem of repairing or rebuilding Swezey Camp preoccupied the Brattleboro Section this year. On the several trail clearing trips into the area the Old Swezey Camp was studied and possible locations for a new camp were looked into. Finally it was decided to repair Old Swezey Camp.

One weekend in the fall, two jeeps carrying seven members of the Brattleboro Section (2 Douglases, 2 Davises, Bill Hawkins and Richard Crosby) plus roofing material and equipment went into Old Swezey Camp. The group tore off the old roof of the camp and replaced it with plywood and new paper shingles. They tarred the seams and put metal drip edging on the edges of the roof. The inside of the cabin was spruced up also by putting new plastic material in the window frames and new pieces of stove pipe on the stove. A good supply of firewood was worked up too, and left for the next hiker or hunter who needs a warm shelter. This group stayed overnight at the nearby Swezey Shelter where they heard bears hooting in the vicinity during the night. Other members joined the group for one day.

Personals

Frances (Dunklee) Taylor and her husband are now living at 1 Forest St. in Wilton, N.H. after living on a Naval Base in Puerto Rico for a year.

Lester Dunklee is going to school on Governor's Island, N.Y.C. until March. He is in the Coast Guard. He had a very enjoyable four week bicycle trip across the country in June and July.

Notice of Annual Meeting

The Annual Meeting for the Brattleboro Section is presently scheduled for January 30, 1969 at 8:30 P.M. at the Meeting Room of the Brattleboro Free Library.

BURLINGTON SECTION

During the fall and early winter, members and friends have participated in such activities as a well-attended corn roast on the Underhill side of Mt. Mansfield; hikes in the Mt. Cushman-Texas Falls area, to Butler Lodge, through Smugglers Notch with supper at French Camp, to Birch Glen where more than 40 had a birthday celebration, and to Indian Caves; a winter picnic with the Montpelier Section in the snowy fairyland of the Breadloaf region; and a showing of some of Clyde Smith's beautiful slides which has added enthusiasm to our plans for future winter hikes and igloo building.

A congenial group of over 140 attended the Annual Meeting on Saturday evening, January 11. Among the illustrious guests present were GMC President Bob Attenborough, Trustees Ben Rolston and Granger Kammerer, and Terry Gulick, Clerk of the main Club. At the business meeting after our usual good dinner of fried chicken and roast beef (only 30 people walked it off next day snowshoeing to the Shortsleeve camp on the slopes of Camel's Hump!) the following officers were elected: President-Harris Abbott, Vice President-Newell Briggs, Secretary-Betty Myers, Treasurer-Russell Farnsworth, Trails-David Fonseca, Properties-Ken Boyd, Recreation-Madeline Miles, Membership-Mrs. Ann P. Clark, Publicity-Elizabeth Thompson, Outings-Coralie Magoon, Trustee for 3 years-Jack Harrington. Committee reports were presented, probably the most impressive being that of Mrs. Clark, Membership, who reported a phenomenal increase of 96 members, bringing the total to 418! She attributes at least part of her success to personal responses to all inquiries.

The guest speaker was one of our own members, Dr. T. D. Seymour Bassett, UVM Archivist, who talked about one of our own mountains-Mansfield-and how the feelings of the people living in its shadow have changed through the years. He read some interesting and humorous accounts of the trials and tribulations imposed by the Mountain on early settlers. He also displayed old photographs and one of the first guest books from the Summit House.

Opportunity was afforded during the social hour before dinner and again after the meeting to enjoy not only this display but two others. Dave Fonseca and Buck Hadden are planning a full week's combination hike-canoe trip in the Algonquin Provincial Park beginning July 4th, and had large maps and pictures posted showing location and trails. Helen Farrington and Betty Myers are going even farther west-to Glacier Peak Wilderness in Washington. Beautifully illustrated books and pamphlets were intended to lure hikers to sign up for the "lazy-man's" trip they're arranging from August 9-17. See you?

Congratulations are in order for Fred Smith, our latest End-to-Ender!

ELIZABETH THOMPSON, Reporter

KILLINGTON SECTION

Shelter and camp matters have occupied the attention of this Section during recent months. The loss of Sunnyside-the second camp in Killington's domain to disappear by fire in recent years-left a need to do some fast planning. So the directors huddled together this Fall and approved plans to rebuild a similar type structure at or near the present Sunnyside site. Hopefully, more details will come forth as the Winter wears on towards Spring.

Meanwhile, an offer from Farm and Wilderness Camps in Bridgewater, Vt., to assist the Section in building a shelter on the Long Trail has been discussed at length by representatives of both the Camp and Section. If all preparatory details can be worked out before summer, a new shelter in the Killington Mountain area could become a reality. Location of the shelter would be away from the main mountain itself, possibly in the vicinity of the Shrewsbury Peak Trail-Long Trail Junction.

Churchill Scott's end-to-end celebration will have to be postponed 'til next year. While he bravely endeavored to finish his last stretch on trail up in the northern reaches of Vermont late this Fall, the weather looked him squarely in the eyes and said "No!" With that, an almost end-to-ender dejectedly returned to the road and reluctantly headed back home.

Members of the Killington Section were saddened when they awakened one November morning to the news that the well-known Long Trail Lodge had burned down. All will remember fond memories of the structure-one that probably had no equal elsewhere. From the steps of the Lodge one September night in 1931 a torch was lit signaling to others to touch off their bon-fires atop Vermont's

MONTPELIER SECTION

Six Adirondack enthusiasts camped out at Lake Eaton on a weekend in October. They explored the lake by canoe and watched a beaver at work. The next day they canoed on Forked Lake, finding more beaver dams. All were impressed by a large stand of native White Pine in the Adirondack Forest Preserve. The weather was beautiful but it has gone downhill fast since then. A trip to Mt. Hale in the White Mountains was called off because of weather. In November two members hiked in the fog from the Jay-Montgomery road to Hazen's Notch. Later in the month four members visited the Audubon Nature Center in Huntington. They were so pleased that they want to do it again in better weather when more people will turn out. December brought a hike to Blue Ridge Mt. near Rutland, weather still not too cooperative. Finally we scheduled a spaghetti supper at Parker Camp, and wouldn't you know? -It rained! This was the same day that the party of 22 got benighted in the southern part of the State. Parker Camp must have a jinx on us, because we tried to have a sugar party there last spring and ran into an awful blizzard. Both times our President, Doris Washburn came to the rescue by inviting us to her house. We have some snowshoeing coming up, one trip at least, by moonlight (?).

And so bedaubed with creosote, creosote, creosote,
And so bedaubed with creosote, the hedgehogs started
fasting.

And sometime Roy specialized, pecialized, pecialized,
And sometime Roy specialized, at this he had no equal,
The outdoor privies that he made, that he made, that he made,
The outdoor privies that he made are known to many
people.

And so if you have known this man, known this man, known
this man,
And so if you have known this man, you'll point to this
with pride,
Forever you will cherish him, cherish him, cherish him,
Forever you will cherish him as Teacher, Friend and
Guide.

The composition above was read when a group of friends
in the Burlington Section met at Don Haven's house on

Roy is making a slow recovery from the eye operation

the spring. If any experience enjoyed with Mr. Buchanan

THE PERILS OF WINTER HIKING

Looking back on that near-fatal adventure of students from Shenendehowa High School, who became stranded at Griffith Lake in an early-season blizzard . . .
     Let us not criticize. But let us use their experience as a warning to others, so that we may prevent another such episode which could very well result in the snuffing out of human lives somewhere on our wilderness footpath.
     Perhaps our best advice can be obtained by reading the paragraph on "Winter Use" on page 20 of our Guide Book:

WINTER TRIPS SHOULD BE DONE ONLY OVER FAMILIAR PORTIONS OF THE TRAIL.

     To all those persons who assisted in the search for and rescue of the stranded hikers, we all give thanks. A lesson has been learned, and may the nation-wide publicity given this experience help to make others aware that winter in Vermont, although beautiful, can be extreme, and that winter trips require expert leadership and preparations in order to be conducted safely.

BEN ROLSTON

NEW LOOK RANGE HIKE

Annual Range Hike in the White Mountains of New Hampshire
Sat.-Sun., July 19 thru 27, 1969

The Annual Range Hike will provide an opportunity to hike approximately 60 miles of scenic trails from Franconia to Pinkham Notch in the White Mountains of New Hampshire, including 40 miles of the Appalachian Trail, and a chance to reach the summit of 10 to 20 of the mountains above 4000 feet. The AMC Hut System will be used for bed and board, thus pack weight can be kept to a minimum. The prospectus will include a list of necessary equipment.

Because of the enthusiastic response to and suggestions during the 1968 Hike, three sections are planned for 1969. Section Able and Bravo, each accommodating 30 persons (15 male-15 female) will, as usual, traverse the Presidential Range-Able going from Franconia to Pinkham, and Bravo beginning at Pinkham and going to Franconia, meeting on the trail near Crawford Notch. The new companion group, Section Charlie, will be based at Pinkham Notch Camp (on Route 16) during the entire time (July 19-27) with the exception of two optional nights in easy-to-reach AMC Huts. This group will take short day trips in the same general area as the Range Hike but these will be less demanding from a physical standpoint-more of an introduction to White Mountain trails. To this will be added the comforts of civilization, such as hot showers, plus the possibility of staying "put" for the day if so desired.

Sections Able and Bravo will be conducted over rough and often steep mountain trails and should be attempted only by those in good physical condition who are accustomed to strenuous exercise. All participants must have adequate clothing, including sturdy leather footwear well broken in, high enough to protect the ankles, preferably with lug-type soles. The Mount Washington area is famous for its changeable weather, even in summer, therefore the leaders reserve the right to change the schedule enroute or to prohibit the participations of any person judged to menace the well-being or safety of themselves and/or the group.

The cost will be the same for each of the three sections, $110 for AMC and ATC members and $115 for non-members. This includes eight nights in the huts, with hearty meals, trail lunches, gratuities, and club overhead. Registration fee is $10 (deductable) and the deadline is June 10, 1969. Please make checks payable to the leader (listed below) of each section. A resume of ones hiking experience should be included with the reservation.

OUT ON THE TRAIL

TWO DECADES OF SHELTER BUILDING

This shelter-building program had a humble beginning in 1947, when the Sterling Section constructed Beaver Meadow Lodge in the basin east of the Sterling Range. Those workers never knew that they were starting something which would be continued for twenty years!

Our newer members, in their perusal of our Guide Book, are aware that we have seventy shelters along the Trail. Our older members will recall the construction of many of these shelters during recent years. But, WOULD YOU BELIEVE 53 SHELTERS, FROM 1947 THROUGH 1967, WITH AT LEAST ONE SHELTER BEING BUILT EACH YEAR?

OK, let's start with 1947, when Beaver Meadow Lodge was built. This was the first post-war shelter (none had been built since 1941). Montclair Glen and Hazen's Notch in 1948. Carmel, Wiley and Cooley Glen in 1949 (Cooley Glen was replaced by a new shelter in 1965).

Sterling Pond and Gorham were built in 1950. Taylor and Spring Lake in 1951 (Spring Lake was moved and renamed Sunnyside in 1955).

Clarendon and Voter-White in 1952. Ross Annex in 1953 (Ross Annex was dismantled in 1961, and the upper part became the top half of Bigelow).

Puffer and Skyline in 1954. Bromley and Sunnyside in 1955 (We lost Sunnyside by fire last August).

Laura Woodward, Cowles Cove and Swezey in 1956. And in 1957 the remains of old hunting camps were reconstituted into Bourn Pond Shelter.

Whiteface, Jay and Deer View in 1958 (Deer View was destroyed by fire in 1966). Worth Mountain and Pico in 1959.

Emily Proctor in 1960. Bigelow and Buffum in 1961 (We lost Buffum by fire in 1966).

In 1962 the U. S. Forest Service began their shelter-building program (which has added 13 shelters to our list). That year they started with Mad Tom, Lula Tye, Little Rock Pond and Greenwall.

Story Spring, Big Branch, Sucker Brook and Boyce in 1963. Sunrise and Wiley in 1964 (The former Wiley was then renamed Buchanan Lodge).

Then two great years of five shelters each! Seth Warner, Glastenbury, Lost Pond, North Bourn and Cooley Glen in 1965. South Bourn, Griffith Lake, Rolston Rest, Theron Dean and Duck Brook in 1966.

The shelter-building spree of two decades ended in 1967 with the construction of Congdon, Vandell, Battell and Atlas Valley.

The year of no new shelters-1968. But perhaps this was the year to catch our breath. It is now 1969. Does anyone wish to predict when and where the next shelter will appear?

BEN ROLSTON
