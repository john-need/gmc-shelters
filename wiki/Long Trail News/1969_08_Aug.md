HIGHLIGHTS OF THE ANNUAL MEETING

GOLDEN HATCHET AWARD

The presentation of the 1969 Better Camping magazine Golden Hatchet Award to the Green Mountain Club highlighted the afternoon session of the Annual Meeting. Miss Catherine McMullen, editor of Better Camping, came from Milwaukee to make the award, which is given to an organization or person who has made an outstanding contribution to family camping in the United States. Miss McMullen commented that both Better Camping (ten years old) and the Golden Hatchet (first awarded three years ago) are considerably younger than the Green Mountain Club. It was Alan Mead, organizer of the new Connecticut Section, who nominated the GMC for this award.

Roy Buchanan received the award for the club. With characteristic wit and unexpected brevity, he remarked that he had been advised that all he needed to do was say "Thank you." and sit down, and he proceeded to do just that!

Complimentary copies of the June issue of Better Camping which featured the article on the Long Trail, were available at the meeting.

JAN ABBOTT

WORKING TOGETHER AS GMC'ERS

A panel discussion with the theme "Working Together as GMC'ers," highlighted the morning session of the Annual Meeting. Viewpoints on the role of the Trustees and Officers, the Sections, and the Members-at-Large in achieving the Club's goals were presented by members of each of these segments of the Club. Dave Otis moderated the panel.

Trustees and Officers - President Bob Attenborough

Bob stressed that all members, and there are over 1,800 of us, make up one club, the GMC, and each should have a voice in its activities if he wishes it. The trustees and officers of the Club, our representatives, set the main policies for the Club, plan for the maintenance of the Long Trail, handle our dealings with the USFS and the State, our publications, etc.

They also sponsor the Intersectional each year. Last year the Club's budget exceeded $15,000. He felt that the trustees and officers could, if desired, offer Sections assistance in various ways, for example, with membership and coordination of hike schedules.

Sections - Dick Church (Worcester Section President)

Dick expressed the fact that most of the membership feels the Club's main reason for existing is the Long Trail and its preservation is the principal responsibility of the Club. The main links of communication between the Sections and the main club are the trustees and the Long Trail News.

He stressed the importance of electing good trustees. He felt the principal communication link was the LT News. He indicated an interest in the Sections by the officers and trustees was always welcome and expressed special appreciation for the interest shown by President Bob.

Members-at-Large - Lloyd Moore, East Meadow, N.Y.

Lloyd pointed out that many people are attracted to the Club and, for various reasons, they join as members-at-large. He posed many questions as to how these members can be more of a contributing segment of the Club: with 800 members-at-large, is there enough representation with two trustees; could they contribute ideas and articles to LTN; could they get together occasionally for special work parties; would they like a section called "Members-at-Large"; would it be feasible to have a meeting where these members could get together and work out ways of taking a more active part?

A general discussion followed which suggested some ways in which members-at-large could be made more a part of the Club's activities if they so desired, i.e., sectional hikes and work parties could be listed in the LT News with name of person to contact for details, and special projects could be designated for members-at-large. Generally it was felt that the large number of members-at-large represent not only those who would like to be more active if they were geographically located nearer the Trail or a Section, but also many who wish to contribute to the Club's goals but do not wish to participate in its activities. Perhaps we need another type of membership—a contributing membership. Members-at-large contribute substantially to our total income.

The question was raised as to whether the GMC should be a collection of clubs or a strong, centrally organized Club. There was definite support for a strong, central organization.

It was suggested that general work jobs organized by the Trails and Shelters Committee be listed in the LTN, if possible, so that both members-at-large and Section members might offer assistance.

Summary

A strong centrally organized Club is needed if we are to fulfill our goals of maintaining and protecting the Long Trail and its environment.

We need active Sections and more assistance on trail and shelter maintenance. Section members, unable to take an active role in trail work, can make a real contribution to the Club in numerous other ways.

The Club has room and need for everyone interested in its goals; we should be in a position to utilize the talents of everyone who wishes to contribute.

LET THE MEMBERS-AT-LARGE SPEAK

One of the thoughts brought out by Lloyd Moore in the panel discussion at the Annual Meeting was that members-at-large might be treated more as a group, possibly even as a loosely organized section. They constitute about a quarter of the total membership, but are spread far and wide. Many of them are active hikers. Some have said they would like to contribute time to work on the trail as section members do. All of them, through their annual dues, become an important source of revenue for trail work. They are represented by at-large trustees, but communications between the members and their representatives are tenuous, although comments and thoughts are welcomed by any officer, or any trustee, and particularly by the Long Trail News.

1969 ANNUAL MEETING REPORT

The 59th Annual Meeting was held at the Chopping Block and Bavarian Castle Restaurants in Proctorsville, Vt., on May 31, 1969. For the second consecutive year, a beautiful spring day helped set a new attendance record. 151 members and guests registered, 6 more than last year, and almost twice as many as 10 years ago. While the northeast provided its usual large majority, there were representatives from Wisconsin, Saskatchewan and Norway!

See "Working Together as GMCers" for a report of the interesting panel discussion featured in the morning session.

Normal business, Section trail reports and Committee reports were broken up by a fine cafeteria style lunch served at the back of the meeting room. Catherine McMullen, editor of Better Camping magazine, presented the magazine's annual GOLDEN HATCHET AWARD of a trophy and $100 to the GMC. Roy Buchanan spoke a few well chosen words as he accepted for the Club.

After 83 had enjoyed another good dinner at the Castle, the evening entertainment was provided in the basement Rathskeller. Ronald Rood, biologist, a former professor and author of numerous books and articles on nature subjects, presented a very interesting slide show and talk on many of the forms of wildlife one might encounter on the Long Trail.

RESUME OF TRUSTEES' MEETING

At a meeting of the Board of Trustees, following the 1969 Annual Meeting of the Club, new officers of the Board were elected. They are: President, Shirley Strong; Vice-President, Granget Kammerer; Treasurer, John Paulson; Auditor, Newell Briggs; and Trustee from the National Forest, Robert Deemer.

The trustees also took action on several other matters. One of these was the approval of a motion to instruct the By-laws Committee to prepare a change in the By-laws to correct problems discussed at the Annual Meeting.

The budget for the 1969-70 year was approved. It was voted to appropriate one hundred dollars to the Guidebook Committee so that Bulletins on the Outlying Trails could be printed.

It was also voted that a resume of trustees' meetings be included in The Long Trail News.

Arthur Koerber spoke to the trustees of his work as one of the three New England representatives on the Advisory Council that has been established to help administer Public Law 90-43, the "Trail Bill." He informed the trustees that Vermont Forest and Parks Commissioner, Robert Williams, is Vermont's representative on this council. He said that at this time he was convinced that the federal government was not trying to take the trail away from us.

There was discussion of the effect of the new Vermont three percent sales tax on our organization. As it is now understood the Club must collect the tax on all items it sells in the State.

GEORGE PLUMB, Clerk

LAST FOUNDER PASSES

The last of the original group of men who founded the Green Mountain Club in 1910 has died.

PROFESSOR KEMP R. B. FLINT, former head of the Department of Social Sciences at Norwich University, died at the age of 88 on April 26 at his home in Great Neck, New York.

K. R. B. Flint was a man of many parts. He had diverse interests but the most abiding of these was his deep feeling for Vermont and anything pertaining to Vermont. At the core of this was his concern for the beauty of the Vermont countryside, its preservation and its appreciation. For this reason he felt concern and interest in the Green Mountain Club and its works as a means of savoring the true aspect of Vermont in its mountains, forests and streams.

It is with regret we note the passing of this man. As one of the founders of our Club, he also, along with the other founders, is a giant to us. He was the last. In point of time, the GMC has turned a corner.

Burial was in West Cemetery, Middlebury, Vermont.

VICTOR H. JOHNSON, Historian

A NEW SUNNYSIDE CAMP

On Sunday, June 1, our new GMC President, Shirley Strong, performed her first official duty—the christening of a new Sunnyside Camp.

Our story has two beginnings. First, a fellow volunteers to build a shelter. And second, a camp burns to the ground and we need a new one.

Back in 1965, Sandy Stare built a shelter at his home on Cape Cod, moved it to Vermont, and a group of GMC'ers hauled it up to its new location on the Long Trail north of Baker Peak. We named it Lost Pond Shelter.

This spring, Sandy Stare told us he wanted to build another shelter. We persuaded him to direct his efforts toward building a new Sunnyside Camp, to replace the old camp which burned down last August.

A consultation with the Killington Section, and an agreement was made. Killington would design a new camp, and purchase the materials, and Sandy would build it. "Doc" Howard became the architect, Sandy became the chief carpenter, and the volunteers came forth.

The big work party assembled on May 17 at the Sunnyside site. The lumber was due to arrive at any moment. The site was cleared, lunches were devoured, and we waited. And waited. But no lumber. One by one, late in the day, the dejected volunteers retreated. Imagine Churchill's chagrin (for want of a better word) when he returned home and found the lumber in his yard!

During the next week, the misdirected lumber reached its proper destination, volunteers hauled it in to the site with Doc's jeep, and work began. By Friday night, the flooring and some of the framework had been completed.

Another work party arrived on Saturday, May 24. Up went the rafters, and other parts and pieces, and by suppertime great progress could be seen. The next day, Sunday the 25th, the clearing was alive with volunteers! Only a shortage of materials prevented the near-completion of the camp.

Sandy stayed on during that next week. More materials arrived, as did various assistants. Everything was NEARLY ready for a dedication.

On May 31, we had our Annual Meeting, so a construction holiday was declared. At the meeting, a suggestion was made that the Sunday hike should combine a trip from Route 140 to Route 103 and the dedication of the new camp.

Sunday was a nice day! Some took the short cut in to the camp, but the majority hiked in from Route 140. At 1 P.M. the ceremony began. Ben Rolston gave a brief history of the old Sunnyside Camp, and told of Sandy's shelter-building experiences of the past and present. "Wes" Weston, President of the Killington Section, extended greetings to all from Killington. Then our new President Shirley swung the plastic bag of Green Mountain Champagne (cold spring water), and Sunnyside Camp (and Sandy Stare) were officially christened. Over 40 GMC'ers and guests attended the ceremony.

Some stayed on to continue work on the camp, and the rest started up the Trail toward Route 103. Late that day, Sunnyside received its first customers—a young couple who had come all the way from Tennessee on the Appalachian Trail. They were to be Sunnyside's first overnight guests, and they expressed appreciation to the Club for the fine Trail and shelters they had enjoyed on their way up from the Massachusetts line.

Sandy remained at Sunnyside for a few more days to put the finishing touches (and creosote) on the new camp. He then left for northern Vermont to continue his hiking toward his End-to-End project.

Sunnyside had many builders and helpers. At the risk of forgetting some names, I will not list them. But they were Killington members from the Rutland area; a delegation of Killington members from Springfield; other Vermont GMC'ers; members from Massachusetts, Connecticut, New York and elsewhere; and some non-members who just wanted to help.

But the major portion of the work was done by Sandy Stare, who gave nearly three weeks of his time to this project. For his second major contribution to our shelter program, we all give him our thanks.

Say, Sandy, did I hear you ask where we want another shelter?

BEN ROLSTON

1970 — OUR 60th ANNIVERSARY YEAR

Sixty years in the life of the Green Mountains is about like three seconds in a year of human life. So, if the mountains could talk, they would say of the Footpath in the Wilderness, "Someone just tickled my back." Still, the Club has moved to recognize its 60th anniversary in 1970 because it represents a long period of human activity devoted to making "the mountains of Vermont play a larger part in the lives of the people."

However, there will be the usual tasks on the trail in 1970 and there are many anniversaries to come. The plan, therefore, is to "recognize" the milestone rather than "celebrate" it. In most respects it will be business as usual, but we will use the opportunity to stress, for members and non-members, the six decades of accomplishments and the related prospects of the Club. If you have some ideas along this line, the Committee will be glad to hear them. Particularly, if you, or someone you know, took part in some previous anniversary celebration, please let the Committee know.

The Committee consists of Art and Sylvia Koerber, Madeline Miles, John Nuffort, George Plumb, Edna and Granget Kammerer.

Question: How old are the Green Mountains? There are a few more than 31.5 million seconds in a year.

MEASURING WHEEL

Photo. by Herbert Ogden

The above measuring wheel was devised and made by the Herbert G. Ogdens, Senior and Junior, of Hartland, Vermont. They are both members of the Ascutney Trails Association and the Killington Section of the GMC. The wheel, of 1/2" plywood, is 25 3/8" in diameter, working on a common bicycle odometer designed to measure in tenths of a mile. By the addition of 9 more pins it now measures in hundredths of a mile. Total weight is 4.5 pounds and total cost is about $6.00. OUTLYING TRAILS REPORT

Progress in checking out various reported outlying trails continues at a satisfactory rate. As of early June, some 41 trails out of a tentative list of about 150 had been inspected and measured. This number will have more than doubled by the end of August.

As of early June four additional bulletins were in various stages of completion, and all should be available by the time this issue of the Long Trail News appears. It is also expected that one or two other bulletins will be ready by late August. The four bulletins in progress cover various trails in the Worcester Range of central Vermont, several trails, including fire tower trails, in northeast Vermont, the Wilmington-Somerset area, and the Groton State Forest area.

All new bulletins plus the original three bulletins will be available for sale at the Intersectional, or they may be obtained from the Club's office in Rutland. For the time being, prices remain at ten cents per bulletin plus a self-addressed stamped envelope; however, it is likely that there will be a price increase in the near future.

Since the Guide Book Committee is still feeling its way in issuing outlying trail data, your contributions, support, and constructive criticism can go a long way in helping us to do a better job in publishing trail information. One way in which you can help is to provide information or leads about possible outlying trails in your area or informing us about individuals or groups who maintain such trails. Another way is to send us your requests for information about trails or mountains which interest you so that we can establish priorities for issuing information about those trails which seem to be of most general interest. Finally we would like to be aware of your likes, dislikes, and suggestions for improving trail bulletins. There is nothing sacred about the content and format of the present bulletins, and your comments and criticisms can do much to insure that future bulletins serve your requirements better.

From time to time, we hope to publish outlying trail information in the Long Trail News with the frequency and amount of information depending to some extent on the available space. Since our quest for outlying trail information often turns up interesting or unusual odds and ends of data, we will try to include some of these in the News from time to time.

GEORGE PEARLSTEIN, Chairman

MT. OLGA

Located east of Wilmington on Vermont Route 9 between Molly Stark State Park and the Hogback Ski Area, this 2,438 foot peak has a fire tower from which there are good views of southern Vermont, Massachusetts, and New Hampshire.

The main trail (white blazed) begins in the state park opposite the caretaker's house, quickly crosses a small brook and heads south at an easy grade following an old stone wall and then a small stream before swinging east (0.5 m.) and climbing steadily to the summit (0.8 m.).

From the summit an unblazed but well defined trail descends steeply to the north, following the telephone line to a junction with a service road (0.1 m.). Following the road and the coinciding telephone line, the trail moderates its grade at an old stone wall (0.2 m.) and passes an old covered spring (0.5 m.) before reaching Route 9 at a point a quarter mile east of the entrance to the state park.

TACONIC CREST TRAIL

Maintained by the Taconic Hiking Club, this 30 mile trail follows the Taconic Range for some 30 miles between North Pownal, Vermont and Berry Pond in the Pittsfield (Mass.) State Forest. A mimeographed guide to the trail (8 pages, 35¢) may be obtained from Mr. Raymond H. Johnston, 50 Newark Street, Cohoes, New York 12047.

WEST POND MOUNTAIN

Perhaps the remotest of Vermont's fire tower summits, this 2,710 foot peak in the virtually unpopulated town of Ferdinand rewards its occasional visitor with outstanding local views of the Vermont-New Hampshire North Country and a breathtaking sweep of the White Mountains from the Franconias to the Mahoosucs.

From a St. Regis Paper Company Tree Farm sign on Vermont Route 102, 1.8 miles south of the village of Bloomfield, a jeep road to Dennis Pond bears to the right. It is recommended that most cars be left at this point, although the jeep road can be driven with caution to the actual starting point of the trail.

From the highway (0.0 m.), follow the jeep road west at an easy grade. Leaving the road (0.9 m.), the trail turns left and crosses Dennis Pond Brook on a wooden bridge (1.0 m.). For the most part heading west or southwest, the unblazed but well defined trail ascends easily through the woods, following the overhead telephone line and crossing several small streams. After cresting the shoulder of a pronounced bump and descending to a shallow col (3.6 m.), the trail climbs steeply to the ridge, turns sharply south (4.1 m.) and continues its stiff climb to the summit (4.4 m.).

Note: The Guide Book of The Long Trail and various topo and county highway maps indicate the trail to West Pond Mountain as beginning at Vermont Route 105 west of Bloomfield. This is the former route which has not been maintained for a number of years. Although easy to find and follow for most of its length, the upper section is obliterated in spots, and use of this older route, therefore, is not recommended.

More news on Roy's bathtub! When we asked Roy more about his bathtub on the front lawn last season, he said he had heard this story: That this man was walking down the street with his arm in a sling. So this, Roy says, is why he got rid of his bathtub. He doesn't want such a dangerous thing in his house.

BURLINGTON SECTION

In looking back over the spring outings, it is interesting to note that there appears to be a correlation between the number of participants and the expenditure of energy involved for any specific activity. Only four hikers climbed Laraway Mountain (on snowshoes in mid-April), five spent the better part of two days traveling 18 miles east from Sherburne Pass along the Appalachian Trail, 10 reached the top of Jay Peak, and a very few faithful members turned out for trail clearing and work parties.

On the other hand, 60 people sloshed through the mud to visit a sugar house (could samples of the Vermont "nectar" have been an added incentive?), 143 attended the annual sugar party and square dance, 28 rambled a mile or two up a woods road in Richmond to a spot affording lovely mountain views, 25 hiked to Bolton Lodge with a spaghetti dinner as the goal, and 19 in a fleet of six canoes paddled along Otter Creek.

Just to prove that no theory is flawless, 17 snowshoers struggled to the top of Mt. Elmore (in late April), and 18 bicyclists pedaled for 14 miles in the Huntington area, but only seven made the relatively easy walk up Brownell Mountain, and an equally small number took advantage of the snowshoe repair session.

At least we did have a variety of activities, and more are planned. We wish members and friends from other sections would join us. Those hiking this summer will be interested to know that Taft Lodge now has a caretaker (and a small fee); his services include gathering wood and carrying water. For those working on End-to-End, the section from Lincoln Gap to Appalachian Gap is scheduled for August 17, and Sherburne Pass to Brandon Gap for September 20-21. Our Outing Chairman, Coralie Magoon of Colchester (phone 878-2035), will be happy to furnish details about these and other early fall trips.

PIONEER VALLEY SECTION

Pioneer Valley Section members cleared the entire length of the Long Trail assigned to us (Massachusetts-Vermont border to Vermont Route 9) the weekend of May 17-18. Blowdowns, not too numerous, were removed wherever found, except where we thought that letting them stay might discourage one of our summer scourges, the motorcyclist, from trespassing on the Trail. The relative ease with which these cyclists can reach, for instance, Seth Warner Shelter, is a concern for all of us.

As of June 15, when this report was composed, considerable repair work needed to be done on the outhouse at Seth Warner Shelter. The shelter itself was in quite satisfactory condition, as were both Congdon Shelter and the adjacent outhouse.

Re-location of this section of the Long Trail continues to play a prominent part in the chapter's aims. Already, there has been a re-designation of the Long Trail in the Sucker Pond-Harmon Hill area. Specifically, the trail described as the alternate Long Trail, in the 1968 Guide Book, running from just south of Sucker Pond to south of Harmon Hill, has now been designated as the Long Trail. The former Long Trail, which went west of Sucker Pond, is no longer being maintained and should not be used.

A possible change during the summer on this newly designated section of the Long Trail, is that instead of Stamford Stream having to be crossed twice, near Congdon Shelter the Trail will be slightly re-routed so as to enable hikers to keep entirely on the west bank of the stream.

Re-location of the Long Trail from about 1 mile south of Seth Warner shelter to about 2 miles south of Congdon Shelter is in the planning stages, to avoid as many wet spots and touches of civilization as possible, as well as to provide for several scenic lookout from higher ground. Much of the pioneering work on this has been done by the chapter's Bill MacArthur. This relocation may occur, using the services of the Long Trail Patrol in early August.

Certainly, the word of advice to hikers using this section of the Long Trail this summer as our re-location work progresses, is to be especially careful to follow the familiar white trail blazes.
