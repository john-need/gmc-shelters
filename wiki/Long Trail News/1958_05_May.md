In regard to the Somerset reservoir in the southern part of Vermont, about which there have been rumors of recreational developments, Mr. Newcomb stated that the Government owns no land adjacent to the reservoir. He added, "We would like some land there for general recreational developments, and are going in there this summer to see what we would do if land can be obtained."

Ted Talbot was appointed chairman of the Guide Book committee. There are 687 Guide Books left of the 1956 printing. A supplement has been issued and may be purchased for ten cents or included free of charge with any purchase of a Guide Book.

John Paulson was appointed chairman of a committee to work on the revision of the Cook Book leaflet.

Uniform registration books should now be installed in all GMC shelters. These books were delivered at the office and distributed to section representatives.

Section representatives among the trustees reported that trail and shelter work will go on as usual this spring with no major building this year. Stoves have been installed in Glastenbury, Clarendon, Butler, and one is going in to Buffam soon. Pico Shelter is on its way out, but it is hoped it will last until the Killington Section's finances are built up.

Jack Martin showed a dummy of a new Short Trips folder that should be out by annual meeting time, or soon after.

The trustees passed a resolution to have the members of the Club vote on a change in the by-laws in regard to amount of assessment sent to the treasurer by the Sections for each adult membership. It was brought out that the 35c per adult member now being sent hardly covers the cost of four issues of the Long Trail News that each member receives. It was agreed that 50c per adult membership should be the amount sent to the main club. This will be voted on by the members at the annual meeting. Notice of this and the exact wording of the change in the by-law will be found on page one. No change is to be made in the junior membership assessment.

Due to sickness and poor driving conditions, five trustees were absent.

John Vondel put in another plea for slides, requesting that members please send him some. The work of his committee in making a new GMC slide collection depends upon their receiving many slides so a choice may be made. Past requests have been practically negligible. His address is 80 Fearing Street, Amherst, Massachusetts.

Miss Hinchey reported that Ben Rolston has been hiking during the winter, and has sent in reports as to the condition of trails and shelters. This information has been placed on the trail information cards.

CONSERVATION COMMITTEE REPORT

During the past few months, we have several times heard that a large commercial recreation area was being planned for the Somerset Reservoir area. If true, this would seriously affect one of the few remaining wilderness areas in southern Vermont.

The persistence of the reports, and the dependability of their origin, led us to write the National Forest Service in Rutland and Mr. Hamilton K. Pyles, Regional Forester in Upper Darby, Pa.

Mr. Newcomb, Forest Supervisor in Rutland, replied—

"As we do not own any of the lands adjacent to the Somerset Reservoir we do not of course have any plans for development of that area. The only area which we have in the general locality is the Mount Snow Winter Sports area, a portion of the ski area is under permit."

Mr. Pyles replied—

"We have no definite plans for this area so there is obviously a misunderstanding over this whole matter. Should ever the area in question come under the management of the Forest Service, all development will be in keeping with our standards which emphasize rustic construction and the maintenance of the natural charm of forest land to the fullest possible degree."

We are glad to be reassured by both sources that National Forest Service has no plans whatever for any recreational development in the Somerset Reservoir area. The beautiful, long lake there and surrounding wilderness would certainly be spoiled for ourselves and succeeding generations if speed boats and other so-called attractions were allowed to come in. Vermont must be careful that for a few commercial dollars now she does not ruin forever the charm and beauty that Nature gave her so abundantly.

The New York Section at its Annual Meeting on March 28th, unanimously passed the following resolution:—

Resolved

That the New York Section of the Green Mountain Club is firmly opposed to further opening of National Forest lands or wilderness areas in Vermont to commercial interests.

Also, that any proposed future developments be widely publicized six months in advance of any action so that all who are interested may have ample time to consider and approve or oppose the project.

LILLIAN P. LODGE
Conservation Chairman

KILLINGTON SECTION NEWS

The snow really piled up this winter in Vermont. Snowshoeing came into its own and proved to be the best means of hiking over fields and trails covered deep with snow. The Killington Section took advantage of this and snowshoed into Clarendon, Sunnyside, Buffam, and Tall Timber.

Other types of activities took in a winter weekend in Mendon, a sugar on snow party in Rutland, and participation in the Rutland Winter Carnival by entering a float in the parade.

Signs of spring are here at last for on the April 13th outing, members came out without snowshoes, and hiked in the Pine Hill area on the west side of Rutland. However, if the mystery hike had taken them on to the mountains, they would have found three to five feet of snow.

BRATTLEBORO SECTION NEWS

Thirty members attended the annual meeting and enjoyed slides of scenes on the Long Trail. Don Kent reported that the old makeshift stove at Swezey Camp had been replaced with a good second-hand stove that had been picked up at an auction.

Five members braved the elements on March 1st, and set out on snowshoes with provisions on a toboggan from the Scout Camp turn in Stratton—destination, Stratton Pond, Willis Ross Camp. They found a foot of new snow on top of the five to six feet of old snow, and all blazes were covered. It took them seven hours to reach Willis Ross Camp using maps, compasses, and trying to find blazes. Just after dark they reached camp and found it in good shape. The register showed that three New Yorkers had been there the preceding weekend, February 22 and 23. It took the Jays, Davises, and Bill Hawkins only two hours to follow their tracks back to the cars on Sunday afternoon.

On March 9th, a wintery Sunday afternoon, twenty-nine members and friends enjoyed a sugar on snow party at "Huck" Fullam's Sugar House on the Old Ferry Road in Brattleboro. Some tried tobogganing in the field nearby, and others went exploring in the woods.

Five members attended a portion of the New England Trail Conference at Amherst, Massachusetts on April 5th. They found the evening lecture on "The Wonderful Life Cycle around Bogs" as told by Stanley Grierson most enlightening and enjoyable. The pictures were wonderful.

A hike into Bromley Camp is scheduled for April 27th. The month of May will be spent by various work parties going into Swezey Camp and Swezey Shelter to work on the buildings and on the trails.

On May 31 and June 1 our Section will be hosts to any members of the parent club attending the annual meeting in Rutland who wish to hike on our section of trails—mainly around Swezey Camp and Swezey Shelter.

NEW YORK SECTION NEWS

The spring hiking season has started out in full swing, and many interesting trips are planned between now and July 1st, when the heavy hiking tapers off for the summer, and Camp Thendara comes into its own.

Our new bulletin lists not only hikes, but canoe cruises, trail clearing trips and delightful Saturday outings. What could be more enticing than an April 26th trip—"Cherry Blossoms in Branch Brook Park, Newark"? Or a later outing—"Dogwood Time in the Watchung Reservation"? It is a well known fact that no one need go to Washington to see cherry blossoms in profusion. Newark has as many, and probably more varieties.

The popular Bird Census Weekend at Thendara on May 10 and 11 with Mrs. Laura Woodward Abbott in charge, is followed by the annual housecleaning weekend up there, when all hands pitch in to give the whole camp a bright new look for the summer months.

Our Section hopes to have good representation at the Annual Meeting in Rutland (Sherburne, Vermont—Long Trail Lodge) on May 31st, despite several competing attractions on our schedule.

LILLIAN P. LODGE

WILLIAM O. WILEY

In the passing of William Odgen Wiley, the Green Mountain Club loses an old member and faithful friend. Mr. Wiley died in January at the age of 95. He had been a Club member since 1926. Although not active in Club affairs for some years, Mr. Wiley never lost interest in our aims and ambitions. He gave the New York Section money for a shelter on the Long Trail, and Wiley Lodge was built and named in his honor. He endowed this lodge so that it may be kept in good order and repaired when necessary.

He also made a generous contribution to the Killington Section for the re-building of Clarendon Lodge.

Mr. Wiley was a great-grandson of the late Charles Wiley, who founded the publishing house of Wiley & Sons Inc. in 1807. He joined the company in 1890, became president in 1925, chairman of the board in 1941, and retired in 1956.

STRATTON POND IN WINTER

By
EVERETT AND NELL SKINNER

Our trip to Stratton Pond was tremendous and will be an unforgettable experience. We left New York city at six P.M. Friday, February 21st and rolled along the New York Thruway in a spirit of great anticipation! But it was like spring on the Thruway—no snow. We were worried then cheered when we approached Albany, it was snowy there. As we neared Vermont, we knew that we would have plenty of snow and cold weather. At Bennington it was winter for real. We had decided to break up our trip and stay at Fay Fuller but on arriving in that vicinity, we could scarcely find the Lodge as it was all but buried in the snow. But we plowed in anyway, and a neighbor looking for some excitement helped us out. We got the door open, and then sort of dropped into the Lodge. Inside, around the door was a small field of ice over which we slipped and slid, lugging our gear in. Soon we had a fire roaring, and were sipping hot cocoa. It was exhilarating, and after a while fatigue settled on us and we crawled into our arctic bags, and were soon fast asleep.

In the morning after breakfast we started for Stratton Pond, wondering if we'd really get there because as we traveled along the snow got deeper until we were driving through tunnels of snow. It was piled at least nine feet on each side of the road. We'd never seen anything like this before! So much whiteness has a spiritual beauty or touch to it.

We stopped at the town clerk's office in Stratton and found the road was open to the Webster Memorial. When we arrived there we found two cars parked, and no room for us (by the way, the roads were wonderful). So we drove into the Scout camp and the caretaker let us park there. At one P.M. we put on snowshoes and followed the fine trail the folks ahead had made. The snow fell softly all afternoon as we forged ahead. At places the trail was muddled. Apparently the folks ahead would lose the trail markers and would fan out until they found them again. Most of the markers were buried and only occasional ones were found.

We arrived at the camp at four P.M. and found the trail breakers were four young scouts and their leader from Massachusetts. We had a great time cooking. Our only liquid was melted snow, and it took quantities of snow! Next morning though, we cut a hole in the pond for water. That was easier and much better. About nine P.M. the snow ceased falling, and the temperature dropped to 10 above zero. The air became clear, and the stars poured out everywhere, and the moon scudded about under dark clouds making odd bright lights in the sky. Next day about seven A.M. the temperature was zero, and it started to snow again. We were as Whittier puts it in "a Universe of sky and snow."

Our trip back was uneventful but we were so stimulated from the excitement and beauty of our holiday that it took a few days to get back to reality. We took pictures, and hope they will show some of what we saw.

GOOD ATTENDANCE AT
NEW ENGLAND TRAIL CONFERENCE

With attendance close to 150, and 40 of those present being GMCers, the New England Trail Conference had a busy and interesting day Saturday, April 5th at the University of Massachusetts in Amherst.

Arthur R. Koerber, President of the Conference presided, and kept the business of the day moving in a precise and orderly way that was a joy to behold. The morning session was taken up with reports on trails and shelters of the various organizations represented. John Paulson reported for the Green Mountain Club, and Paul Newcomb reported for the U.S. Forest Service on the parts of the Long Trail that go through Federal forests.

The Panel Discussion held in the afternoon was entertaining as well as instructive. Ben Ralston did a splendid job in describing and displaying what a hiker would need to carry in his pack for a weekend trip. It was surprising to see what could be carried in a small pack. Sixteen pounds was the total weight for all equipment and food needed. Jim Green's demonstration of work on the trail with a swivel stick, and his helpful hints on trail work were well received. Harold Cary told some interesting campside stories, and gave menus and recipes a little on the unusual side. To complete the panel, John Vondel gave some helpful pointers on photography.

"On The Trail—Connecticut to Canada" slides and commentary by Max H. Sauter left the impression with this reporter that there are many GMC shelters in need of rebuilding. However as some one said, "they are better than no shelter or better than carrying a tent, and a welcome sight to a tired hiker at the end of a long day." This refers to perhaps five or six of the shelters. On the other hand, there were many slides of shelters, lodges, and camps of which the GMC may be justifiably proud.

After dinner in the evening at Masonic Hall in Amherst, the group returned to Skinner Hall at the University, and enjoyed the usual good humor of a skit put on by the Metawampe Club. This year it was a one man performance by Karl Heindrickson.

The special feature of the evening was the lecture and slides of Stanley O. Grierson, naturalist and photographer from Katonah, New York. His subject, "The Wonderful Life Cycles Around Bogs" was presented in such an interesting way that many who heard him are sure to want to go searching for more knowledge on this fascinating subject.

LAKE CHAMPLAIN CELEBRATION

A mammoth celebration to be held in 1959 on the 350th anniversary of the discovery of Lake Champlain has been given financial support to the tune of $50,000 by the Vermont Legislature. A commission has been appointed with Perry H. Merrill as chairman to make arrangements for the state's official participation. New York state is cooperating with a financial outlay several times that of Vermont and the Province of Quebec will also cooperate.

The celebration will be a year long affair, starting with skiing and other winter sports, continuing with pageants, boating and other water activities, and including many land events. Whether mountain climbing on the Green Mountains enters into the picture remains to be seen.

Dear Sir:

As a member of the Club for 20 years' standing, I should like to add my small voice of protest at the projected Lincoln Mountain Ski Area. As one of your correspondents says, skiers have a sufficient choice of places to ski in Vermont and elsewhere already without appropriating such an important treasure of untouched wilderness. Like the aforesaid, I, too, enjoy skiing. However, I am forced to admit that not all skiers are equally careful of lodgings and facilities put at their disposal; and I believe the record shows that certain Long Trail hostels have suffered from the depredations of irresponsible skiers.

I, too, feel that certain desirable areas of the calibre of the one in question should be left to be enjoyed by those who have the energy and initiative to climb to them, rather than be carried to them.

Maintaining the present minimum of wilderness area in its hills would seem to me to be the only judicious disposition of Vermont's rich scenic resources. Without such conservation the lovely state of Vermont cannot hope long to retain in its entirety that wonderful appeal which at present still distinguishes it from any other state.

I am enclosing a check to cover my annual dues as a Member-at-Large, and a small contribution for Club needs. ($7.00 in all).

Sincerely yours,
FREDERICK B. HYDE
Associate Professor of Music History
University of Alabama

Dear Miss Perkins:

This is a belated answer to your request for members' opinions regarding the proposed Warren-Lincoln Mountain ski development in the Green Mountain National Forest.

As a skier and hiker, such proposals induce conflicting opinions in me. I can remember the thrill of (just once) riding up the Mt. Mansfield chair lift and skiing down that wonderful mountain. Still, as a hiker, I would as soon that such outstanding mountains were shielded from skiing developments. I can foresee the possibility that some day every peak in Vermont near the 4,000-foot class will have its chair-lift; and something will thereby have been gone from its wilderness beauty.

After all, if the GMNF were a National Park, wouldn't a privately-operated, or any, ski development be out of the question? (Not that pressures for the commercialization of the National Parks are not themselves increasing!)

I would like to see ski developments, if possible, deflected to secondary and outlying mountains (relative to the major mountain areas), of which I think there is a goodly number in Vermont. I think the trustees' suggested restraints on the proposed Lincoln Mountain development were quite moderate, if anything.

Finally, in view of current Federal policies which are encouraging increased private business operations on public lands: I would like to recommend that the Club look into the matter of securing from the federal government a more protected status than that of National Forest for the primary wilderness and scenic areas of Vermont.

Very sincerely yours,
ARMAND SIEGEL
723 Church Street
Ann Arbor, Michigan

Dear Mrs. Lodge,

I would like to add my protest to the others you have received concerning the proposed Lincoln Mountain Recreation Area. I am a member in good standing of The Green Mountain Club and of The Appalachian Mountain Club. I am an avid skier and I am very interested in the improvement of skiing and ski conditions.

Despite these interests I am opposed to the Lincoln Mountain development for several reasons. I believe that this area would constitute an over-development of skiing in the state of Vermont. The Vermont Development Commission lists no fewer than 30 ski areas in the state. This, in addition to other recent ski developments outside the state (Whiteface, Wildcat, etc.), add to the mounting number of ski areas with which this area would have to compete. Another consideration is the fact that several ski areas in Massachusetts and in Southern Vermont have snow making machines. This means that the skier doesn't have to and won't go so far north for good skiing. All this adds up to a tremendous competition for a new ski area in this part of the state.

Another reason I am opposed to this area is that it is to be on National Forest Land. I fail to see how a development of this type furthers the growing of timber. The National Forest System was set up to further conservation and to help replace the depleted supply of lumber in this country. This development would do neither. Vermont has few wild areas of natural beauty left; this development would raze still another one.

For these reasons I would like to voice my opposition to the proposed Lincoln Mountain Recreation Area.

Sincerely,
DONALD M. WALLACE
49 Fletcher Place
Burlington, Vt.
