OUR CHALLENGE
By PRESIDENT LARRY LIEAN

As the Green Mountain Club Inc. enters its 36th year of continued existence, we should consider our efforts and take an appraisement of the club as a whole. If we but do this we find that we have indeed much to be proud of. We are probably the second oldest mountain climbing club in the east, and, in spite of small numbers and limited resources, we have built one of the finest and best known hiking trails in America. Facts show that our existence as a Club has had a very definite triple value:

First. Our organization, through its club activities and its development of trail and shelters, has made it possible for more people to enjoy the sport of hiking and mountain climbing.

Second. Through the Green Mountain Club the State of Vermont has the distinction of being one of the few states to boast of a foot path the whole length of the state built entirely without cost to the state.

Third. The Long Trail has brought many people to Vermont from other states and from several foreign countries thereby becoming of distinct value to the state. It has truly become one of the state's so called trade marks.

GOVERNOR PRAISES G.M.C. WORK

In a recent letter to our club president, Gov. Mortimer R. Proctor writes, "The Green Mountain Club is a valuable asset to this state and its people. It is bringing health and enjoyment to those who use the trail and fine publicity to the state. I want to cooperate in every way I can to further the work of this notable Vermont organization."

OUR CHALLENGE

Let us remember that when James P. Taylor "sparked" the Green Mountain Club movement back in 1910 he was prompted by the wish to open, expand and improve trails and shelters for all who enjoy hiking and mountain climbing, thereby promoting the sport. Thirty-six years after finds that still is the club's aim.

While the Long Trail has been completed in a sense, in another it has not and never will be, for the Club should ever improve and keep it in condition, both the trail and its shelters. Yes, there is still, and there will always be, much work to be done.

During the past World War II period, limitations curtailed trail and shelter work to the point of near neglect. A few hikers complained about it, but we had justifiable excuses, and most of them realized that fact. Now the War is over, and it can no longer be an acceptable excuse for continued bad trail and shelter conditions. We are facing a challenge. We must put the trail and shelters back in as good, yes, in better shape than they were before the war began, and keep them so. Hikers, like anyone else, will only continue to come and repeat if conditions are favorable. While news of good trail condition circulates rapidly, news of bad trail and shelters travels much faster, and hurts not only the reputation of the Green Mountain Club but that of the State of Vermont as well.

Our whole trail and shelter system needs repairing and rebuilding in spots, trail blazes must be repainted and brush mowed all summing up to a lot of work. Undoubtedly the Long Trail Patrol will get the "go sign" to expand their work this coming season, but the section units must do their parts too. The trustees of the Club fully expect each of our sections, in or out of the state, to push its own section work to the forefront, and do it early. Indications are that a larger hiking and mountain climbing trend will be experienced this year than in many years past. If we get busy with our trail and shelter work we will help to encourage it, and make it worth while for those who come to Vermont.

As President, therefore, I would like to say that the Green Mountain Club has for the past thirty-five years taken upon itself the development and guardianship of the Long Trail system for the increased enjoyment of all, and it is my belief that we should take pride in our work. I expect all will want to help meet the challenge before us at the very earliest period.

Governor Proctor's Club

Gov. Mortimer R. Proctor has recently expressed his praise and opinion of the worth of the Green Mountain Club in a statement to the club president:

"The Green Mountain Club is a valuable asset to this state and its people. It is bringing health and enjoyment to those who use the trail and fine publicity to the state. Further the work of this notable Vermont organization."

J. P. Taylor Was Organizer

Larry Dean, president of the main organization of the Green Mountain Club, Inc., pointed out that mountain climbers and hikers owe James P. Taylor, now secretary of the state chamber of commerce, extreme gratitude for his aim to "spark" the Green Mountain Club movement. Had it not been for Taylor, said President Dean, there would probably not have been a mountain-climbing organization nor any Long Trail, and mountain peaks would still not have approaches cleared and trails marked. Taylor fathered the club that has become the second oldest mountain club in the east, ranked only by the Appalachian Club of Massachusetts in years of continued existence.

EDITORIAL
GMC ANNIVERSARY

Nearly everybody takes the Green Mountain Club for granted, but the 36th anniversary of its founding got some attention in the press through statements by Governor Proctor and the president of the club, Larry Dean of his city.

President Dean pointed out that mountain climbers owe the existence of this unique institution to James P. Taylor, now secretary of the State Chamber of Commerce. While teaching at Vermont Academy, Taylor started taking some of the boys on weekend hikes in the nearby mountain areas. He discovered that there were few well-marked trails and that few Vermonters tried to climb their mountains. He thought that a pity and decided to do something about it, though not himself a native Vermonter.

Soon Taylor had groups organized in various sections of the state for the purpose of making trails on nearby mountains. These were the groups which became the various sections of the Green Mountain Club, each taking responsibility for construction of trails and overnight cabins in their respective areas. Many from outside the state became interested in helping to build the Long Trail, this "footpath in the wilderness," which now extends the length of the state along the principal mountains, a total of about 260 miles.

Where another state might have spent considerable public money to construct and publicize such a unique undertaking, Vermont's Long Trail has developed primarily as a labor of love on the part of enthusiastic mountain climbers who have devoted their time, efforts and money to the construction and maintenance of the trail and cabins.

Footpower journeys over our mountains may be too much work for the average man in these days of speed and motor power, yet thousands of boys and girls and some adults have learned to love Vermont through such journeys and have become lifelong friends of the state.

IT DEPENDS ON THE POINT OF VIEW

We promised to print some gleanings from Long Trail lodge registers, and here is one to start with, from the register at Ritterbush Camp, dated July 23, 1945. We will not say who wrote it.

"Left Parker at 8:30 this morning—arrived here at about 4 P.M. 'How is the Trail?' There isn't any a good share of the way,--just make one for yourself. Nettles and weeds are not waist high now, but shoulder high in spots. Waded over my shoe-tops in muck and water in many places. It is not only difficult but actually dangerous to go over the trail (parts of it) between here and Laraway for brush and weeds obscure the footing, and with a heavy pack you are likely to stumble. I fell once in a bed of nettles, in a swamp. Devil's Gulch is a treacherous place and poorly marked. Ferns hide the entrances to horrible holes where you could stumble, pack and all, and never be found 'till doomsday! I have been crazy about the Trail for years and have spent weeks on it, but if it were all as deadly monotonous and totally uninteresting as from here to Laraway, I'd never go on it again. And that isn't just because I'm soaking wet either! There is no view, can't tell where you are or where you are getting to,—just peg along. Wish the Patrol (bless 'em) would be consistent in blazing. In some places there will be six or seven needless blazes, and where one needs one desperately there are none for long stretches. But all honor to the trail workers anyhow. Why don't some of these hunters help the trail a little? They seem to be the main ones to use it."

There's real enthusiasm for you! Next time we're around Ritterbush Camp we'll go up in Devil's Gulch and look in some of those horrible holes just to see if there might not be a hiker in one of them.

And now here is a quotation from a letter written last fall by a hiker to Prof. Buchanan, the Director of the Long Trail Patrol.

"I am beginning to feel that I just must not write in the Registers any more. I seem to convey the most doleful impressions! Two years ago when I got stuck (with my ten year old son and a huge pack) in all that mess of blowdowns between Shooting Star and Laura Woodward, and didn't get in until eleven at night, I wrote some kind of discouraged remarks in a register. I was told later by some hiker friends that Mr. Dorney had read it and was highly disgusted, the Trail being, of course, the apple of his eye. VALUES

By FREDERICK W. BRANCH

The land I own is hardly worth
The taxes that I pay,
The fields are squares of worn-out earth
Which crop but little hay,
The wood lots hold no stands of pine,
Nor groves of maple trees,
The tired soil within my lines
Lies resting at its ease.

But though the land is what it is,
The tenants don't complain,
The pheasants raise their families
Beside the meadow lane;
With paw- and hoof-prints in the snow
The other tenants sign
Their leases, as they come and go
Across this land of mine.

If I were young, I'd try to rouse
The fields to work for me,
I'd mend the walls, and pasture cows
Where pastures used to be;
But men, like land, grow tired, too,
So, as the years go by,
My land will rest, as I shall do :
The taxes aren't too high.

MEMORIES

By RICHARD BIRCH

Some small things will remain with me
No matter where I go
The fragrance of a mountain trail,
Moon shadows far below;
The saffron tint of early morn,
Its chill and searching breeze:
The scarlet hue of bunch berries,
Clustered beneath the trees;
The silver needles of the rain,
Beating hard against my face;
The rainbow up against the sky,
The fleeting storm to chase.

These things they will remain with me,
No matter where I go.
The open road, the woods of home,
Because I love them so.

CHARLES R. CUMMINGS

On November 25, 1945, the Green Mountain Club lost one of its best friends and most loyal supporters, in the death of Charles R. Cummings at Hartford, Vt. Mr. Cummings was born at Newport, Dec. 24, 1870, but he grew up and spent most of his life in White River Junction where he was engaged in the printing business. In 1906 he purchased "The Vermonter," founded eleven years before by Charles S. Forbes, and published it until his death, with credit to himself and benefit to the state. "The Vermonter" was never a profitable enterprise but when Mr. Cummings was once urged by friends to give it up, he refused, saying, "I have just hung on because I love the work." Yes, and he loved Vermont too.

He believed in the objects of the Green Mountain Club and recognized the value of the Long Trail to the state as well as to its users. He was a familiar figure at the annual meetings of the club, and he was always ready to give it a boost in the columns of his Vermonter. We feel keenly our loss in his passing, and we hope "The Vermonter" will live and thrive as his monument.

WORCESTER SECTION

Extracts from the Annual Report for 1945 of the Worcester Section of the Green Mountain Club, Inc.

As we slide into 1946 let us take a quick look backward into 1945. It has been a happy and prosperous year, especially happy in that we are able to welcome so many of our returning service people. At the close of the year there were only two left overseas, the rest were home or on the way.

We are still giving thanks for Camp Cobb. Many of our activities have been centered there. Many members and friends have contributed time and work in renovating the Camp and grounds. Many gifts have been received, notably a very fine flag and all the fixings, it has now been installed and Old Glory proudly flies whenever we are out there. Barn has been resilled, lights installed in the barn, etc. At last count 576 members and friends had registered there during the year.

Shelters and Trails made two trips to the Long Trail. Practically rebuilt Webster and patched up Willis Ross. Cleared trail, etc., in the pouring rain. As usual the Najerog boys were a big help in keeping their part of the trail in good hiking condition. We wouldn't know how to get along without them. This Committee has plans for a new camp to replace Webster. We'll hear more about that when conditions improve. We are sorry to lose Louis Bigelow's guiding hand in this work, but, we feel sure that Jim Green will carry on in true G.M.C. tradition.

Outing Committee has been exceedingly energetic. Has planned and executed 26 Outings. While the weatherman has not always been kind, members and friends have turned out to an average attendance of 23. Owing to travel restrictions many of our gatherings have been held at Camp Cobb or at the homes of members.

Entertainment Committee has furnished varied and pleasing forms of entertainment, consisting of pictures (both slides and movies) a magician and a hobby show.

Hospitality Committee has endeavored to keep in touch with all service people, also those of us who have sorrow or been blessed with a little sunshine.

Membership has been gaining slowly—a net gain of three for the year, with two applications pending, making a total membership of 135. It has been this Section's proud privilege to carry all service personnel (23 of them) on the active list without cost to them.

LOCAL SECTION, GREEN MTN. CLUB
ELECTS OFFICERS

From Burlington Free Press, Jan. 25, 1946

Burlington Section, Green Mountain Club, at its annual dinner meeting last night in the Waterman building elected the following officers for the coming year:

President, Leland Brown; vice-president, Richard Cowles; secretary, Miss Alice Rogers; treasurer, Ernest E. Smith; outing chairman, Alberta S. Dean; entertainment chairman, Kenneth Fisher; trail chairman, Prof. R. O. Buchanan; property chairman, Larry Dean.

The sense of the meeting emphasized the necessity for much work to be done on the section's trails and shelters this approaching year.

After the business meeting the president opened the session to an entertainment period. A few remarks were offered by James P. Taylor, state chamber of commerce secretary, and founder of the Green Mountain Club movement in 1910. Judge C. P. Cowles, Prof. R. O. Buchanan and L.B. Puffer spoke of experiences on the trails and a poem was read by Secretary Alice Rogers, written by Miss Mildred Armstrong, an active mountain climber.

Following the talks, kodachrome slides were shown of hiking and mountain climbing activities. Several of the slides were from the files of Prof. Buchanan and Puffer and from those of Kenneth Fisher.

Arrangements for the meeting were under the supervision of Mrs. R. O. Buchanan.

Miss Marion Smith and her father and mother arranged the table decorations. The dish-gardens depicted spots and events on the trail, with white candles set in birch logs adding to the glow of the settings. Miss Smith's place cards were appropriately done.
