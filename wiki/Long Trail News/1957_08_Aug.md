Trustees Meet:

At the adjournment of the meeting in Rutland, the trustees drove to the Long Trail Lodge in Sherburne and held their meeting there. Arthur Koerber was elected president, Ted Talbot, vice president; Ted Godard, treasurer.

Evening program:

Dinner was enjoyed by the many members who stayed for the evening at the Long Trail Lodge. After dinner the group went over to the Chalet where Arthur Fieldsend, a very talented photographer, showed some excellent pictures from his Vermont Album collection.

Sunday's program:

The big event for Sunday was to be the opening of the Clarendon Gorge Bridge, and then a hike up to Clarendon Lodge. But old man weather didn't cooperate. It was raining so hard that morning that all plans were cancelled, and new ones made. Everyone interested who could be contacted was called and told to meet at the Perkins Camp. Here, seventeen GMCers gathered, sat around the fireplace, told stories, viewed the new slide collection that the Killington Section has compiled, ate lunch, and then the rain stopped. It was a quick decision. Everyone wanted to go down to see the new bridge. We had already seen pictures of it under construction, for Emile Boselli, the builder, had shown them on the screen. Now we would see the bridge, and have the opening after all.

Walking across the fifty-five foot span some thirty feet up in the air, gave the group quite a thrill. The news photographer had arrived, a sufficient number of GMC dignitaries were on hand, and the green ribbon was in place ready to be cut. All this took considerable maneuvering on the part of Jack Martin, president of the Killington Section. Three year old Carol Martin, daughter of the president of the section, did the cutting of the ribbon. Besides Carol and her Dad, Arthur Koerber, new GMC president, Emile and Cecile Boselli, the builders, were present for the official picture taking.

Next came the speech making, and Jack, very appropriately told the story of the bridge, and then presented Emile and Cecile with two small gifts from the Killington Section to show appreciation for the work they had done.

By this time it was getting late in the afternoon, and several members had miles to go before reaching home so "Good-byes" were said, and thus closed another good Annual Meeting.

NEW GMC COLOR SLIDE COLLECTION

A new Long Trail color slide collection will be formed this fall to replace the GMC 100 slide set that has been in circulation many years.

The committee of John Vondell, Frances Green, and Arthur Fieldsend are eager to view all types of scenes and activities taken on the Long Trail. Donations of originals would be doubly appreciated, but it is expected that most of the collection will be duplicates of borrowed slides. It will be necessary to hold the slides about a month for selection and duplication. Slides should bear descriptive captions. Final slides will list makers credit.

The final set will include shelters, views, human interest, people hiking, equipment, signs, wild life, flowers, cooking, sleeping, and, in general "life on the Long Trail."

Please ship slides to John H. Vondell, 80 Fearing St., Amherst, Mass. Acknowledgement will be returned at once.

JOHN H. VONDELL, Chairman

MONTPELIER SECTION REPORT

The Montpelier Section has tried to plan activities about every other weekend, but we have not had very good attendance. We entertained the Burlington section and were entertained by them once. We have tried to vary our activities between climbs and road walks, with the usual sugar party and corn roast. Camel's Hump, Belvidere, Clark Brook, and Wiley Lodge attracted the largest crowds.

I feel that we should do our share of Trail work, and if a portion of Trail is assigned to us, I will try to get our able-bodied members together.

NEWS FROM BRATTLEBORO SECTION

At Bromley Lodge two small panels had been missing for some time from the bottom of the door, and had been replaced by a sheet of plywood. A trail hog came along and cut up the plywood to start a fire, consequently our enemy, the Porky, gained an entrance to the camp and had a field day. Temporary repairs were made from pieces of tin found at the old lodge, and then we gave the place a good sweeping.

On the way down the mountain, half way between the camp and the road we were fortunate enough to witness a little wilderness drama. A noise in the brush—and we paused expecting to see a deer leap out. Imagine our amazement to see the retreating back of a fully grown black bear. As she signaled danger to her cubs, our attention was drawn to a year old cub racing up the trunk of a large maple nearby. Another cub was heard racing through the brush. The mother turned and rose up on her haunches to face us. She made a splendid picture, standing erect, her ears cocked for any sounds we might make. A white patch of fur on her chest showed up prominently in the half-gloom of the forest.

After this exciting event, the rest of our trip was routine. We checked Swezey Camp and Swezey Shelter, placed Forest Fire posters and GMC posters in each, and cleaned out a spring at the new Shelter. We returned home happy and bursting with news of our eventful trip.

DON KENT

NOTES FROM THE NEW YORK SECTION

Here come the lazy summer months when Camp Thendara lures the city members with promises of warm days, cool nights, swimming, canoeing, hiking and of course, eating. The camp will be open during the week as well as on weekends. Many of our members have for years spent all or part of their vacations at Thendara and found it an ideal spot.

With the leaves of the calendar turning to September, we see many fine all day hikes listed. Canoeists have a weekend late in September; trail clearers have two outings to work on; the Catskill party comes on October 12th and 13th; several Saturday activities promise much fun; and the annual Long Trail weekend comes along August 31st and September 1st and 2nd.

So, you see that while we may loaf during the hot months, once the fall arrives we find plenty to do.

Our Section's attendance at the Annual Meeting on June 1st was not as good as hoped for, due to the fact that several activities were scheduled on that same weekend. We think we can do better in coming years.

LILLIAN P. LODGE

TRAIL REPORT

NEW YORK SECTION

The New York Section of the Long Trail on the whole is in fair to good condition. There is no major storm damage, and all shelters are in good condition with the exception of one, Glen Ellen, which is still usable.

The Trail from Lincoln Gap to Glen Ellen Lodge is in only fair condition. The section in the National Forest was cleared of blowdowns last year, but not brushed, and as a consequence parts leave much to be desired. From the National Forest to Glen Ellen Lodge there is some rough going where two winters of blowdowns still rule the Trail. The Trail is by no means impassable, however.

Glen Ellen Lodge is not in good condition. A few windows are broken, and someone used part of two bunks for firewood.

From Glen Ellen to Appalachian Gap the Trail is in fair condition. There are scattered blowdowns and some brushing out is needed.

The Trail from the Gap to Birch Glen Camp is in good condition. Birch Glen Camp is in fair condition. There are some leaks in the roof, and a new stove is needed.

The Trail from Birch Glen Camp to Huntington Gap is a little overgrown but from Huntington Gap clear to the Winooski River the Trail is in very good condition. There are very few blowdowns and in a few short spots the Trail is a little grown in, but nowhere should the hiker have trouble except for wet pants in the rainy season.

The rest of the shelters, Cowles Cove (newly built), Montclair Glen, Gorham and Wiley are all in very good condition. There are fifteen miles of good hiking trail over Burnt Rock Mt., Ethan Allan and Camel's Hump.

Of the side trails: Jerusalem, fair to good; Beane, very good; Forest City, very good; Allis, no report; Dean, no report; Alpine, poor; Callahan, fair to good; Burrows, fair to poor.

WILLIAM BARTLETT, Chairman
Vt. Trails & Shelters
New York Section, G.M.C.

KILLINGTON SECTION NEWS

Between hikes and work parties, most of Killington Section's Trail has been visited this spring and summer.

The pancake breakfast, held at Clarendon Lodge, was enjoyed by a good sized group; the Governor Clement hike turned into a work party; and the hike to Little Rock Pond was, as usual, a big success.

Trails chairman, Craigue Perkins, went over the Trail from Governor Clement shelter to Cooper Lodge and reported that though the road into Gov. M.C.M. MEMBERS HIKE ON
THE LONG TRAIL

Four Mountain Club of Maryland members spent ten days on the Long Trail during the summer of 1956. In a very interesting story published in their Spring issue, one of the hikers describes in detail each day's hike. Each hiker carried a 38-pound pack that included all the food, clothing and equipment needed for the trip.

Starting at the Beane Farm they proceeded to hike north for sixty-four miles. Plans for this trip had started months in advance with Prof. Roy Buchanan and Larry Dean giving advice and suggestions. The particular area between Birch Glen Lodge and Smuggler's Notch was chosen because it was considered to be the most scenic, spectacular, and rugged on the Long Trail. Prof. Buchanan also assisted the hikers by driving their car to Smuggler's Notch, parking it at the ranger station, and leaving the key with the ranger.

We will quote as much of the story as possible here, and regret that space does not permit using the entire story.

"38 POUNDS AND INDEPENDENCE"

"THE STORY OF A GREEN MOUNTAIN PACK TRIP"

"The Trip"

"Monday June 26 . . . . The Beane Farm is 1.5 miles east of Hanksville, Vermont. (This is interjected here so the reader will know exactly where we started.)

"After one half hour's hiking we had covered the 9-10 miles from the farm to Birch Glen Camp where we would spend our first night. Birch Glen was an open-front shelter about twice the size of the A-T lean-tos in this area. The bunk room was in the rear of the structure. The roof and floor extended forward to form an open room of porch which was enclosed by a solid railing about three feet high with a gate at one end.

"The most amazing thing about the building was that the lower portion was completely sheathed with sheet metal. Why should anyone go to all this trouble? Betty, who was the only member present who had camped in Yankee country before, explained that the metal was to keep porcupines out. We were to find that all of the huts had some provision for preventing the entrance of these unbelievably destructive pests.

"That evening a large porky strolled past the shelter. He was the first of a long line of porkies which we were to encounter, and which still parade through our memories today.

"Tuesday, June 27 .... After breakfast we started out in a light drizzle .... Soon the rain was falling by the bucketful. The Trail rose gently and became more rugged. . . . After lunch we ascended Burnt Rock Mountain over an irregular, rocky trail. ... The rain stopped for a short while but was to continue intermittently for the remainder of the day. We crossed several knobs and came to Ladder Ravine, so named because a rustic ladder is placed here to aid the hiker in ascending or descending the face.

"We made the steep climb up the skidway sloshing through the water toward the summit of Mt. Ira Allen. . . . The Trail was overgrown over much of the distance and blow-downs were everywhere. For hours we toiled along, slogging through high, wet brush, climbing over and crawling under blow-downs. In places we lost the trail and had to backtrack to pick up the blazes.

"In this manner we crossed Mt. Ira Allen, the south and north peaks of Mt. Ethan Allen and dropped down sharply to Montclair Glen Lodge. This was undoubtedly the roughest day I have ever spent on the Trail. We had consumed ten hours in traversing nine miles and we were bushed. All of us were soaked to the skin and our hands and clothing were black with pitch of trees we had fought our way through. In short, we were a mess .... We were in bed with the sunset.

"Thursday, June 28 .... Farther along we stopped at Allen Smith spring. This was one of the many excellent springs we passed on our trek. Somehow it seemed a little unfair that the Green Mountains should have such an overabundance of springs while there are so many thirsty miles along our trails in the Blue Ridge.

"After crossing two minor knobs the Trail became quite steep. We toiled upwardly for about a mile until we came out on the open rocks. The wind nearly blew us from the mountain but the sky was beautiful and there were fine vistas all about us. The Trail crossed the rocks to west side of the mountain rising to the summit as it did so.

"Camel's Hump, the fourth highest mountain in Vermont (elevation 4083), is a king-sized Old Rag. It was a magnificent hunk of rock and was indeed worth climbing ....

"Gorham Lodge was well situated on the side of the mountain, with a sparkling brook running nearby .... Before retiring we played "Spill & Spell," a game in which words are formed from letters on small dice.

"Friday, June 29 .... A light fog stayed with us for the first several hours of our jaunt. This was unfortunate because the Guide Book had mentioned the numerous and outstanding views on this section of Trail.

"The Trail lost altitude rapidly then leveled, crossing several rocky knobs and coming out on an escarpment. From the escarpment we headed steeply downhill through heavy woods, and as we did so, the rain returned, making footing slippery. After following the ever-descending path for nearly two miles we reached Wiley Lodge.

"Saturday, June 30. . . The sky was superbly clear, the bum knee was much improved, and the group was in excellent spirits as we followed the old logging road which wound northward from the lodge . . . . In thirty minutes time we found ourselves on the south bank of the Winooski River. Our Guide informed us that if we wished to cross at this point, we would have to avail ourselves of the

services of a ferry operated by the May family who lived just west of the Trail.

"A rap on the door of the May mansion brought a sleepy-eyed gentleman to the door. He allowed that he ferried folks across but he said his boat leaked badly and, in general, he seemed loath to exert himself at that early hour. After telling Mr. May a sad narrative about my car being somewhere on the other side of this great natural barrier, and telling him that we were all excellent swimmers, and that our insurance was paid up, and that we were willing to chance the voyage, he reluctantly led us to a half submerged punt. We turned the boat upside down to spill out the water, and then launched the craft. Water poured through the leaks at an alarmingly rapid rate but our ferryman was of the opinion that he could paddle across before the craft sank. The ferry was too small to carry more than two passengers at a time, so Allen and the Supreme Commander, being gentlemen as always, allowed the ladies to make the first voyage. Our boatman returned triumphant from a successful first run and again the ark was beached and drained.

"On the second excursion Captain May said, 'Those girls must like you two. I offered to leave you stranded on the other side but they told me to bring you over.' We assured him that it was not a case of affection, and that Ella and Betty hadn't allowed us to be marooned because Allen was carrying the day's meals and the Supreme Commander had the cook-kit.

"After taking leave of the skipper, who charged 50 cents per passenger, we passed through the tiny village of Bolton and followed a gravel road northward.... two and one half miles to Bolton Lodge .... reached the summit of Bolton Mountain. Here we had magnificent views both of Camel's Hump to the south and Mansfield to the north.

"Puffer Lodge had not yet been completed. The shelter was just a shell, having four walls, a roof and a door. The place lacked a floor, bunks, a stove, and windows.... We decided we would make the best of things as we did not relish the thought of hiking by night..... Our stay at Puffer was actually one of the most pleasant and memorable of the trip. We were able to cook much more quickly over the open fire than we ever had on the wood stoves, insect life was at a minimum, the weather was perfect, and the views of Nebraska Notch below and Mt. Mansfield beyond were excellent.

"Sunday, July 1. The day was beautiful and a bright sun shone down on us as we dropped down slightly then came up over Mt. Mayo into the Mayo-Clark Col. Crossing the col we skirted one side of Mt. Clark and descended into Nebraska Notch to Taylor Lodge. This was the plushiest lodge we had encountered and we stopped there for lunch. On to Butler Lodge. There were a number of day hikers here, but by evening they had all disappeared.

"Monday, July 2. The dawn was exquisite, and the sky stayed clear until nearly 7:30 A.M. when a heavy fog rolled in to stay. Undaunted by the weather, we climbed upward from Butler, passing through a formation known as the Needle's Eye.

"From this point to the summit the Trail was a thing of joy and delight to any mountaineer's heart. The Trail was immeasurably steep and rugged, passing over and around massive rocks and climbing ever heavenward. Ladders and cables have been provided to help the climber up the cliffs and along the ledges. All the while we climbed, a gale blew from the west, and condensation from the fog matted our hair and soaked our clothing. The sensation was one of excitement and exhilaration, and every few seconds one of us would pause to exclaim, 'Boy! Isn't this the most?'

"As we followed Mansfield's razor-back ridge the wind increased in intensity and the fog became so thick that it became hard to see from one blaze to the next. Soon the Trail dropped sharply downward and finally became a stream where we had to slosh along. At noon we arrived at Taft Lodge .... as the fog had not lifted, we holed up for the remainder of the day.

"Tuesday, July 3. The day dawned beautifully and in contrast to the zero visibility of Monday, miles of Vermont countryside could be discerned from our window....

"We determined to leave our packs at the shelter and do as much of the Mansfield summit trails as we could during the day. There was much to be seen. (The Supreme Commander wrote the Governor a letter asking him to have Mt. Mansfield towed to Patapsco Park so that it would be more easily accessible to MCM'ers, but has received no reply as yet.)

"That night we were treated to a fireworks display which was given at Barnes Camp in the Notch below .... Our attention was occasionally diverted from the display by the antics of the porkies in the front yard. We were surrounded by them.

"Wednesday, July 4. Reluctantly we left Taft Lodge and started out on the last leg of our journey. .... by nine o'clock we came to the highway at Barnes Camp. This should be the end of our story. But, isn't that an interesting mountain ahead? So it was decided.

".... We had left quite a store of canned meats, fruits and vegetables in the car in case of emergency. This was not an emergency but it developed into one of the most exciting episodes of the trip .... we packed the newly selected foods in our knapsacks, .... we drove to Smuggler's Cave. Here we parked the wagon. .... we departed in haste for Sterling Pond Lodge.

"Thursday, July 5. By nine o'clock we had reached the car and soon were on our way back to the wicked city ....

"Perhaps I have placed too much stress upon the weather, the maddeningly irritating insects, and the other torments we experienced. Actually, when I think back over the days on the Trail, these minor discomforts seldom invade my thoughts. Instead, I remember Vermont's beautiful green scenery, its rugged trails and its challenging mountains. ....

Most of all, I remember the feeling of freedom and tranquility that came to us as we isolated ourselves from the complexities of modern living. This was indeed independence."

"CHARLES G. CLARKE"
