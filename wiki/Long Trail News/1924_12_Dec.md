THE GREEN MOUNTAIN NEWS
Published by The Green Mountain Club, Inc.
Brandon, Vermont, December, 1924

NOTICE.

The annual meeting of the Green Mountain Club will be held at the Hotel Berwick, Rutland, Vt. Saturday, January 10, 1925, at 11 a. m. There will also be afternoon and evening sessions. This is a meeting open to all members of the club, including members of the sections. An outing program will be arranged for Sunday, for those who can stay over, with headquarters at the clubhouse.

Proposed Amendments.

The following amendments have been proposed, and will be voted on at the annual meeting:
1. Increasing dues of members at large to $3 annually.
2. Increasing life membership dues to $50, and providing that all such dues paid to sections shall be turned over to the general treasurer.
3. Limiting members of sections in Vermont to those residing not more than ten miles from the town or city hall of the town or city in which the section is organized.

Special Meeting of the Trustees.

A special meeting of the trustees was held Oct. 18 at the clubhouse, the following trustees being present: J. Walter Mueller, New York, Fred H. Tucker, Boston, J. A. Allis of New York, as proxy for Prof. W. S. Monroe, and the following from Vermont: G. A. Mathers Bennington, C. P. Cooper, C. J. Cleveland, W. M. Ross, Rutland, T. S. Dean, Burlington, Profs. R. H. White and P. C. Voter, Middlebury, E. S. Marsh, Brandon, Mortimer R. Proctor, Proctor, and Herbert W. Congdon, Arlington. For the sake of privacy the afternoon meeting was held at Mr. Proctor's house on Pico Pond. After listening to a report from the house committee and suggestions from the president, it was voted to proceed at once with the building of an addition to the clubhouse to contain an office to be used also as a sleeping room for the caretaker during the winter. Plans for this addition were presented by Mr. Thayer, architect of the clubhouse, who was present. Cost not to exceed $550. The need of such a room has been felt very acutely during the past season. Here will be gathered together various necessities and conveniencies which have heretofore been scattered about the building, with someone in charge to receive guests, keep books, give information, etc.

The meeting was continued at the clubhouse in the evening. A resolution recommending raising the dues of members-at-large to $3, and life membership to $50, and that all life membership fees received hereafter be deposited in the general treasury, was passed, and these propositions will be voted on at the annual meeting in January. Many other matters were discussed, but no action of importance taken.

Most of the trustees spent the night at the clubhouse, and decided to put in what time they had Sunday in the neighborhood. Some visited the caves or climbed Deer's Leap, while others cut a short trail from the clubhouse to "Adirondack View" a point where a view can be had of the Adirondack mountains to the west. After dinner they departed, well pleased with the good meals and sleeping quarters provided by Mr. and Mrs. Lester.

A bronze tablet which had been placed in the clubhouse commemorating the fact that the house was the gift of Mortimer R. Proctor and the furnishing the gift of his mother, Mrs. Fletcher D. Proctor, has been removed, in deference to the wishes of Mr. Proctor.

The membership-at-large and that of the Killington section of Rutland, have risen to the highest figures on record, as a result of the attractions of the new clubhouse.

Work on The Trail.

Willis M. Ross, chairman of the trail committee, reports that Killington section of Rutland has built an open camp in Mt. Tabor with a rustic fireplace in front, at what is called the "old job", where the village of Griffith once stood. This camp is about 5 miles from Lake Griffith on the south and 3 miles from the camp in an old charcoal kiln toward the north. This section also has put in a pipe which supplies running water at the camp under Deer's Leap.

The entire trail has been gone over, blazes newly painted, and is considered in good walkable condition, with camps at convenient distances.

The Marjorie Fulburd trail, Johnson to Belvidere Mountain, 22 miles, is now finished, blazes having been entirely painted. However, some further improvements will be made, such as straightening some portions, and providing shelters. A new camp has been built just below Devil's Gap at Bitterbush Pond, about 6 miles from the summit of Belvidere.

About 8 miles of the trail from Belvidere Mountain to Jay Peak, as far as Hazen's Notch, has been completed by the trail committee. The balance is being done by the Jay Peak section, and of this about two-thirds has been cut out and blazed. Blazes have been painted for about three miles south of Jay Peak. About 10 miles farther has been cut out and blazed, and two miles still farther scouted and marked. Which leaves only about two miles unexplored.

Professor Monroe spent the summer months as usual at Montclair Glen Lodge, and with the assistance of many members of the New York section and of some from the Burlington section, the Monroe Skyline trail was put into A-1 condition. One new camp, called the Theron Dean Shelter on Stark Mountain, was built, and is said to be one of the best camps on the trail.

The Clubhouse.

Under the able management of the house committee, George N. Harman, chairman, and with Mr. and Mrs. Chas. H. Lester in charge, achieved great popularity and a fair measure of financial success. Mr. Harman's report to the trustees showed a balance of over $1500, after the payment of running expenses, and after paying bills for furnishings and improvements to real estate not properly chargeable to expense account. This surplus, however, will be diminished during the winter, when expenses exceed receipts. For a first season, with the trustees and house committee without experience in that line of business and doubtful as to just what policy should be adopted in the management, the showing is a very creditable one. Some changes in the details of operation will be made next year, and with the new office and manager in charge much more agreeable conditions in some respects than has been the case hitherto will prevail. One complaint that has been heard frequently has been slow service at meals due to the large numbers of patrons, more at times than could be served without delay. Measures will be taken to remedy this defect so far as possible.

Much has been done in the way of permanent improvement since the opening, largely thru the generosity of Mr. Mortimer Proctor. Three comfortable cabins for the help have been built. Also a garage accommodating 12 cars. Other outbuildings are an ice house, a woodshed and tool house, and an engine house. The grounds, as well as the house and outbuildings, are well lighted by electricity, a larger engine having been installed to take care of the increased load.

On the whole, the Club is to be congratulated on its attractive clubhouse and successful first season.

Mellinger E. Henry, in an article in the Rutland Herald, says that he tramped from Brandon to Sucker Brook lodge, a distance of 15 miles, a good day's work. He says he found no water on the trail till he reached the camp, a fact which should be noted by intending hikers. There was plenty of water on the old trail, but since it has been relocated into a skyline trail, it seems to have gone dry. Mr. Henry claims to be the champion heavy weight hiker, weighing 220 pounds. We should be glad to hear from anyone who is inclined to dispute this claim.

A Farm Boy's Inspiration.
From the Decatur (Ill.) Herald.

We can guess what inspired President Coolidge's announcement of a committee to work out an out-door recreation policy. He received, as did we, notice of his dues as a member of the Green Mountain club.

With the notice that $2.00 would be acceptable came the little newspaper, "The Green Mountain News" organ of the club an association that has absolutely no commercial aim in its charter, and merely seeks to make an extraordinarily beautiful part of America available to decent people who want the solitudes of the open spaces.

With the paper is a map showing the progress made in projecting foot trails over those distant green hills and down in one corner is a dot labeled "birthplace of President Coolidge." It follows then that President Coolidge looked at the map, suffered a slight pang of homesickness as he pictured the hills around Plymouth and called in his secretaries to "devise a plan for increasing the physical vigor, moral strength and clean simplicity of mind of the American people."

Plymouth, where Calvin Coolidge was born and where he was when the summons came to the leadership of the American nation, is not 10 miles across country from Herb Haley's farm where you take the trail for Killington peak, ascending for hours beside a murmuring trout stream. From the top of Killington you can see Champlain on one side and on the other the White Mountains 50 miles away. Old Mansfield looms in the distance, the seeming end of the chain of hills. You plunge down off Killington and walk for miles through the forest seeing nothing but the pine trees and the trail ahead until you come to that terrace where the trees have been cut away to frame the picture of White Face mountain. You pause to drink in the beauty of it. A little farther on, and you pass Pico and then come to Sherburne pass which dips down to Rutland.

And we can imagine a tired and harassed man looking up from a desk and out across the mall, forgetting a fidgety Congress up the street, and seeing not the hurried traffic of automobiles, or hovering airplanes or even the monument, or the Lincoln memorial, but instead a winding footpath leading to the summit of heaven kissing hills, swept eternally by breezes and distant valleys with quiet farms and the glint of waters—and seeing resolving that he will do his best to get America out of doors into God's free air and sunlight for the souls good of America.

BOOK NOTICES.

The following volumes have been received this year, and added to the Club's library:

The South American Tour: A Descriptive Guide. By Annie S. Peck. New and revised edition. New York, George H. Doran Co., 1924.

Profusely illustrated from photographs. Valuable as a guide to those visiting South America, and interesting to anyone who desires to acquire knowledge of those countries. Miss Peck has had much experience in South American travel, and is a famous mountain climber. The frontispiece is a view of Mt. Huascaran Peru, 21,812 feet high, and the statement is made that Miss Peck ascended this peak in 1908, "the highest point on this hemisphere yet attained (1924) by any North or South American."

Games and Recreational Methods for Clubs, Camps and Scouts. By Charles F. Smith. New York, Dodd, Mead & Co., 1924.

Many illustrations. An excellent book for those directing recreational work in camps. Contains chapters also on trees, fire and fire-making, hike cooking and knots, hitches and bends.

Camp Grub. By Elon Jessup. New York, E. P. Dutton & Co., 1924. Illustrated.

A store of valuable information about camp diet, what to cook and how to cook it, and related subjects. Some good recipes are given. We have tried the one for baked-bean loaf on p. 89, with excellent results.

Down the Grand Canyon. By Lewis R. Freeman. New York, Dodd, Mead & Co., 1924.

Many fine illustrations from photographs taken by the author. A detailed story of the expedition of the U. S. Geological Survey thru that world wonder, the Grand Canyon of the Colorado. Well written and interesting.

Shank's Mare. By Charles C. Stoddard.
New York, George H. Doran Co., 1924.

   An entertaining book on the pleasures of tramping, a melange of information, anecdote and genial philosophy. The author is bitter against automobiles, and says that the "automobilist goes far toward making obsolete this fine sport of walking. He has all but spoiled the highways for the pedestrian by his insane thirst for speed, and is only exceeded as a nuisance by the motorcyclist." His walks seem to have been mostly in the highways, with an occasional ramble through fields and woods. We suggest that he try the Long Trail. No automobiles will get him there. They are strictly forbidden, and any motorist caught trying to navigate his devil-wagon on the trail will receive short shrift.

Ski-ing for Beginners. By Arnold Lunn.
New York, E. P. Dutton & Co., 1924. Illustrated.

   This manual, written by a resident of Switzerland, gives detailed instructions in the technique of this fascinating sport, all that are necessary, if carefully followed, to turn the novice into an expert.

The Call of The Trail.
By LEW EVANS.

They've inveigled me back, and they've taken my pack;
   They've encircled my neck with a collar;
With my bald plateau in a modish chapeau
   I trail the elusive dollar.
For the spell of the town has settled me down
   To the grind of the old treadmill,
But the days and the nights on the mountain heights!
   Their spell is upon me still.
Now I've panted and pawed at a mountain's facade
   Till my hobo-nails began to perspire
And wet and profane in a mist or a rain
   I've shivered a draft for the fire.
With a thirty-pound pack on a desk-ruined back,
   I've skidded on slippery rocks
I've twisted my pins, put dents in my shins,
   Kicked porcupine quills through my socks.
But I've knelt at her shrine, been warmed by her wine,
   And kissed by her quickening chill;
I'm a faithful knight of the Mountain Sprite
   And her spell is upon me still.
So never you weep, Green Mountain Bo-Peep,
   Like the sheep, next summer will find me
Beating it back by the Long Trail track,
   A-trotting my pack behind me.

Jay Peak Day.

   September 6 was a rainy day, but upwards of 200 people gathered at the foot of Jay Peak and resolved that the Jay Peak section of the G. M. C. should and would put through that portion of the Long Trail from Hazen's Notch to Jay Peak, a distance of about 17 miles. The slogan unanimously adopted was "Jay Peak or Bust." Speakers were F. B. Hammond, President of the Jay Peak section, of North Troy; Judge C. P. Cowles of Burlington, Curtis S. Emery of Newport, and James P. Taylor of Burlington. Since then considerable headway has been made on this part of the trail. For details see report of the trail committee in this issue.

Notes.

   An excellent article on the trail, the Green Mountain Club and Vermont in general, by Walter H. Sears, was published in "The Open Road." This has been reprinted by the Club, and will be sent to members in due course. Anyone who wants a copy sooner than that can be accommodated by applying to the treasurer, or to T. S. Dean, Masonic Temple, Burlington, Vt.

   Capt. Herbert Wheaton Congdon of Arlington, Vt., has surveyed and mapped the Marjorie Hulburd trail from the junction of the new and old trails on Whiteface to Frying Pan Brook a distance of 22.8 miles. The highest elevation on this route is 2890 feet.

   Hunters during the recent open season have made use of the Long Trail for stalking game, and have been liberal patrons of the cabins. Many bucks have been killed in the vicinity of the trail, and in Chittenden and Goshen several large black bears.

   The U. S. Geological Survey completed levelling to the summit of Mt. Mansfield last summer, and found the elevation of the Chin to be 4393 feet above sea level.

   The Killington section (Rutland) will keep open house at the Green Mountain Clubhouse all day January 1, and will hold their annual meeting at 2 p.m.
