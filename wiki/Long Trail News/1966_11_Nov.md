LONG TRAIL PATROL

During the first week, beginning June 1, as reported in the last issue of the LTN, the Long Trail was speed-cleared from Ithiel Falls to Frying Pan Brook. Then Montclair Glen was extensively repaired and next the Patrol built a log, open-front shelter at the site of old Theron Dean shelter. The New York Section paid for this work. This was completed on July 2. Then they moved north where they found many blow-downs. Between Frying Pan Brook and Hazen's Notch they cut out 174 trees down in the trail. The Long Trail was then standardized from Hazen's Notch to Canada. This was finished by the first of August.

About Aug. 1 Cooper Lodge's roof was repaired, and the table and bunks patched up where vandals had put two holes in the roof and destroyed part of the table and some of the bunks. No attempt was made to close the window openings except for hen fence to keep porkies out.

During the second week in Aug. a new frame, open-front shelter was built near the cascades in Duck Brook on the relocated trail about 1½ miles north of Jonesville. Burlington Section paid for this shelter.

An open-front frame shelter was then built south along a brook locally known as Jimmie Deane Brook, to replace Noyes Pond Shelter. It is, I believe, about ¾ of a mile south of the old Noyes Pond shelter and is on land belonging to Mr. Harvey Burch who has a beautiful home near Noyes Pond. He was very helpful to the trail gang.

After this job was done, the Patrol moved up north again where they painted blazes on most of the trail that they maintain between Ithiel Falls and Canada.

I'm sorry that this report looks so lopsided and tells so little about black flies and porkies and vandals but I'm agin them all. And I'm not sorry enough about all this to rewrite it all.

The Scout Reservation where we were is a pleasant area, some 1000 acres in all, with a central grass-covered plaza kept clear of campers, recreation and administration buildings suitably rustic and weather-worn, a small building where the Buchanans maintained Headquarters, a camping area in front of them, three camping areas fanning out from the road leading farther into the camp, in pine woods, on the slopes toward the lake, with cookhouse and pit toilets in each. BURLINGTON SECTION

The Burlington Section had its usual active summer, with hikes nearly every weekend. Tops in attendance were the Intersectional, and the swimming, boating, and hiking (Moosalamoo) outing at Marion Holmes' camp on Lake Dunmore, each of which attracted 50 people. Trips involving water sports seem to be quite popular, as Madeline Miles had seventeen on her "canoeing, swimming, eating and bird-watching" day at Green Mt. Reservoir.

There were two out-of-state hikes this summer, with Larry Dean leading a group up Mt. Lafayette in New Hampshire in July, and a joint canoeing-hiking weekend in the Adirondacks led by George Saunders and Pete Eberhardt.

GMCers who are frequently in the Mansfield area will be happy to hear that almost all of the side trails, in addition to the Long Trail from Rt. 2 to Smugglers Notch, have been cleared and blazed this summer. Response from newspaper announcements was good, and many non-GMC members helped us launch the massive weekend assault on most of the thirty-six (!) side trails. Roy Buchanan was host to thirteen of the workers at Ranch Camp that Saturday night.

Jim Forrester spent a good part of his vacation working on Taylor Lodge, and work trips have been made to Taft, Butler, and Puffer, so Burlington Section Cabins should be in good shape for the winter. By the time this issue of the Long Trail News appears the dedication of the new shelter at Duck Brook will be history. Our thanks to the Long Trail Patrol and the three generations of Buchanans who built the shelter.

Congratulations to Newell Briggs of Essex Junction, our latest End-to-Ender. Many of us have hiked carrying one infant, but not many couples have taken two on the trail at once, as have the Briggs.

Our hike schedule is as follows Nov. 6, Taft Lodge; Nov. 19, Harvest Party; Dec. 3, Moonlight hike at former Ft. Ethan Allen; Dec. 11, Snake Mtn.; Jan. 7-8, Ranch Camp Overnight; Jan. 14, ANNUAL MEETING (for reservations, contact Helen Farrington, 802-863-2088); Jan. 15, Camp Hoot Owl N. Duxbury; Jan. 28, Fifteenth Annual Oyster Stew Supper; Feb. 5, Skiing and Snowshoeing—Jay Peak; Feb. 18, Ritterbush Pond.

JAN ABBOTT, Reporter

KILLINGTON SECTION

Our Section has continued with the traditional "hike every two weeks." We've been doing it since 1938 and can't stop now. Bill Gragen, Kevin Hogan, and Churchill Scott are speeding along toward their End-to-Ender goals, so we have scheduled several trips a long way from home. They report many people traveling the Trail this year.

This past season has been an unusually productive one for Trail and Shelter improvements. The Ogdens, Jr. and Sr., have placed signs at all our shelters and at many places along the Trail. Almost every camp has had extensive repairs. At Governor Clement, the stone chimney has been rebuilt and hardware cloth stretched on the bunk.

Another very active group in maintenance work has been the people from the Farm and Wilderness Camps. Too bad we can't find some way to recognize the work some "campers" are doing for us all.

CRAIGUE PERKINS, Reporter

MONTPELIER SECTION

Our summer activities began with a hike up Camel's Hump and on to Jonesville via the Long Trail on June 26th. The temperature was over 90°. It was a long, hot hike! Dave Morse was the leader and Dave Otis and I the followers.

A somewhat larger group led by Dick Babcock climbed Carrigain in the White Mts. on July 10th, and larger yet was the group which gathered at the Desilet's Camp at Groton Pond on July 24th for a chicken barbecue and an afternoon of swimming, boating and water skiing.

August 7th found 20 hikers, led by George Brady and Cynthia Mix, hiking up Spruce Mt. in Plainfield to visit forest ranger Neil Rogers and his family. Actually the hikers arrived in shifts. On August 21st seven members led by Dave Otis enjoyed a hike on top of Mt. Mansfield (they drove up) hiking the Lake View Trail loop and the Long Trail to the Chin and back.

Ten Montpelierites attended the Intersectional. Gene Bamforth, Doris Washburn and I stayed the entire week. Although Doris and I packed up and went to her brother's Sunday night, we didn't leave the neighborhood until early Monday morning. On the way home we hiked the Long Trail from Brandon Gap to Carmel Mt. and down the New Boston Trail to Chittenden or thereabouts. The road leading to the New Boston Trail was so badly washed out, we could only drive in part way.

On Saturday, Sept. 10th, eight of us, led by myself, hiked up Scragg Mt. in Waitsfield, and on Sept. 18th the same number, with Dave Morse leading, hiked up Carter Dome in the White Mts. And that completes our activities as of this writing.

Scheduled for this weekend is a hike from Appalachian Gap to Burnt Rock Mountain, (which, if all goes well, will complete the Long Trail for me) and also a shorter hike up the Hedgehog Brook Trail to the Long Trail and Burnt Rock.

MARION GORHAM, Reporter

PIONEER VALLEY SECTION

Our first summer as a Section was fun-filled and successful, with good attendance at our hikes and a steadily growing membership. Many of us had never belonged to a Section before and it is proving to be a new and rewarding experience.

There is some talk of a snowshoe hike and also a work party to Deer View Shelter in the late fall or early winter. We are hoping for a good attendance at our first annual meeting sometime in January. This will be a good time to meet fellow members and get in on the planning for our next year's schedule of activities.

See you on the Trail....

WILDLANDS PROFILE STRIP

The Green Mountains are important to Vermont—they offer wilderness and scenic beauty, as well as quality recreational opportunities. Whether one skis, one hikes, one sits and looks, or one drives along our Vermont roads and looks up and over at them—they are an important asset of the "Beckoning Country"—and they are also important to Vermonters themselves.

Changes are taking place rapidly and the wilderness character of the mountains is continually yielding to new developments—housing, skiing, resort areas, etc. For example, just this year in the mountains between the Winooski River and Canada, two new extensive ski resort developments are underway and expansion of two other ski areas is being planned.

It's true—all these changes have economic importance to the State. However, mountain wilderness plays an important role in Vermont's recreational plan. How much emphasis is being placed on the importance of preserving some of this? What is the State's policy for its lands at the high elevations? Is there a definite long-range plan for permanently preserving some mountain wilderness for future generations to enjoy?

For nearly a year a group of individuals from various fields of interest have been meeting together in an effort to arrive at some constructive plan which would protect and preserve some of our choice mountain highlands. The concept of a "Wildlands Profile Strip" has evolved, based on ecological studies which indicate the value of preserving as natural areas the land above 2500'. Above this elevation, vegetative growth is slow and easily destroyed; lumbering is unprofitable.

The plan calls for the creation of a "Wildlands Profile Strip" to run the entire length of the State, following the high elevations of the Green Mountains. This strip would encompass the mountain top areas above 2500' elevation, such as Mt. Mansfield and Camel's Hump, and would include land on the ridges and in the gaps needed to connect these high points.

All future encroachments which would destroy the mountain wilderness and disturb the ecological balances within these areas would be prohibited. Specifically prohibited would be the erection of buildings, towers, roads, and any practices which would destroy the wilderness character of the mountains. Additional ski developments would definitely be discouraged. Lumbering would not be a general practice but would be limited to selective cutting so as not to alter the ridgeline or disturb the wilderness aspect of the area. There would be no cutting above 2500' except in these few areas where restricted cutting is feasible and such would not upset the balances which provide for conservation of soil and water.

Governmental and commercial communication facilities would be permitted only after a careful review by the Forests & Parks Board and the Interagency Committee on Natural Resources indicates there is no alternative. Building specifications would be outlined so that any necessary construction would detract as little as possible from the character of the wildlands area. It would also be desirable to take measures to ensure that existing facilities are adequately screened and blend in with the natural environment.

Such a "Wildlands Profile Strip" would provide for the preservation of the Long Trail, and wherever possible at least a quarter of a mile in width on either side of the Trail would be maintained in as undisturbed condition as possible.

The implementation of the plan would be a cooperation venture, encouraging private land owners especially large forest-based companies, to participate and cooperate in adopting policies consistent with the overall concept of the "Wildlands Profile Strip." The State should take the initiative in "filling in" the Strip by means of land purchase, agreements with private land owners, acceptance of gifts, easements and rights-of-way.

Such a plan would preserve: 1) the unique natural areas on the mountain tops, such as the alpine tundra on Mansfield; 2) a prime wildland area needed by so many people today; 3) an area which would serve as a valuable outdoor classroom for educational and scientific purposes; 4) the historic Long Trail. It would encompass all these, together with other recreational opportunities and such historic points as Smuggler's Notch, Hazen's Notch, and Webster's Monument. Due to the nature of the Green Mountains and their central location, this "Wildlands Profile Strip" would be easily available to the people by means of an extensive trail system, roads through the mountains and ski lifts.

This plan was submitted in June to the Department of Forests & Parks for consideration, since that department administers the largest amount of state land. It is hoped that this department will adopt the plan and set aside immediately all lands above 2500' in the Green Mountains which it now administers and preserve them in their natural state. In so doing, the department would contribute significantly to the preservation of the wilderness character and the profile of the mountains. Further, it would be an initial step in implementing the "Wildlands Profile Strip" and offer encouragement to groups, both private and state, to participate and cooperate in the achievement of such a strip in its entirety. Hopefully, also, the State will adopt this concept as a long-range plan.

The "Wildlands Profile Strip" would be truly a unique and appealing feature of our Green Mountain State and would fulfill the ever increasing need of people for wildlands areas.

If you wish to lend your support to such a plan, send a letter to the Commissioner of Forests & Parks, Robert B. Williams, in Montpelier, Vermont. Your support would be appreciated.

NEW SHELTER AT GRIFFITH LAKE

The United States Forest Service has added another shelter to the Long Trail!
This shelter, of the standard USFS frame lean-to design, has been constructed on the east shore of Griffith Lake, 1000 feet south of the 3-way junction at the north end of the lake.
Per the note on Page 49 of the 1966 Guide Book, this shelter will be called Griffith Lake Shelter. The former Griffith Shelter has been renamed Peru Peak Shelter.
Our thanks to Paul Newcomb, Forest Supervisor; Robert Deemer, our new USFS Trustee; Sidney Davis, District Ranger; and the USFS staff and shelter crews for this fine addition to our Long Trail shelter chain.
This gives us THIRTY shelters between Massachusetts and Sherburne Pass, all of them vitally needed due to the increasing number of hikers walking this southern portion of our Footpath in the Wilderness.

BEN ROLSTON

PERRY MERRILL RETIRES

Out-of-state GMCers should know that Perry Merrill has retired as Vermont Commissioner of Forests and Parks and is running for the office of Lieutenant-Governor of the State of Vermont. Under Mr. Merrill's competent administration, tremendous strides have been made in the development of Vermont's forests and parks.
A former trustee of the GMC, Mr. Merrill has always maintained his membership and interest in the Club and its activities. Mr. & Mrs. Merrill attended our Annual Meeting last year as special guests, and two years ago, Mr. Merrill served as our annual meeting speaker.
Replacing Mr. Merrill as Commissioner of Forests and Parks is Mr. Robert Williams, former Deputy Commissioner.

OUT ON THE TRAIL

FROM WHENCE COMETH HIKERS?

I am sure that all of you readers are aware of the increasing number of hikers walking our Long Trail System. And you know that they come from many places. You've met not only Vermonters on the Trail, but hikers from Massachusetts, and New York, and Connecticut, and a few other states. And maybe someone from Canada, or even a foreign country.
