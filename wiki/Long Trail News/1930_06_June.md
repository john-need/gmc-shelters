Two Long Trail Weeks This Year.

Last year's Long Trail Week was such a success that this summer there will be two such trips, as previously announced. They are so arranged that members of Party A may continue as members of Party B, thus having two successive weeks on the Trail. Plans are also offered for those members of Party B who want to go on for a second week, independent of a guided party.

Party A will assemble in Rutland on Saturday, August 16, and proceed to the newly enlarged Long Trail Lodge. Those arriving in their own cars will drive direct to the Lodge; those arriving by sleeper from New York or Boston will be taken there by Rutland members. Walks will be taken to Pico, Killington, and Carmel as last year, alternated with days of rest which may be used either for motor trips to interesting points or for short unguided walks. Wednesday morning the party will be moved to Prof. Monroe's Couching Lion Farm, a beautiful auto trip of about 75 miles, stopping for lunch in Montpelier, and taking from the Farm the short 3-mile hike to the "tin huts" on top of the mountain where the caretaker will provide supper and breakfast (plain camp fare) and the party will sleep under blankets in true camp style, two to a bunk. Friday morning they will hike the beautiful new trail six miles down to the Winooski River, where the cars will take them to Lance's "Lodge in Smuggler's Notch" for the good supper always to be found there, and where they will sleep in comfortable electric-lighted tents with good beds. Saturday they can go up Mansfield by the bus at 10 a. m., lunch at the hotel on top and come back on foot by the Hazleton Trail, 2½ miles, to Barnes Camp, where cars will take them out to Stowe and Waterbury in time for the sleeper that night, or they may linger at Barnes Camp and go out by the Sunday sleeper. Those who are going on with Party B will remain

at the Mt. Mansfield hotel at the summit, which is reached by Party B for lunch on Saturday.

It is estimated that the cost will be the same as last year, $55 including everything, from arrival at Long Trail Lodge to delivery to trains in Waterbury.

No packs will be carried save something in which lunch may be carried on certain days, sweater for wear on mountain-tops, etc. You will sleep in your clothes in the tin huts on Couching Lion (Camel's Hump). Those preferring to omit this experience may stay at Prof. Monroe's and walk out to the road where the cars taking the more strenuous will pick them up and take them to Lance's.

Party B will assemble in Burlington via sleepers from New York or Boston on Saturday morning, August 23, and proceed by motor to the "Halfway House" up the Underhill Valley. Those with their own cars will drive there direct. From Halfway House they will hike up less than 2 miles to the Mt. Mansfield House on the summit, for lunch; both parties will be at this meal so there ought to be quite a jollification. The night will be spent at this hotel and on Sunday the party, carrying light packs, will hike to Taft Lodge via the Subway (a curious rock formation) and the Lake of the Clouds, where they will lunch; a total distance of only two miles but packed full of wonderful beauty.

Three nights will be spent at Taft Lodge; the accomodations here are rough but good. Sleeping will be in wide bunks, wide enough for four persons but we shall not have more than three in a bunk and probably fewer. Blankets will be provided by the caretaker who will also prepare meals, plain but substantial. The Lodge itself, a log cabin with a rarely beautiful outlook, is the home of many happy memories for those who have visited it. The trail down to Webster Camp we found both tedious and arduous, partly because of the flies and the heat, and also because the footing was so bad, for I think we were the first ones to go over it since it had been cut. Stratton Pond we admired for its beauty, and congratulated the Worcester section on its taste in locations for new camps, but we spent that night in Swezey Camp, a shelter which we should have scorned except for a thunder shower, and where we killed with our hatchet the first of our seven porcupines.

In coming to this camp from Bourge Pond we followed the old logging railroad bed, and crossed fearsome trestles on a single log, which the packs on our backs made something of a feat. May I request that the detour around these trestles be marked if it hasn't been? I understand there is one. At Manchester Depot we went out for supplies and a fine meal at the Kelley Homestead, and on the top of Prospect Rock spent a night of full moon, with the lights of those beautiful valleys below us. It seemed a pity to sleep, but we did.

The trail from Mad Tom Camp (where a deer jumped across between us as we lay in the ruts of the abandoned road) to Buffum Pond, almost sent us home to mother, for weaving a pattern over and under fallen timber with a pack is no way to spend a summer holiday, and Styles Peak was a nightmare, but a swim in the pond and the lovely road down to Griffith Camp cheered us considerably, and the views between here and Clarendon made up even for the difficulty we had in finding our way across the meadows between the East Wallingford and Gulf roads. Contributing factors to the restoration of our senses of humor were Buffum Camp, the raspberries on Button Hill, and a swim in Shrewsbury Pond.

At East Clarendon we went out for mail and were sorry to find Roskenney Farm closed, especially after we made the acquaintance of Mr. Farrell at the postoffice. On Killington we slept in the "tin tent" on account of rain, and Long Trail Lodge and Mrs. Bates looked very good to us when we splashed down to them the next day—not to mention the pantry shelf, for we were

first night in an open field about a mile south of the county road. It was in situations of this sort that we blessed our biscuit, cheese, and dried prunes, for they provided a thoroughly satisfactory waterless meal, and although we used them every day for lunch, we considered that meal always our most tasty repast. The trail to the county road we found rather obscurely marked in places, and we followed the W.O.C. signs by faith rather than because the guide book mentioned them. The map of this section, as of all the sections, is of course useless as far as topography is concerned, and we were always glad of our government maps, even though they took up so much room.

From the county road we found the trail very clearly marked and in good condition throughout the Bennington section. The black flies were bad just here, and the midges frightful, so that we slept with our heads tied up in bandannas and buried in our sleeping bags, and awoke every morning feeling like boiled owls and convinced that sleeping under the stars was no fresh air cure.
