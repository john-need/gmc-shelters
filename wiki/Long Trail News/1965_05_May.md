The Lung Trail News

ANOTHER MOUNTAIN RUINED?

Mount Greylock, 3491 ft., the highest mountain in Massachusetts and one of the most interesting and beautiful points on the central section of the Appalachian Trail is scheduled to be transformed into just another commercial resort. The Mt. Greylock Tramway Authority, originally conceived for the single purpose of installing a simple passenger tramway to the summit of Mount Greylock has now announced a massive development to complement the authorized tramway. The maps of the planned development show, in addition to the "world's largest" tramway, six chairlifts, more than 11 miles of 100 foot-wide ski trails, restaurant, cocktail lounge, ski shops, swimming pool, terminal stations "international shopping center," golf course, motor inn riding stables, skating rink and huge parking lots at the base. In other words, it is quite obvious that the plan calls for transforming another of our virtually unspoiled mountains into just another honky-tonk area.

Where is this process going to stop? When are our elected officials going to put a halt to this give-away of our beloved mountains? When are they going to realize that we must begin now to conserve for the future the remaining wilderness and virtually unspoiled areas that we have taken for granted in the past?

It is still NOT TOO LATE to save Mount Greylock if we all pull together. Remember how the other hiking clubs in the country came to the aid of the Green Mountain Club several years ago and helped save the summit of Mt. Mansfield from despoliation. It was a massive and effective effort. Now it's our turn to help our hiking companions in Massachusetts. If you agree, please read on.

The bulldozers have been temporarily halted by a legal suit instituted by a volunteer group called the Mount Greylock Protective Association, and two bills have been introduced before the 1965 Massachusetts Legislature. The first of these bills would abolish the Mount Greylock Tramway Authority. The second bill would clearly redefine the purpose of the Mount Greylock Reservation which was originally established to preserve the mountain in its original unspoiled state, clearly define the duties and responsibilities of the Greylock Reservation Commission and specifically deny commercial development of the mountain. In addition, the Protective Association has offered to the Commission a positive, constructive program for increased use of the Reservation for recreational and educational purposes without injuring or destroying its basic character and beauty as a natural area. This proposed program encourages outdoor activities such as camping, hiking, picnicking, skiing, bird watching, and nature study. However, throughout the program, the fundamental point is emphasized that even developments aimed at encouraging increased use for these healthy and desirable outdoor activities must remain subordinate to the fundamental purpose of the reservation whose principal attraction is nondeveloped natural grandeur.

What can we do? If you are convinced, as I am, even as an ardent skier, that the original concept of the Mount Greylock Reservation has been grossly violated and that the original intention of a simple tramway has been used as a cover for a massive commercial exploitation of Mount Greylock, then sit down and collect your thoughts. Then pick up your pen and direct your own personal thoughts to either (a) your representative, if you are a citizen of Massachusetts, encouraging him to read carefully and consider the virtues of Senate bills S651 and S440 and House bills H2408 and H808 or (b) the Honorable John A. Volpe, Governor of Massachusetts. Many of you will fall in the latter category. The governor is under considerable pressure to encourage massive development of Greylock to promote tourism as an aid to local economy. To combat this pressure, you may wish to consider including the following economic thought in addition to purely conservation arguments, that is, if you agree. As a tourist or potential tourist to the Commonwealth of Massachusetts you will pay money to see unspoiled out-of-doors, rippling streams, wide vistas of verdant forest and to be able to hike on a peaceful undeveloped mountain. Conversely, you will not spend money to visit Massachusetts to see just another massive commercial development. By including this monetary approach I do not mean that you should de-emphasize your personal convictions regarding the need to preserve our remaining wilderness and virtually unspoiled mountains. What I do mean is that in addition to pleas for conservation also stress the dollar aspect; it may be unfortunate but it is a fact the dollar approach sometimes gets the message through when seemingly idealistic and future-looking conservation pleas are easily cast aside. Both approaches should be used if you agree that Greylock should be saved in its natural state, but most important of all sit down now to write your letter. A few years ago we worked together and saved Mansfield. We can do it again to save Greylock if we all get behind the effort. We must continue to hold the line.

NEW ENGLAND TRAIL CONFERENCE

A larger-than-ever group of trail enthusiasts assembled at the University of Massachusetts on March 27th for the twenty-ninth annual New England Trail Conference. Once again to our own John Vandell go the honors for creating a program of the usual high caliber for which these meetings are noted.

As we looked around the Hasbrouch Laboratory Auditorium, we saw that GMC'ers once again added greatly to the attendance. There were Section members. There were members-at-large. And special mention goes to the large delegation of GMC'ers from Springfield, Vermont.

The program suited those of all interests. There were trail reports from the constituent organizations. There were magnificent nature slides and movies. There was an extensive slide coverage of beautiful White Mountain scenes. And there were exciting movies of rock climbing which kept the adherents of the horizontal clinging to their chairs.

Our own Art Koerber MC'ed the panel discussion on trail maintenance. But due to the shortage of time, the panel members had only a fraction of the time originally allotted to them. So when Koerber sez you can talk for only seven minutes, you'd better finish your speech in six minutes and fifty-nine seconds, 'cause when his alarm clock rings—you stop!

It was a day of enjoyment—both in program and in seeing friends again. The losers were those who DIDN'T attend. See you at Amherst next spring.

BEN ROLSTON

THE 1965 INTERSECTIONAL

Remember our 1961 Intersectional at Camp Plymouth? And the 177 GMC'ers and guests who attended? And the hikes we took? And the visits to historical Plymouth? And the wee flakes of gold that our prospectors found? And the swimming, and singing, and slide showing, and just plain good socializing?

Well start making your plans for August 28th through September 6th, 'cause the GMC is going back to Camp Plymouth for another wonderful Intersectional!

You can bet that our committee will cook up some interesting activities for the 10-day event—hikes on the Long Trail, visits to nearby points of interest, some more searching for gold, and a well-rounded program of evening entertainment.

Information sheets will be distributed to all members attending the Annual Meeting, and to the rest of our Section and At-Large members shortly thereafter. YOUR attendance can add to OUR pleasure, so plan to be there!

LONG TRAIL PATROL

I have done nothing on the trails this winter and, much to my surprise, I learn that Mother Nature has laid down on her job too.

Several reports have come in from GMC trips saying that fewer windows are broken than usual.

A member of the Burlington Section has been doing much winter hiking and he reports that the trails and shelters show but little winter damage. Between January 10 and March 14 he has hiked just over 100 miles of trail. He now has only about 55 miles more to do to become a winter end-to-ender. He even sent me a snowy picture of the summit of Buchanan Mt. and reports that no one has jumped my claim to it. There can't be much gold up thar!

The worst trail trouble that he reports, in going from French Camp to Canada, is on Jay Peak. Here, between the Long Trail junction with the Jay Village trail and the summit, the ski development took over late last summer. The chair lift now goes to the summit, with extensive construction there. A service road follows the Long Trail westerly from the summit, then loops northerly and then easterly and recrosses the Trail enroute to the old summit station. Snow-cats are hauling cement and asphalt mixture to the temporary shelter on top."

ROY BUCHANAN

AT BILL REINTRODUCED

In May 1964, during the 88th Congress, Senator Gaylord Nelson of Wisconsin introduced a bill for the protection and promotion of the Appalachian Trail. The bill was co-sponsored by Senator Williams of New Jersey but unfortunately it was held in Committee.

On January 22nd, during the first session of the 89th Congress, Senator Nelson again introduced US Senate Bill S. 622, which begins as follows:

"Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled, That (a) in recognition of the public benefits already received from the establishment of the Appalachian Trail, extending generally along the Appalachian Mountains, from Maine to Georgia, for a distance of more than two thousand miles, and in order to promote and perfect the delineation, protection, and management of such trail, the cooperation of Federal, State, local, and private organizations and persons, for these purposes, is hereby declared to be in the public interest.

(b) In furtherance of these purposes, the Secretary of the Interior, the Secretary of Agriculture, the Secretary of Defense, and any other Federal officials who now or hereafter administer Federal properties traversed by the Appalachian Trail shall coordinate their efforts in providing uniform administration and protection of the trail; and they shall give encouragement to and cooperate with the States, local communities, and private organizations and persons in promoting the purposes of this Act."

In Sections 2, 3, and 4, the provisions of the Bill are further identified and explained. Anyone may obtain a copy of the Bill by writing to his own Senator or Senator Nelson.

This year some 15 Senators have co-sponsored S. 622. Identified by their States, the Senators who have signed this Bill are:

Coincidentally Vermont, Massachusetts and New York are "home grounds" for the Section of the Green Mountain Club. Are we GMC'ers not aware of this Bill? Are we not interested in this Bill? Or have we just been remiss in letting our Senators know how we feel? A letter or even a post card to your Senator indicating your personal interest in having him support S. 622 will help. Why not do it today.

BURLINGTON SECTION

Up The Trail Apiece

The 54th Annual Meeting of the Burlington Section, held on Jan. 16th at Hotel Vermont, brought out 150 folks. They came from near and far–the Ben Rolstons from Schenectady; the Chet Buchanans from Syracuse; the Bob Attenboroughs from East Hartford; the Myron Morrises from Sea Girt, N.J., and Lois MacKay from Montreal, besides "us-uns."
   We elected one of our section's most popular and hikingest young women as our section president for the ensuing year, Miss Shirley Strong. Past president Albert Wurthmann "left big shoes for Shirley to fill" but she will do it, wait and see.
   Jan. 23rd and 24th, with Joe Frank heading the event, we went to our leased camp in Ranch Valley up Smuggler's Notch way for snowshoeing and fun.
   Feb. 7th Fred and Flossy Eaton held a skating party.
   Then on Feb. 13th came the Section's 13th Annual Oyster Stew Supper at the James P. Taylor Lodge in Nebraska Notch on the Long Trail. 55 turned out for that one. Bev and Don Remick took good care of that event as they have done all these years. Important people of the press were there: Peg Pons, editor of the Long Trail News; Peter Miller, managing editor of the Vermont Skiing Magazine and Phyllis Meras of the New York Times. Though snow was not the best for snowshoeing the day and event were first class.
   Feb. 20th a group of us trekked the 3.1 miles up to Bolton Lodge in what was supposed to be a snowshoe hike but it was better walking for lack of snow.
   We found the best snowshoeing of the winter out from the Middlebury Snow Bowl when we followed the Middlebury "Puffers" for a day of snowshoeing on the Long Trail, March 7th. A damp snowfall the night before made the woodland way a fairyland and we were blessed with comfortable temperature. Seventeen made that trip.
   Six went to Glen Ellen with Andy Buchanan with hopes of skiing and snowshoeing on March 14 and they found conditions better than expected.
   We ran off a "two barrel" event on March 20–21. Harris Abbott headed a group of six to the Adirondacks and over all the high points of Algonquin. On the evening of the 20th, Wilda Gigee headed a party of 20 on a night hike on Brownell Mt. After the group of night prowlers returned from the mountain that evening, Wilda and her committee treated them at her home in town.
   On March 28th Rod Rice led a group of snowshoers in by Ritterbush Camp and up into Devil's Gulch on the Long Trail. Rod took over for Roy Buchanan who is at present doing a good job as nurse (unregistered or what have you) tending Helen who is recovering from a slight heart attack at their Westford home.
   We are now looking forward to our annual maple sugar-on-snow this coming Saturday April 3rd. If I don't eat too much, I'll "walk along with you" next time the News comes out.

LARRY DEAN, Reporter

MONTPELIER SECTION

On January 16th fifteen of us hiked up to Taylor Lodge from the Mt. Mansfield Trout Club for oyster stew prepared by Ann Otis and Doris Washburn. We started out on snowshoes but found them unnecessary so some of us shed them along the way. Before feasting, several of us hiked into Nebraska Notch to explore the "cave." Back at the Lodge it seemed warmer outside than inside and we found our stew cooled before we could eat it. Nevertheless we enjoyed the outing.

On January 27th we met at Dave Otis's to plan a new schedule and our mission was accomplished despite our tendency to digress. We're a "chatty" group as well as an "eating" group.

The following Sunday, six members with Dave Otis as leader, rode the Sugarbush gondola to Lincoln Peak and snowshoed on the Long Trail over to Mount Ellen, eating on the Trail and descending via one of the Glen Ellen ski trails.

On Valentine's Day eleven of us with Al Eldred and Jerry Dix as leaders snowshoed in to East Long Pond, dragging an ice cream freezer and provisions on a toboggan. At "This Is It," Al's camp, the men dug the fireplace from under the snow and built a fire while the women made ice cream. Dave Hine chopped a hole in the ice for water to make coffee and Al Eldred brushed the fireplace grate with balsam before cooking the hot dogs and hamburgs. Incidentally we heated chocolate sauce for the ice cream. Dave Otis made the comment that it made it interesting to have a couple of "crackpots" in the Club who would haul in an ice cream freezer to make ice cream in the middle of winter to eat out-of-doors around an open fire. We agreed.

On February 27th Dave Otis and Dave Morse upheld the honor of the Club, which never cancels a hike, when despite icy conditions they climbed Mt. Mansfield via the Hasleton Trail, hiked on up to the Chin, then down to Taft Lodge where they ate lunch, and down the Long Trail to 108 and back to the parking lot. They were not the same two who hauled in the ice cream freezer.

Our next hike was on St Patrick's Day. Dave Morse was unable to lead it as scheduled so I took over. It was supposed to have been a moonlight hike but it might have been more appropriately called a flash-light hike. Fourteen of us hiked an unplowed road in Berlin and climbed a small hill to look for the moon. On our way back we almost caught a glimpse of it. Refreshments followed per usual.

Yesterday, March 28th, nine of us climbed Mt. Hunger. We reached the area by means of Howard Bloom's Jeep truck which plowed right through the drifts. We began hiking about 10:15 A.M. Most of us shed our snowshoes at the rocky summit which we reached about 1:00 P.M. but Dave Otis wore his to the very top. It was an unusually clear day and we were able to identify various peaks of the White Mountains. The ski trails at Sugarbush, Glen Ellen, Mad River Glen, Mt. Mansfield and Spruce Peak were easily identifiable. Camel's Hump was prominent against the skyline as was Vermont's Whiteface. It was also possible to see Lake Champlain and the Adirondacks beyond through Nebraska Notch. The view from the 3554 foot Mt. Hunger surpasses that from the 4083 foot Camel's Hump and 4393 foot Mt. Mansfield. It is less obstructed and affords a wider range of vision. After eating lunch and enjoying the view we left the summit about 2:30 P.M. and were back to the Jeep by 4:00. Dave Morse led the hike.

On April 11th we have a road walk in Moretown scheduled; our annual meeting is April 23; and on May 2nd we are climbing Worcester Mountain. All of these will be gone by before the News is out but copy is due April 1st so the details will appear in the next issue.

MARION GORHAM, Reporter

The Long Trail News

On January 31, Jim Green was our leader on a Sunday afternoon hike at Straw Hollow. This was one of those times we wished we had brought along the snowshoes. The road looked awfully good to the girls especially—the fellows, of course, never get tired. Fran Green hospitably invited us back for coffee and real Viennese strudel which she and Luise Wadsworth from the Killington Section had baked up the day before. Nellie Sternstrom had brought along some freshly baked molasses doughnuts (just in case) which she says were made from a recipe that is 100 years old. It was a feast for a gourmet!

On February 7, we had a lucky 13 out on a hike around the Kettle Brook Reservoir. On Valentine's Day an outing at our Leicester camp was followed by a bring-your-own-type supper (coffee served) and entertainment in the form of some of Mauri Winturri's excellent slides. This outing brought out the largest number of people—32! Ben and Dot Rolston surprised us by showing up at our camp for a too short visit, but we were delighted to see them!

On February 21, we enjoyed a beautiful hike on a warm sunny day in the Barre Falls Dam area. It turned out to be a fairly long hike and was made even longer by our short cut through a Tamarack grove. It was a lovely place to see and we are sure glad we have seen it, but wouldn't it have been nice if the road hadn't all of a sudden come to a dead end in a thicket?

On February 28th, 17 of us slipped and slid on a hike out by the Quinapoxet Reservoir. All along the way we had to stop now and then to watch the golden drops of sap oozing out of the sugar maples. Tin buckets were hung everywhere.

On March 7th, 16 of us enjoyed a hike in the Millbury area which brought us down an abandoned road and ended up in a pre-revolutionary cemetery where we all had fun reading the epitaphs on the tombstones. This was followed by a delightful repast of cocoa and cake at the home of our hike leaders, the Christensens'.

And so ends our winter hiking season for this year. Our sugaring off is just ahead and our lovely spring season.

LINNEA ELDRIDGE, Reporter


OUT ON THE TRAIL

Are you looking for new trails to conquer? Would your north-south legs like to try east-west for a change?

There is an 18-mile section of the Appalachian Trail which is maintained by our Club, which is in Vermont, but which is not a part of the Long Trail. Interested? Then perhaps some details may be appropriate for this column.

First, some background. In past years these 18 miles were annually speed cleared by the Long Trail Patrol. But speed clearing alone cannot groom a trail to desired standards, and the Patrol's services are usually more urgently needed on the Long Trail and shelter maintenance. As a result, this section slowly deteriorated into a sub-standard condition.

Now where is this trail? It begins at Sherburne Pass and leads the hiker east to Route 100, then to Stony Brook Shelter, then to the old Chatauguay Road, then to The Lookout, then to Gulf Shelter, and finally to Vermont Route 12 about four miles north of Woodstock.

We have now described the location of this trail, and the urgent need for improving its condition. But who was to do this work? The answer—volunteers. Last spring the writer began the search for volunteers, and several persons and groups stepped forward to heed the call.

The Weeks from Springfield, Vermont, offered to clear from Sherburne Pass to Route 100. The Flemings of North Sherburne volunteered to clear from Route 100 to the Ottauquechee River. The Logans from New Jersey offered to clear from the Ottauquechee to Stony Brook Shelter. Farm and Wilderness Camps wanted to help, so they were assigned the section from the shelter to the old Chatauguay Road. Mauri Winturri from Fitchburg, Massachusetts, volunteered to carry on from Chatauguay to The Lookout. The Logans said they'd also clear from The Lookout to the Lookout Farm Road. And Mrs. Whitney from Randolph Center promised to bring a group to complete the section from the Lookout Farm Road to Route 12.

With the entire 18 miles assigned, what happened? THE VOLUNTEERS WENT TO WORK! Additional volunteers—Harry Woodbury from Woodstock, the Whites from Lunenburg, Massachusetts, GMC'ers from Springfield, and others—assisted those who had already volunteered. Axes, saws, clippers, swizzle sticks, and paint brushes were all applied in proper sequence in one mass effort to convert the 18 miles of trail into an excellent condition of which the GMC could be proud.

In just a few weeks, ALL of the section was cleared, most of it was brushed, and most of it was newly blazed. The remaining brushing and blazing, plus a complete speed clearing, will be done this spring by our dedicated volunteers.

This is an interesting section of trail, and several access points are available. Or for those who wish to walk the entire section, Stony Brook and Gulf Shelters are available. Complete details are contained in the Guide to the Appalachian Trail in New Hampshire and Vermont, or a condensed description of the route can be obtained by forwarding a stamped envelope to the writer of this article.

This section is a vital link in the Appalachian Trail route from Maine to Georgia. We'd like YOU to help wear down the footpath, and to enter your names in the registers along the way. And our volunteers will be pleased to have you inspect their fine work. Why not walk it THIS season?

BEN ROLSTON
