PRESIDENT'S MESSAGE                          INTERSECTIONAL AUG. 27-SEPT. 4

With the recent Annual Meeting down in history as one of the most enthusiastic and well attended as one can turn our attention to participation in some of the activities ahead. I feel that the Club is in good condition trail-wise, socially, and financially. Keeping it that way will require steady work by the recently appointed committees and the support of all members.
   But first, do you go back and re-read the LONG TRAIL NEWS from time to time, and discover choice bits that you overlooked when you skimmed over the highlights the first time through? I do, and more and more I realize what a fine publication we have, and how fortunate we have been with such excellent editorship.
   Coming up soon will be the treat of the summer—The Intersectional. I know that most of those who attended the Lake Eden session will plan to come back and camp at Plymouth this year. Being in my home county I am more than pleased with the prospective event. I think that we have entered an era of Intersectionals which will provide fun for all, and help the growth of the Club. SEE YOU THERE!
                              JOHN VONDELL, President

Last minute notice to all you campers that we are going to have another good week of fun in Mid-State Vermont, so don't forget the date and let's meet old friends and make new ones at the 1961 Intersectional.
   Information sheets were sent to all members-at-large, and section secretaries sent to local section members.
   The Place: Camp Plymouth, on shore of Echo Lake, one mile north of Tyson Village on the east shore of the lake. Turn off Route 100, go over the bridge opposite Echo Lake Inn and watch for GMC signs to camp. There is lots of room for tents, a good beach and swimming. Bring your camp gear, folding tables and chairs, and hiking equipment.

ANNUAL MEETING, MAY 27, 1961

The Annual Meeting of the Green Mountain Club, Inc., was called to order May 27, 1961, at 11:05 A.M. at the Long Trail Lodge by President Don Kent.
   Reports of the various sections were read, and the usual accounts of activities and trail conditions were given.
   A report of vandalism in the Burlington Section led to a discussion by President Kent and Trustee John Maslack on how vandalism had been met in various parts of the Trail running through the National Forest. All spoke of the need for prosecution of known vandals.
   Roy Buchanan reported for the Trails & Shelters Committee, stating his full written report was in the May issue of the Long Trail News. He added the sad news of many broken windows in Cooper Lodge.

John Maslack reported for the U.S. Forest Service section of the Long Trail. He stated that Mad Tom Shelter will be completely rebuilt. Trail work will be limited to bare necessities because of the lack of funds.

A discussion then followed concerning cooperation between the Middlebury College Outing Club and the GMC. More adult guidance was needed for the college outing club, and a promise of USFS aid would be forthcoming in the Middlebury area.

John Martin reported for the Publicity & Membership Committee. The new short trip folders were out with 3000 copies available through the GMC, and 10,000 through the Vermont Department of Development.

John Vandell reported for the End-to-End Committee. He gave a short history of the End-to-End Awards. The official count of End-to-Enders is now at 107.

Ben Rolston reported for the Guide Book Committee. 2252 copies of the Anniversary Edition Guide Book have been distributed since it was printed two years ago.

Bob Humes reported for the Intersectional Outing Committee, adding a few facts to a mimeographed sheet his committee had ready for distribution. Everything seemed to be in readiness for the outing at Camp Plymouth on Echo Lake next August.

The GMC Treasurer, Ted Goddard, distributed copies of his report, and added a few comments to it. His report was accepted by vote of the club and filed.

John Paulson reported for the Annual Meeting Program Committee, stating an unusually large number of members were present today, well over 100. Plans for hikes in the area of the Long Trail Lodge this afternoon and tomorrow were announced, after which we recessed for lunch.

Reconvened at 2:30 P.M.

Ted Goddard reported for the Trust Fund Committee. The main item was the renewal of the Trust Fund contract for a period of ten years. A written report was accepted by the members and placed on file.

Mr. Stevens gave a briefing on the Appalachian Trail Conference at the Delaware Water Gap, and extended an invitation for the GMC, which is a member of the Conference, to host the Conference in 1964, at Manchester, Vt. A committee of Peggy Pons, John Paulson, and Isaac Bartlett was appointed as a steering group to look into this possibility and report by conference time at the Delaware Water Gap the weekend of June 3 and 4. It was then established that about six members of the GMC would be attending the Conference, and would act as GMC delegates.

Mr. Cleveland of Randolph, Vt., introduced himself, and expressed his desire, and that of 15 or so of his hiking friends, to activate a new Randolph Section of the GMC. Craigue Perkins will work with Mr. Cleveland to establish this new section.

The Nominating Committee, headed by John Vandell, proposed the following names of members who were elected to the respective offices: Trustees (5): Roy Buchanan, Harlan Farnsworth, Miss Marion Hardy, William Bartlett, Raymond Catozzi. Clerk: Don Kent. Corresponding Secretary: Miss Minerva Hinchey.

The clerk was then instructed to read the proposed changes in the by-laws regarding the election of Trustees. Much discussion followed bringing out pros and cons of these changes. A motion was eventually made, seconded and passed to accept the changes as proposed, which are to go in effect January 1, 1962.

The auditor's report was read and accepted.

A standing applause and vote of thanks was given to retiring President Don Kent for the splendid work he had done during his two terms of office.

Adjourned at 4:15 P.M.

RAY CATOZZI, Clerk


LONG TRAIL PATROL

Although traces of winter's white blanket were still found in mid-June, the Patrol began the preparations for the ceaseless job of trail and shelter maintenance. The gang started work on Monday, June 26th with three men, Everett Washer from Johnson, Bryan Schumacher from Burlington and, Leader, Harris Abbott from Burlington. That day they went from Burlington to Rutland and worked the AT east from the Long Trail Lodge. Tuesday they planned to do the window repair job on Killington's Cooper Lodge. After that they were going to work farther east on the AT. At week's end they will come back to Burlington and next week will be put on the New York Section trails. Harris and I checked the leaky roof on Birch Glen last week and since then we have cussed and discussed its repair, with no result so far.

ROY BUCHANAN

BURLINGTON SECTION

Up The Trail Apiece—

Under the leadership of our President, George Saunders and Andrew Buchanan, our Outing Chairman, the Burlington Section has engaged in a weekly outing schedule instead of one every two weeks as has been the past custom. The committee feels such a schedule offers more of its members a greater choice and a greater opportunity to take part in more of the activities.

The outing highlights of the Section thus far were the Oyster Stew supper at Taylor Lodge on March 18 when over forty snowshoed in some three miles for the supper stewed up by the Remicks. The Maple Sugar party and square dance on March 25th brought out one hundred and ten. The baked bean dinner on Mt. Philo brought out seventeen in spite of a cold drizzling rain. Clyde Smith's beef stew dinner and tramp to Cantilever Rock in the Underhill State Park area turned out a large group.

The canoe trip to Lake George June 24-25 was small in number but full of spirit and enthusiasm. Daan Zwick and his family from Rochester, N.Y., joined the Burlington group. Daan was a very active member of the Burlington Section before going to Rochester and was for some two or three seasons hutmaster at Taft Lodge, up under the Chin of Mt. Mansfield.

Seriously, this worked out surprisingly well, considering the cooks are only fourteen years old and this was their first experience with mass production.

Other Reasons You Should Go To Thendara: The hosts & hostesses throughout the summer are delightful people to know and with whom to live for as long as you can stay. They are anxious to have you—and you—at camp as often as you can get there. They have many surprises in store for you. You know you always have a good time as well as good food. Of course you do some work but you do more if you stay home. "I'll still have it when I return" you say. But, you'll have much more vim and vigor after a jolly weekend.

The Fall Season is unusually interesting at camp—take your camera each weekend, the coloring should be captured.

NEW BAKER PEAK TRAIL

Now hear this! All hikers in search of new mountains to climb! Report at once to Route 7 south of Danby, and prepare to hike Vermont's newest trail.

As you park at the railroad siding where the marble is loaded, and look to the east, you will see a long ridge topped with a small bare-rock knob. The USGS topographic map calls this Baker Peak. If you want an afternoon of pleasant hiking, with a grand view, then don your hiking shoes and join us.

We drive south a short distance from the quarry area, then take the first dirt road to the left, which leads to the beginning of the USFS Lake Trail, where we park our cars in the field by the trail sign.

The Lake Trail is considered by many to be one of the nicest side trails in our Long Trail system. It follows the old Griffith Road over which teams used to travel to Griffith Lake, so although the climb is steady, it is not steep.

In about 1.6 miles we cross a bridge over an interesting rock face, and we can see the metal bars drilled into the rock which used to support a trestle. A short distance beyond, a spur trail on the right leads up to an open rock which gives a fine view of the valley below.

We soon reach the head of the "dyke", and after having followed the Lake Trail for about two miles, we cross the brook and begin the new trail to Baker Peak. As we have already gained considerable elevation, the remaining mile to Baker Peak requires very little climbing.

Leaving the Lake Trail, we utilize an old woods road for 2100 feet. We then leave this road, and after a short climb, we reach, at 2400 feet, a level area where we hike through very nice open hardwoods. At 3600 feet we cross a small stream, and just beyond we cross another small stream. It is hoped that these streams will be a source of water through all but the very driest part of the hiking season.

We soon reach an area of scrub birch growth, and at 3960 feet, or exactly 1/2 mile from the Lake Trail, we reach "Quarry View". Here is an area of open rocks where a splendid view may be had of the marble quarry on Dorset Peak directly opposite.

We now reenter the woods and head in a direct bee-line for the summit. We climb, but not steeply, until we reach, at 4660 feet, the beginning of the open rock ridge top of the mountain. We leave the woods and traverse the backbone of this ridge for 560 feet, and at 5220 feet, just short of a mile from where we left the Lake Trail, we reach the highest part of this open summit and the terminus of our new trail, at an elevation of 2780 feet.

Here the view is magnificent! We can see for many miles the valley through which run Route 7 and Otter Creek. We get an especially fine view of Danby. Green Mountain, above Little Rock Pond, lies just to the north. We look directly across at Dorset Peak, with the marble quarry clinging to its side. Toward the south we see Mt. Aeolus, and in the distance beyond Equinox Mountain stands out clearly. Due to a higher ridge beyond us, there is no view to the east, but we can see other peaks of the Green Mountains to the north and Black Mountain at Lake George and other Adirondack summits to the distant northwest.

We know that you will like the new trail up Baker Peak. Its beginning at the Lake Trail brook crossing is marked with a sign, and the trail is blue-blazed and well cleared for your hiking pleasure. We hope that you will have the opportunity to make this hike during the 1961 summer or fall season. With its fine views, Baker Peak should make a splendid foliage hike.

BEN ROLSTON

(Our thanks to Ben Rolston for locating, clearing and blazing this new trail)

OUT OF THE PAST

Continued from May Issue

We now begin the long ascent to Killington, where just below the summit is Killington Camp, a round sheet metal hut. Our next lodging is at Clarendon Camp, with bunks for 20 people, where we are instructed to keep porch gate closed because of cattle. We then see Clarendon Gorge, go over the ridge above Shrewsbury Pond with views of Killington, and reach Buffum Camp (*) which has bunks for 25 people. After going over White Rocks, and passing Little Pond (also called Rock Cliff Pond), we reach Black Branch, near which we can find bunks in an old charcoal kiln.

After crossing the Danby-Weston highway, we reach the deserted town of Griffith, pass ruins of charcoal kilns, then pass a red schoolhouse and reach a shelter known as Three Shanties open to hikers by permission of the owners. An old lumber camp at Long Hole will provide some shelter, and at Buffum Pond we can use the porch of the old Griffith House.

On the deserted East Dorset-Peru highway a GMC camp provides bunks for 24. And after going over Bromley Mountain and crossing the Peru Turnpike, we reach Windall Pike where there is another GMC camp with bunks for 24 persons.

After enjoying the view at Prospect Rock, we follow the old Rootville Road to the Buck lumber job and then follow an old highway to the Dufresne Job at the foot of Stratton Mountain. While on this road we reach the remains of a railroad; we may follow this, with its serpentine curves and log bridges, to Bourne Pond and thence to Sunderland. From Bourne Pond, where there are boats, a trail can be followed to Stratton Pond and the Dufresne Job.

After reaching the Dufresne Job via the old highway, where deserted buildings offer some shelter we ascend Stratton Mountain, then descend to the Arlington-Wardsboro Road and Grout Job, an abandoned lumbering village. Here is Camp Webster, leased by members of the Bennington Section, which can be used by obtaining the key in advance at Bennington.

From the Arlington Road the Trail leads south joins an old railroad right-of-way, and reaches the road leading to Somerset Dam. The caretaker, with advance arrangement, will provide board and lodging, and at Somerset Bridge accommodations are available at a farmhouse. The Trail now leads westerly to Little Pond and Hagar Clearing, and then reaches Hell Hollow Camp, another camp furnished by the Bennington Section. Here we find a side trail to Glastenbury Mountain.

After crossing the highway leading to Bennington we complete the remaining distance to the Massachusetts state line, and end our 271-mile trip on the Long Trail, guided by the text of our 1924 Guide Book. We have climbed over many mountains and fully agree with a claim quoted in our Guide Book that if Vermont could be pressed out smooth, its area would probably exceed that of Texas!

(*) denotes shelters still standing.

THE GUIDE BOOK COMMITTEE

HOW I JOINED THE GREEN MOUNTAIN CLUB

In May, 1914, Captain Herbert W. Congdon, Co. A, 23rd Regt., New York National Guard, asked three of us enlisted men to go with him in the late summer on a hiking trip on the Long Trail in Vermont. We thought it would be a fine idea to wear our uniforms and take along shelter tents, blankets, ponchos, etc. in the upright packs which were a great improvement over the old chest-crushing blanket roll and hip jolting haversack. So Congdon wrote to the Adjutant-General of the National Guard at Albany for permission to use our uniforms and equipment for this two weeks' trip in Vermont. Albany then wrote to Headquarters of the Vermont National Guard for permission. The latter replied that it would permit troops from a neighboring state to "invade" Vermont only on the condition that we would constitute an official reconnaissance patrol that would make a thorough report on our trip covering "all pertinent military features," such as the practicability of roads, trails, camp sites etc. for an army including artillery, etc. This we agreed to do and were given permission, after many weeks of correspondence. We also wrote to Dr. Paris, Secretary of the Green Mountain Club, and received from him a great deal of valuable information concerning the Long Trail, with sketch maps showing good camp sites, places where the Trail was incomplete and hard to follow and so forth.

In our packs, which weighed about 38 lbs. each, we carried soft food such as oatmeal, dried pea soup, dried prunes, apricots, etc. for only five days; and we sent by parcel post similar five days' food supplies to two towns in the valley.

Late in August, 1914, we took the night train to Burlington, a jitney to the entrance to Smugglers' Notch, and started our fifteen day hike there. At what is now Barnes Camp we started up the Long Trail and set up our pup-tents near the top of Mt. Mansfield. That night it rained and for the next four days we walked mostly in fog or drizzle, slept in a shelter at Montclair Glen one night and the others in our tents set up on the trail. When we reached Bolton, we stopped at the first large house; the very kind and hospitable couple, Mr. & Mrs. Huntly, invited us in for the night. We hung up masses of soggy clothing, blankets, etc. on the lines stretched across their warm kitchen and slept in two up-stairs bedrooms; also enjoyed typical Vermont supper and breakfast in generous quantities.

The rest of our hike was more pleasant, as the weather was fair, although the nights were cold. We usually halted at 5 P.M. to set up camp, cut spruce boughs for bedding, make a fire and prepare supper. Capt. Congdon, an experienced woodsman, taught us many things, such as cutting rock maple and yellow birch, the only wood that will burn green and wet. We set up the tents with the front openings facing each other and the fire in between and whoever was awake at any time would put more wood on the fire.
