Governor Wilson On The Long Trail.

In the December issue of the News appeared a brief extract from the address of Gov. Stanley C. Wilson of Vermont, broadcast from Boston September 26 last. We now give the text of this address, and commend it as an excellent exposition of the Trail and the Club, and coming from so high a source should prove a material aid to the cause to which the Club has devoted its energies. It has been printed in pamphlet form by the Vermont Bureau of Publicity.

"Two weeks ago we celebrated a birthday in Vermont and as the final event of the celebration we had in the evening a display of fireworks that extended nearly two hundred miles. At Long Trail Lodge on the crest of a mountain pass through the Green Mountains on the cross-state highway between Rutland and White River Junction a signal fire was lighted. Immediately the signal was seen and another fire was lighted on the mountain above the pass. Then in quick succession flare after flare blazed out on the mountain tops to the north; first on Cushman Mountain; then Lincoln Mountain and Camel's Hump; then mighty Mt. Mansfield; then Belvidere Mountain, and finally Jay Peak, towering almost at the Canadian border, from which place the flare was readily visible in Montreal.

Meanwhile a similar line of signal fires went south from the mountain pass appearing one after the other on Mt. Pico, Killington Peak, Bromley Mountain, Stratton Mountain, Glastenbury Mountain, and finally on Mount Greylock in Massachusetts near North Adams.

It took but a few minutes to pass the signal from the Long Trail Lodge to Massachusetts and to Canada, but this great display was witnessed in whole or in part by countless thousands not only in the State of Vermont but over the border in Massachusetts, New Hampshire and New York.

Fourteen flaming signals over an irregular course approximately two hundred miles long on the sky-line of a state! Did you ever see a signal blaze break out in the darkness and wonder where it was and what it was? What about seeing a series like this and knowing that each fire was on the crest of a noble mountain, that by each fire was a group of people intent on telling in the ancient way of the Indian to another group off on a far away mountain a message in turn to be passed on to others in the distant darkness.

The message was one of grateful acknowledgment and reply to good-will greetings earlier received from the Governor of Massachusetts and the Prime Minister of the Province of Quebec on the occasion of this birthday. These friendly messages were brought in the late afternoon to me as Governor of Vermont by messengers on foot over the Long Trail—a trail such as exists nowhere else, extending along the crest of the Green Mountains. It took many days to bring the messages. The answer was flashed back in primitive manner along the same course but with a speed that rivaled the modern means of communication.

I had to be where I could only see one flare but others who climbed to hill tops or mountain tops saw many. I learned of some who counted eleven. They must have seen the length of the state. Word has come from the Adirondacks in New York and from the White Mountains in New Hampshire of observation of some of the signals.

But you ask what was the birthday and why this novel birthday celebration. It was the 21st birthday of the Green Mountain Club, an organization containing nearly 1500 members who enjoy outdoor life and love the Green Mountains. The headquarters of the club is in Rutland, Vermont, but about two-thirds of the membership is from outside Vermont, largely in Massachusetts and New York."

Long Trail Lodge.

Two new cabins were built at the Lodge last season. One, called Cedar lodge, to care for four guests, was built and furnished at the Lodge expense, except for a generous donation from Paul W. Thayer, the architect and builder. The other, a gift from Mrs. Charles H. Bayley, of Newbury, Vt., was built after the close of the season, and will be furnished ready for use the coming season. The Woodfin cabin was completely furnished and plumbing installed.

The greatest need of the Lodge now is for more cabins. Hardly a week-end passes in the busy part of the season without seeing some guests turned away for lack of sufficient rooms.

The kitchen equipment was greatly improved by the substitution of a modern double hotel-type range for the smaller one which had been used ever since the Lodge started. This change will make this part of the equipment much more efficient.

Much work was done around the Lodge to enhance the natural beauty of the surroundings. The trees and bushes were trimmed to open up the view from the house and beautiful vistas thereby obtained. The drive approaching the front of the house was regraded.

It is expected that the Lodge will open early in June, with Mrs. Bates in charge.

The Proposed Memorial Bridge.

The suggestion in the last News of a bridge across the Winooski at Bolton as a memorial of Prof. Will S. Monroe brought forth numerous comments, both oral and written, all of which were favorable, with one exception. J. A. Allis of New York, custodian of the fund raised some years ago for some sort of a memorial to Dr. Monroe, writes that he does not favor the bridge suggestion, and that he has another plan which he will unfold at the meeting of the trustees in June. He does not give the reasons for his disapproval. Below we quote three of the letters which were received on the subject.

Boston, Mass.
Feb. 1, 1932

I note the proposal to build a bridge across the river at Bolton as a memorial to Prof. Monroe and I wish to voice my hearty approval of the plan. If it is decided to do this and more funds are needed a notice to that effect in the News would bring results.

EDITH M. ESTERBROOK.

Pelham, N. Y.
Feb. 3, 1932.

Just a word on two points in the last Long Trail News, which I always read with interest.

I think the project for a bridge over the Winooski as a memorial to Prof. Monroe is fine, and hope it can be accomplished.

Second, I think the suggestion to have fifty miles or so of continuous stretch of trail manned by caretakers with blankets and food, is also fine, and would make delightful trips possible to many who do not feel that they can undertake a long trip with a heavy pack (of whom I am one!)

Sincerely yours,
ELIZABETH I. TOMS.

Philadelphia, Pa.,
Feb. 1, 1932

I see by the current number of the "Long Trail News" that the question was raised at the annual meeting of the G. M. C. of building a bridge across the Winooski at Bolton as a memorial to Dr. Monroe; that some funds are believed to be in hand which might be applied to this purpose, and that expressions of opinion on the subject would be welcome.

As a warm personal friend of Dr. Monroe, and the frequent recipient of his hospitality at Couching Lion Farm, I wish to raise my voice in the fullest commendation of this idea if it can be put through. It seems to me that no more suitable memorial to Dr. Monroe could be conceived of: I am sure

News From The Sections.

Bennington.

This Section is getting ready for summer activities. Two camps have been cleaned and repaired and are now in first class condition. Several new members have joined.

Burlington.

The annual Washington's birthday hike was enjoyed by sixty people. They went up into Smugglers' Notch, hiking from Morse's Mills, going by bus to that point. On the return journey they had supper at Smugglers' Notch Inn at Jeffersonville.

A committee has been appointed to choose the location and build the Mabel Taylor Butler lodge, presented to the Green Mountain Club by Albert Butler in memory of his wife.

Manchester.

This new Section has had several walks during the winter. They are planning to build a shelter the coming summer on their part of the Trail, and to improve the Trail itself.

New York.

In addition to the regular week-end outings, a longer trip than ever before undertaken is proposed, one of five to six weeks to the Canadian National Parks and Glazier National Park. Also one of ten days to the White Mountains, similar to the one of last August.

Rutland.

This Section had an outing trip on Washington's birthday, going to upper Pine Hill, where Mrs. Fostman and Miss Tye opened their camps and served hot coffee. From there they hiked over to the two ponds. Several other outings are projected, and letters will be sent to each member to stimulate interest.

Notes.

Several types of metal trail signs were exhibited at the trustees' meeting. It is expected that metal signs will gradually displace these of wood now in use, as the latter wear out.

The trustees at their meeting were provided with detailed maps of the Long Trail provided by Mr. Fay, which showed side trails, camps, places where meals and lodging may be had, main roads, county roads, town roads and camps to be built or rebuilt.

The desirability of more uniformity in the nomenclature of trail shelters was brought up at the meeting. At present these are called rather indiscriminately camps, shelters, cabins, lodges and huts. The term "lodge" seems to imply something rather more pretentious than the others, like Taft lodge and Taylor lodge. No action was taken.

A poet in the Vermonter says:
     Music quickens your heart,
     Mountains, mine;
     Green or blue or purple—
     They are wine.

We would remind this poet that if this is a fact they are in violation of the 18th amendment and should be abolished.

Anyhow, we started something by the suggestion of a memorial bridge in the last News. If it results in some definite plan being adopted for the proposed memorial, which has been hanging fire so long, whether a bridge or something just as good, as they say in drug stores, or better, then it will have been worth while. We hope to have something of interest on this subject in the next issue.
