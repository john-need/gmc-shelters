Dear Mr. Buchanan:
   I have just been reading the November number of the LONG TRAIL NEWS which arrived this morning. Your report on the work of the Patrol was of immediate interest because my son and I hiked that northern part of the Trail this past summer. We saw evidences of your activity all along and at Emery's (on the Johnson-Waterville Road) heard of your recent visit in that vicinity.
   Your comment on continuing the work of the Patrol this coming summer is to the point. Might it not be well to make an appeal to the G.M.C. members asking for a special contribution to a Long Trail Patrol Fund? We can't all work on the Trail but we might assume the cost of one day's work or a fraction of it. If others are willing, I would be glad to put $5.00 in the kitty.

OUT OF DOORS IN WORCESTER COUNTY
    We have received a copy of a booklet with the above title, prepared by a board of editors mostly associated with the Worcester Museum of Natural History and the Worcester Public Library, which might well serve as a model for similar publications in other cities. After brief accounts of the geology and history of the Worcester region it devotes nearly fifty pages to the out-door attractions of this territory. Birds, plants, ferns, hiking, horseback riding, skiing, boating, swimming and driving are all included and the facilities provided by the city parks are listed. All in all, except perhaps for the maps, it is a very complete, concise and useful booklet, worth much more than the 25c price to anyone interested in Worcester and the country around it. Incidentally we note the names of two well known members of the Worcester Section of the G.M.C. among the editors, Jessie Phillimore and Stuart Van Buren, and there may be others whom we do not know.

MT. MANSFIELD SKIING
   The above title is the name of the official publication of the Mt. Mansfield Ski Club, and, although the ski season is nearly over—it was 98° in the shade in Burlington a week ago,—we quote the following from its issue of Dec. 15, 1948.

                           * * *
                AROUND THE MOUNTAIN
                   By A. W. Coleman
   The Stowe-Mt. Mansfield region moves into its sixteenth season as a ski center with what to me seems just about everything an eastern skier could expect in the way of facilities. This not to imply that I think everything is better than anywhere else, or even that there is nothing desirable to be added. But what Stowe has is a variety of developed terrain, uphill carriers and accommodations, with a first class ski school and provisions for indoor recreation, not surpassed in completeness today by any other area in the east.
   Aside from the natural advantage of a 4000-foot mountain and outstanding snow conditions, I personally consider Stowe's primary single attraction to be the chair lift to the top of the mountain. There will be many who violently disagree with me on this, but it is my opinion that vertical descent, served by uphill transportation, is the important measure of what kind of sport is available for the downhill-only skier.
                                       * * *
   According to a survey of eastern U. S. ski lifts which I made for the current American Ski Annual, only three centers this winter will provide uphill transportation in the neighborhood of 2000 vertical feet, and those three are practically equal in this respect and in a class by themselves. They are: Mt. Mansfield (chair lift) 2030 feet; Cannon Mountain (aerial tramway) 2022 feet; Mad River Glen (chair lift) 1970 feet.
   Next to these come three areas with lifts providing over 1000 feet of vertical rise, utilizing in each case two lifts in tandem. They are: Big Bromley, Manchester, Vt. (J-bar) 1260 feet; Cranmore Mountain, North Conway, N. H. (skimobile) 1240 feet; Thorn Mountain, Jackson, N. H. (chair lift) 1110 feet.
   Of the remaining nineteen lifts (as distinct from rope tows) in the east at present, it is interesting to note that the T-bar at Mt. Mansfield, with its vertical rise of 986 feet, is first in the under-1000-foot class. Detailed figures on all these, incidentally, appear in the Annual.
                                       * * *
                        By Charles D. Lord
   Each year finds added improvements and new facilities, all of which are rapidly and surely keeping Stowe well ahead of the major ski centers of the East. But with all the developments to date, the vast potentialities of the Stowe-Mansfield region have as yet been only partially utilized.

                    NEW DEVELOPMENT IN OFFING
   Also of tremendous interest and speculation is the proposed new ski development in the Spruce Peak-Sterling Range area which is located across the Notch to the East of the present Mansfield developments. While work has started on an access road and preliminary surveys have been made, plans have not yet reached the stage where any public announcement can be made.

                       THE SAME OLD QUESTION
   The New York Times of Sunday, Jan. 9, 1949 had an article by Richard L. Neuberger entitled "Ski versus Snowshoe," from which we venture to quote a few paragraphs, hoping not to be sued by our esteemed contemporary, the Times.
   In the white silences of the North Woods a social barrier exists as wide as the Yukon River and as high as a Ponderosa pine. Climbing Mount St. Elias or winning an Eagle Scout badge is simple compared with surmounting it. The folk on opposite sides barely communicate by smoke signals, let alone engage in normal conversation. The barrier lies between those who ski and those who snowshoe.

In the winter solitudes of the Northwest and the sub-Arctic you are of one clan if you ski. You never speak to the plodder on snowshoes or deign to recognize his existence. You and he refuse to invite each other into cozy shelters on sub-zero afternoons, never pool forces, even if the wolf pack or a mountain lion should be in close pursuit.

The ski and the snowshoe are more significant symbols in the wilderness than is a bonnet on Fifth Avenue or a mink coat at the opera. The fellow on snowshoes is the backwoods dweller of long standing. He may be a trapper or United States Forest Ranger or a constable in the Royal Canadian Mounted Police. He acts as if he had been in the frosty fastnesses since the year one. He can light a campfire in a blizzard and survive a night outdoors at 70 below.

"Dude" of the Ranges

The skier is the "dude," the part-time inhabitant of the ranges. He is in the woods for fun. No pay check from Uncle Sam or the Hudson's Bay Company inspires his presence in the great outdoors. His womenfolk often brave the high white slopes with bare legs, bare midriffs and incredible garments. In a few weeks he may be back on a Hollywood stage set or in a Park Avenue office.

The typical skier thinks the average snowshoer has never been off the frontier. He looks upon him as a rude fellow who has yet to see a trolley car or two-story building.

The genuine North Woods dweller would sooner be caught in a blizzard without mittens than on a pair of skis. To the pioneer in the snow country snowshoes are as essential as a parka. For this there are good reasons: He must often pack heavy loads on his back; his knapsack is full of traps, ammunition, bacon and beans. Balancing on skis is precarious indeed, with ninety pounds of grub and equipment strapped to one's shoulders.

A few years ago a pioneer named Ellsworth Jaeger wrote a book called "Wildwood Wisdom." It told how to do everything in the wilderness from panning for gold to parboiling a Kodiak bear. Mr. Jaeger referred to snowshoes no less than 114 times. He discussed Ojibway snowshoes, Nascopie snowshoes, barrelstave snowshoes, goose-necked snowshoes. But not a single reference to skis!

Despite this snubbing, the skiers think they have a pretty good time. They insist they would rather whiz across the tundra at 40 miles an hour than plod across it at four. Nor do they apologize for the ski tows on which the snowshoers look with such disdain. The snowshoer insists that the wilderness is desecrated by the presence of an internal-combustion engine. The skier is for anything which will increase his opportunity to flash downhill.

P.S. We like snowshoes too.

THIS AND THAT

We are proud to say that we have 43 Junior members in the Killington Section of the G.M.C. Some of them are away at college, others are high school and grade school students while others aren't old enough to be hikers yet.

Junior members are important members in our club. In a few years they will be the leaders. Now is the time for them to learn about the trails and the mountains so that when their turn for leadership comes they will be ready.

Why do we have so few active Junior members? It is surprising that in a community the size of Rutland more boys and girls are not clamoring to get out on our mountain trails.

A day on Killington, a hike to a mountain lake, or a ski or snowshoe hike over mountain trails is an event worthwhile and enjoyable.

Come on boys and girls, get your gang together. Come out on our scheduled trips and have some fun. Let us hear what you would like in the way of a Junior program.

ICE CAVES TRIP

Well for the first time in history the Killington Section hiked—not up on a mountain—but underground! Twenty-eight members and guests made the trip to the Pittsford Ice Caves on Saturday, November 6th.

Guided by our Vice President, Leone Smith, about twenty "toured" the cavern. As the elevators didn't seem to be operating, we had to use the "stairway." Then one by one we were forced through the "lemon squeezer." This brought us to the "big-room" from which we entered another passage leading to the underground spring.              HAVE YOU SEEN THEM?

   The Green Mountain Club has a collection of 85 kodachrome slides available for club or private showings. The pictures are of scenes on the Long Trail and Views from the Trail. They depict hiking on the Green Mountains and show the types of shelters found along the 260 miles of trail. Some animal and plant slides are also included.

   There is a short written introduction that goes with the slides but no lecture. Each slide is named. A complete showing takes about forty-five minutes.
   This collection was compiled by John Vondel J, chairman of publicity for the Club. They may be obtained by writing to Miss Lula M. Tye, secretary of the Green Mountain Club, Chamber of Commerce office, Rutland, Vermont.

                        * * *

                 GIFFORD WOODS

   Have you been to Gifford Woods State Park lately? If you have never been there, take Route 4 over the mountain and turn left on Route 100. About one half mile further on you will find it on the left in a big grove of maples.
   The caretaker's house and covered terrace face the parking area. Beyond these are the picnic spots with fireplaces and running water in the summer. Off to the left are five log shelters with benches and fireplaces, and an excellent toilet building.

The Appalachian Trail runs through the Park. This section was newly painted last summer and can be followed west from the Park to Long Trail Lodge or east to the Sherburne Hollow Rd.
   Gifford Woods is a cool quiet place in summer and a good spot from which to make short winter hikes.
                                      * * *

                         A SPRING LAKE CAMP?

   For several years one of the most popular hikes on our schedule has been the annual swimming party at Spring Lake, or Shrewsbury Pond, if you prefer that name.
   What a spot that would be for a new shelter! Well, about a year ago the Trails and Shelters Committee decided to make a few inquiries and do a little figuring.
   The land in the open pasture on the northwest side of the pond is owned by Ralph Congdon of Wallingford. He has given his consent to the building of a camp near the top of the hill where the trail south enters the woods.
   An inspection of the abandoned Blue Triangle Camp revealed a source of material. There is sufficient material here to build a fair sized, closed camp at the new location.
   Now all that remains to be done is to find a magician who can wave his wand and accomplish the following: tear down the old building, load selected parts on a truck, transport it to Spring Lake, float the lumber across, carry it up the hill a quarter of a mile to the selected site, and build the camp.
   All that sounds like a lot of work and it will be a lot of work. However, if you are as enthusiastic about it as we are, you just know we will get the necessary help somewhere. Maybe next year we can carry out this project. Any volunteers?

                                                                  * * *

                         REPORT OF BIRD MT. HIKE
                                   By Ray Catozzi

Threatening skies and hunters' bullets were braved on this outing, but another good turnout enjoyed the day. In spite of a chill wind we had fun, and nearly everybody scrambled their way to the top.
   There are some rugged high cliffs on the south face of the mountain about fifty yards from the top. If you don't scare easily it's worth your time to climb down and look them over, otherwise the view from the top is still splendid to the west and south.
   A few newcomers came along, including a lone hiker from E. Poultney who met us on the peak after being told at home he'd find no one there in November!
   After lunch, while we were resting, a couple of diehards, the Conlons, showed up. Which proves that you can't keep seasoned hikers in bed Sunday morning.

PANORAMA

Of course it is a little late, but we'd like to congratulate the editors and publishers of Panorama, "Burlington's Magazine of Current Interests," on their January 1949 issue, devoted primarily to skiing in Vermont. Printed on slick paper and well illustrated, it gave the prospective skier everything he needed to know (except where to find snow last winter). Next winter we'll have more snow (it is already on order), so if those who have this issue of Panorama will save it, they can use it then. Incidentally, this issue carries a quotation from Edward Everett Hale (Tarry at Home Travels, MacMillan Co.) about the city of Burlington. "So far as nature has anything to offer to the eye, I have certainly never seen in the travels of forty years, any position chosen for a city more likely to impress the traveller as remarkable, and to live always in his memory." The Burlington Section of the Green Mt. Club is glad to endorse his sentiments.

THE ADK

I have before me a little folder which says, on the outside, "This is The Adirondack Mountain Club," and on the inside, "What is the ADK?" It then answers this interesting question in a way to indicate a rather close kinship with our own club. They call it the ADK to avoid confusion with an older and larger AMC. The ADK cleared and marked the Northville-Placid Trail, the main trunk-line trail of the Adirondacks, 132 miles long (almost exactly half the length of the Long Trail) and does all it can to promote outdoor recreation and wild land conservation. The latter is a very live issue in New York state since raids on the state's wild lands, often very cleverly disguised, are constantly being undertaken by various interested parties, and it is only by continual vigilance and frequent protest that organizations of nature lovers have so far been able to fight them off.

The ADK now has about 1375 members, somewhat more than the G.M.C., in ten active chapters. It owns and operates Johns Brook Lodge, a hiker's and climber's lodge in the heart of the Adirondack High Peak region three and a half miles by foot trail from Keene Valley, and it leases and operates Adirondack Loj on Heart Lake, at the northern gateway of the high peaks. The Guide to Adirondack Trails is published by ADK, which should be in the possession of anyone intending to do hiking or skiing in the High Peak region, as well as a bi-monthly magazine, Adirondac, that appears with much more dependable regularity than the LONG TRAIL NEWS.

ANNUAL REPORT—1948

Suppose we review the activities of the Club for the year gone by.

The slated six business meetings were held during the year. The reports of the officers and Committee Chairmen were heard and approved. In May the resignation of David F. Johnson, as 1st-Vice-President and Outing Chairman was read and accepted. It was immediately voted to elect Miss Josephine M. O'Brien to succeed him. In June it was voted that the Trustees send out a "Questionnaire to members that they may express their views regarding the Camp Situation." The result of this vote will be found in the Trustees' Report.

Treasurer reported a busy year: what with receiving money with one hand and paying out with the other (and not getting all mixed up) the year ended with a balance of $231.62, with all bills paid including the addition to the G.M.C. Camp. The only obligation the Camp has is its $900.00 mortgage held against the Camp property.

Shelters and Trails did their usual two work party jobs on the Long Trail, and despite being rained out, did put a new roof on Webster Camp and cleared trail all along the line. Again our "Thanks" go to the Najerog boys who always do a fine job on the Caughnawauga trail. It is reported that someone took away the Register from Willis Ross Camp so we have no record of how many hikers had visited the Camp.

Outings this year were many and varied. The most

REPORT FROM THE BOARD OF TRUSTEES

Late in April the Trustees, assisted by a group of members, started moving out of Camp Cobb, and May 1st. was official moving day.

In July a letter was sent out regarding the local camp, and a printed card was sent to each member to be signed and used as a ballot, in order to get definite decisions on the question. With this as their guide the Trustees, assisted by a building committee started work, and the first post hole was dug on Sept. 25th. Now, although some of the windows are temporary, the building is weather-tight. It was used for the first time on New Year's Day. Our feet were a bit chilly but the second flooring has now been laid. The next job is the electrical wiring and painting the whole structure.

To date $122.82 has been spent. $164.05 has been earned for, or given to the fund by enthusiastic members. The idea of paying for a few square feet of floor space at 20¢ per square foot has intrigued folks into contributing $32.40 of the above sum. (There are still blank spots on the floor plan). More cash will be needed in the Spring, but the fever is catching, and we hope more folks will have money-raising ideas, and will help sponsor them. We also hope that we will be able to pay off some of the mortgage this year.

There is still much work to be done. Anyone wishing to join in the fellowship of working together will be very welcome. Just drop a card to me or to Pearle Clarke, and we will put your name on the list for work party notices.

Anyone driving to Camp in snowy weather should park their car on Salisbury St., just beyond WAAB radio towers, and cut across the open field to the right of the white house opposite towers. Camp can be seen from road when the leaves are off the trees. Permission to use this cut has been granted by the owners.

We hope all members will get as much pleasure out of using the camp as the "gang" seems to get out of working on it.

Respectfully submitted,
JESSIE PHILLIMORE
Charmn. of Trustees for 1949
