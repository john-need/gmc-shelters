REPORT OF TRUSTEES MEETING

Thirteen trustees of The Green Mountain Club met Saturday, April 13th, at the Club's office in Rutland at 108 Merchants Row. Harold Collins, acting president, conducted the meeting, and many important matters of business were discussed and voted on. A tentative budget was set up, and committee chairmen gave reports.

Mr. Collins announced that through the efforts of some Burlington Section members a mountain has been named for Prof. Roy Buchanan. The mountain that has been unofficially known as Old Splatterfoot has now been officially named Buchanan Mountain. There is to be a dedication ceremony sometime this summer. It would be nice if it could coincide with our Intersectional. This is just a suggestion from the editor and may not be workable. However, we do hope all Roy's mountain climbing friends will be notified of the date of the dedication. The announcement of the naming of Buchanan Mt. brought a round of applause from all the trustees.

The slide collection has been sent out to different clubs and organizations sixteen times this past year. It was suggested that the collection should be brought up to date with slides of the new camps and lodges that have been built in recent years.

Saturday 17th and 18th of August were the dates set for the Intersectional. More details on this event will appear later.

Arthur Koerber, chairman of the Guide Book Committee, is keeping information up to date for the next revised issue of the Guide Book. Only one half of the printed copies of the 1956 Edition remain unsold. Secretaries of Sections are requested to send to Mr. Koerber notice of any errors in the 1956 edition of the Guide Book, any re-locations of trails or new camps constructed or under construction since the last Guide Book was printed. This information may be sent to Green Mountain Club Inc., 108 Merchants Row c/o Mr. Arthur Koerber.

In reporting for the Trails and Shelters Committee, Roy Buchanan stated that the Trail north of Johnson is consistently kept in good repair and may be considered standardized. He also mentioned that the condition at the Winooski river crossing at Bolton remains the same. There is no boat there to enable a hiker to cross the river. He suggested re-locating the Trail three miles in the woods on the north side, avoiding putting the Trail on route U.S. 2 but eventually coming out to it at the bridge at Jonesville. A similar re-location would be necessary on the south side of the river. He was instructed to go ahead with this project.

Mrs. Lodge was instructed to order GMC emblems so that they may be distributed at the annual meeting. Individual members may purchase them at fifty cents each. Sections may purchase them in lots of ten or more for thirty cents each.

Roy Buchanan was elected trustee to fill the two year unexpired term of Eugene Bamforth.

John Martin was elected trustee to fill the one year unexpired term of Leone Smith.

Ted Goddard presented a sample of a poster to be displayed in Green Mountain Club shelters. It starts off "Read and Heed," and goes on with good advice written in an interesting and humorous style that insures reading every word to the last line. There will be more of these posters available at the annual meeting and they will be given out to Section officers or their representatives.

Craigue Perkins reviewed trail assignments to Sections, and with the help of other trustees these assignments were confirmed or corrected. He was instructed to send to the secretary of each Section a list showing the exact piece of Trail and the shelters that Section was responsible for; this to be approved or rejected by the Section. The purpose of this is to know who is responsible for all parts of the Trail and its shelters.

Mrs. Lodge, chairman of the annual meeting, reported that due to other business arrangements, Grover Wright can not have the Green Mountain Club meet at Long Trail Lodge during the morning of June 1st for their annual meeting. However, we may meet there any time after 2:30 P.M., have dinner there at night, and also an evening program. So, plans for the business part of the meeting (as to location of meeting) are that we meet at the Baptist Church meeting hall at Rutland 10 A.M. for the coffee hour, 11 P.M. for the business meeting and lunch. Details are given on page 1.

The date for The Green Mountain Club Long Trail Conference was set for November 2nd.

GMC MEMBERS ATTEND
NEW ENGLAND TRAIL CONFERENCE

More than thirty Green Mountain Club members attended the New England Trail Conference held April 6th at Amherst, Massachusetts. The total attendance was 135 so that makes the GMC representation look very good.

We are pleased to report that our GMC trustee, Arthur Koerber, was elected Chairman of the New England Trail Conference, and that our GMC trustee, Roy Buchanan, was re-elected a member of the Executive Board.

At the morning session, when the reports of all the Clubs were given, Roy Buchanan reported for The Green Mountain Club.

The social part of the program included talks by three speakers with slides describing their subjects. Titles of the talks given were: A Trail is Born; Along New England Trails; A Trip to the Caribbean. Trail clearing tools were also demonstrated in the afternoon.

Supper was served at the Methodist Church after which a humorous skit was put on by the Metawampe Club.

NEW YORK SECTION REPORTS

The annual dinner-dance, to which so many of us look forward with pleasant anticipation, fell on the night of the winter's worst snowstorm. Several New Jersey couples were stranded in various snowdrifts, while their more fortunate New York friends made merry at the hotel. The attendance was off but the fun went on in spite of it all.

Our annual business meeting March 28th drew a good crowd to hear reports from all committees and to discuss several club matters.

The April to June bulletin shows that Camp Thendara will be open every weekend, except the Easter one, when no activities are scheduled. The spring season at camp is highlighted by the always popular and interesting Bird Census weekend on May 11th to 12th. This is conducted by our own Mrs. Laura Woodward Abbott, for whom Laura Woodward Shelter on the Long Trail is named.

Sunday hikes come along regularly each week.

The weekend activities include trail clearing parties in the Kittatinnies and Wyanokies; two canoe cruises; and a springtime ramble over the Connecticut hills.

Picnics are featured at three of the Saturday outings and other Saturday walks take us to various points of interest in New York and New Jersey.

Several members have expressed a desire to attend the Annual Meeting in Vermont on June 1st, and we are hoping for a good representation then.

New York Section officers are: Grenville T. Anderson, president; Louise C. Goeres, vice president; Alice Burnet, secretary; Worth J. Smith, Treasurer.

LILLIAN P. LODGE

A NEW VENTURE FOR THE NEW YORK SECTION

On Saturday, March 9th, five members of the New York Section met at the entrance to the Scout Camp at Grout Pond on the Arlington-Wardsboro road, equipped with snowshoes, two pair of which had been decorating the fireplace of our Camp Thendara for as long as any of us could remember. Snowshoe trips have been missing for many years from our otherwise well-rounded schedule of outdoor activities, due to lack of sufficient snow in the New York area in recent years, and also due to the great popularity of skiing. We like to ski, too, but also like to try out something new.

The prospects seemed rather dim as we left New Jersey on Friday night in a drizzling rain, and they became even dimmer when we arrived in Bennington and found no sign of snow. We spent the night at Fay Fuller Camp and were pleasantly surprised the next morning to find six to eight inches of snow on the ground. Farther north at Stratton, about fifteen inches had accumulated and we could hardly wait to get started.

At one P.M. we started out down the unplowed portion of the Arlington road leading to the Long Trail. Only one of us had previous experience in snowshoeing. But it didn't take long before we learned you must always go forward; log bridges must be crossed sideways; and when you can't lift your left foot, it's probably because part of your right foot is on top of it. We decided it was much easier than ordinary hiking; no need to look where you put your feet down, or worry about slipping on rocks or getting wet feet.

It continued to snow most of Saturday. Poor visibility combined with blazes on which the paint had almost completely disappeared or were covered with snow, caused us to flounder around several times in an effort to locate the Trail. During the winter, you are completely dependent on the blazes, and it really makes one appreciate well painted blazes, at least at eye-level.

We arrived at Willis Ross Camp at four P.M. A stiff wind was blowing across the frozen pond. The storm obscured Stratton View Shelter at the far end but the Annex stood out cold and forlorn, making us grateful for the protection of a closer cabin. The previous visitors, Brattleboro scouts, in February, had left everything in tidy condition along with a good wood supply. By the time supper was ready, the temperature inside had risen to a comfortable 80 degrees.

The wind roared all night and deposited huge drifts all around the cabin. The next morning, our tracks had all but disappeared. By the time we had breakfast, cleaned up, and replenished the woodpile, we could see patches of blue sky through the steamed-up windows and as we started up the Trail at 10:30, the sun came out. The scenery along the Trail was breathtaking, and will be remembered for a long time: snow covered evergreens against a deep blue sky; an occasional snowshoe rabbit hopping across the Trail; the singing of a bird in the otherwise noiseless forest.

Maybe because our previous day's experience had made us more expert, but more likely because we knew lunch was waiting in the cars, we made the four mile trip out in two and one half hours. We all felt as though we had used hitherto undeveloped muscles, but still weren't half as exhausted as we were after the six hour drive back to New Jersey.

We enthusiastically recommend this trip to anyone who likes cold weather camping. We selected this particular portion of Trail because it is fairly level terrain which is ideal for snowshoeing; the distance is about right for beginners; and a closed cabin assures comfortable lodging when weather conditions are unpredictable.

Our thanks to the other members of the group, Karna Salmon, Therese Baroux, and Hylke Halbertsma for their support in this venture.

ACTIVITIES

Although Dave Otis and his outing committee have staged some grand hikes, once every two weeks and sometimes once a week, and which have included over-nights to Taft Lodge and to Wiley Lodge, the annual maple sugar party proved the most popular thus far on the schedule.

87 Green Mountain Club (Burlington Section) followers gathered around the supper table at the Colchester Grange Hall on March 30th. How good the supper was was attested to by the consumption of dozens of deviled eggs, pounds of baked ham and dozens of dishes of escalloped potato. Then everybody topped the feed off with maple sugar on snow until, as Roy Buchanan says: "they got green around the ears."

An evening of square dancing followed the supper—what fun, for old and young.

Of course there was the traditional hike before the supper up Cobble hill—a few "old faithfuls" made the climb.

KILLINGTON SECTION REPORTS

At the annual meeting of the Killington Section, president, Jack Martin suggested a unique method of checking shelters for repairs. He said that if shelters could be visited by other members than the repair crews, and a report made previous to the date of scheduled work parties, of what materials and tools were needed, more work could be accomplished. Several members volunteered to help out in this checking. All seven of the Killington Section's shelters were assigned to cooperative checkers.

In January a work party went in to Tucker Lodge, cleaned the place up, and set nine lights of glass. Another party in February did considerable work at Sunnyside. We are pleased to report that our young junior members are proving to be a big help in this shelter repair work. They have also taken over the checking of some of the shelters and refer to them as "those are our shelters." Justifiable pride goes a long way in doing good. Senior members could profit from this attitude also.

Besides the work parties, we have had the winter weekend in February, a trip to Mt. Carmel, a moonlight snowshoe hike into Tucker, a hike up Bird Mt., and a sugar on snow party in Mendon.

The Killington Section, under the chairmanship of Helen Bailey, is collecting a number of slides with the hope of soon having enough good ones to use as a slide collection for showing to groups in our area who might be interested in knowing something about our activities. About forty slides have already been selected by the committee. Duplicates are being made and will soon be ready for use.

The Killington Section officers for 1957 are: John Martin, president; Helen Bailey, vice president; Eleanor Walker, secretary-treasurer.

HOW TO KNOW FERNS

It is easy to learn how to identify ferns—much easier than flowers. But a living teacher, in a natural or planted fernery, is essential for a first lesson.

Buy "Ferns of Northeastern United States," by Farida A. Wiley, Am. Museum of Natural History, W. 78th St., New York City 24 ($1.56).

TWELVE COMMON FERNS

Bracken, Brake, Eagle
"It is found in all parts of the world, and is said to be the most common of all our North American Ferns."—Tilton

Christmas
Known and beloved by all. Moist, rich hard-woods.

Cinnamon
Their cinnamon-brown spore stalks adorn our wet pastures in May and June.

Hay-scented, or Rock Fern
Overruns our dry pastures. Encircles boulders and low lying ledges.
"Its singularly delicate, tapering, pale green fronds, curving in every direction, rank among our most beautiful and noticeable ferns."—Parsons

Interrupted
Very common along roadsides and edges of woods and meadows. The name identifies the fertile fronds. Easily cultivated.
"The Interrupted Fern is a peculiarly graceful plant."—Parsons

Lady
Very common. Almost everywhere along roadsides, shady places, upland and lowland. Easily cultivated. Spore cases crescent on lower pinnae.
"Queen of Ferns,"—Lowe

Maidenhair
"The Maidenhair, a plant which probably is more generally appreciated than all the rest of the ferns together."—Parsons

Ostrich
Like ostrich plumes. Clustered. Stiffly erect. Basal pinnae very small.
"This handsome species thrives under cultivation. For grace and dignity it is unrivalled."—Tilton

Royal
Spores on tip of center fronds. About 30 in. high. In clusters.

Sensitive
Common in wet places. Not lacy. Bead-like spore cases.
Perhaps the oldest living fern. The only modern fern found as a fossil.

Shield, Marginal. Evergreen Wood
Rocky hillsides in rich woods. Spore-dots near margin of pinnules. Evergreen. Easily cultivated—valuable.

Spinulose, Wood. Spinuloso Shield
Looks like the Lady Fern. Stem grooved, with scales. Long lower pinules.

CLARENCE P. COWLES

August 3, 1956

The Green Mountain Club, Inc.
Rutland, Vermont

Gentlemen:

I am writing this at the Rhode Island shore where Mrs. Kammerer and I are finishing up a vacation which started last Saturday with three days on the Long Trail. . . .

I know you like to hear of experiences on the Trail, and since ours this year were all so pleasant, here are some.

Saturday night we stayed at Fay Fuller Camp and enjoyed our campfire by the side of tumbling Glastonbury stream. We had stayed there in 1951 with our children. This year Mrs. Kammerer and I, were alone, and although we slept out in the field we noticed that the shelter was in good condition.

. . . As we were going in to Stratton Pond (Sunday A.M.) a very healthy, hearty group of men were coming out. This was a work party from the Worcester Section, they told us. We would like to have heard more of this matter of the enthusiasm that is generated at distant points by the Long Trail but there was no time for discussion.

At Stratton Pond we made up the last two of the eight occupants of the shelter at Willis Ross Camp Sunday night. The annex also was occupied. The log book noted that the work party we met had brought in the fine new stove in the Willis Ross shelter. We could understand better the importance of work parties as a result of our stay at Willis Ross.

Monday we climbed to the top of Stratton Mt. . . . returned to Stratton Pond, using the Stratton View shelter Monday night. Being able to watch the play of the sun and clouds on the mountain, leads me to say that this is the shelter to stay at on Stratton Pond, even if it is a quarter mile off the main Trail.

On Tuesday we returned to our car on the Wardsboro road . . . .

Our application for membership and the accompanying check are enclosed and are really just tokens of the pleasure we derived from our stay on the Trail. Please let the Worcester Section know how grateful we are to them for their work around Stratton Pond.

If all goes well, we will certainly return for further samples of the "footpath in the wilderness."

Sincerely,

GRANGER L. KAMMERER,
Hempstead, N.Y.
