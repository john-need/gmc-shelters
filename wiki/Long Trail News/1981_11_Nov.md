Intersectional A Rousing Success

By Frenchy Garfield

They descended on Camp Plymouth in Tyson from all points of the compass and participated in every kind of activity imaginable. And well before the last of the 113 participants in the 1981 GMC Intersectional packed up to go home it was obvious that the event was a smashing success.

Before the week was out GMCers had covered the Long Trail from Brandon Gap to Bromley, panned for gold in Buffalo Creek, gotten filthy dirty in the depths of Morris Cave (which is Vermont's largest), learned how to rappel and rock climb and sipped on soft drinks chilled with ice from the Pittston ice caves.

There were nature hikes led by Clint Banks and Len Daley who made their annual pilgrimage from Ohio, sightseeing trips to the Coolidge homestead and cheese factory in nearby Plymouth, canoeing on the Echo Lake chain and nearby rivers and swimming for those tough enough to brave the chill waters.

Intersectional Activities

Although no square dance caller was available, Intersectional Chairman Bob Carey lined up an outstanding array of evening programs which ranged from songfests and star gazing (we even had Northern Lights one night) to discussions of the GMC's role as host to the Appalachian Trail Conference in 1985. There wasn't one slide shown the entire week!

The annual GMC-Explorer auction, with Frenchy Garfield manning the gavel, drew more laughs than some Broadway plays and added some $90 for the GMC treasury. Items donated ranged from customized leather belts made by the Explorers of Post 2, Sudbury, to lovely straw wreaths and floral pieces made by Pat Howard of Columbia, Maryland.

Barbeque Celebrates Fiftieth

The evening highlight of the week was the Saturday evening barbeque celebrating the 50th anniversary of the completion of the Long Trail, which attracted more than 50 GMCers including Club President Jim Wilkinson of Barre and Executive Director Harry Peet.

Lincoln and Randy Meade of the Burlington Section were on hand to work the same magic over the barbeque grills that made the Connecticut Breakfast such a success at this spring's annual meeting. The piece de resistance was a huge cake decorated with a map of Vermont, the LT and a GMC patch.

Ten sections of the club were represented at the Intersectional; Worcester leading the way with 20, followed by Pioneer Valley with 16, Killington with 12 and Burlington with nine. Members at Large topped all totals with 21.

There were some personal highlights during the week. Ann Mara Scheff of Lincoln, Mass. completed an end to end trek on the LT with her brother James, Dot Knight of the PV section and Andy Howard celebrated their birthdays. They share the same date, but nobody's telling how many years apart.

Member Stricken

The final Saturday also brought a note of sadness. Charlie Royal of Schenectady, New York, attending his first Intersectional, complained of chest pains late Friday night and was driven to Rutland hospital by the camp ranger. Doctors there said he had suffered a heart attack and he was admitted.

GMC President Jim Wilkinson (R) does the honors with the help of Intersectional Chairman Bob Carey of Killington section and chef Randy Meade.

Intersectional committee members Carey, Garfield, Knight, John Kirby, Bob Poole and Hans Puck got plenty of help from the members of Explorer Post 2, Sudbury, Mass. who served as Intersectional staff for the third successive year, handling the registration and trash collection details as well as assisting with the program. Their ultimate frisbee games in the field in front of headquarters will be long remembered.

The Intersectional was a financial as well as organizational success with a healthy surplus turned back to the GMC treasury after the last bill was paid. The friendship and fellowship that everyone enjoyed on the shores of Echo Lake in Tyson will be the things that are long remembered.

Board of Directors
Meets at Camp

Keewaydin
Section Dues
Assessment Increased

by Harry Peet

On October 17th the Club's Board of Directors met at Camp Keewaydin in Salisbury, Vermont. The weather was more suited to hiking than conducting business – it was a sunny, nearly cloudless day (one of very few this autumn). Appropriately, "sunny" is also an accurate description of the overall mood of the Board. Reports of successfully completed trail and shelter projects, and a favorable report on the Club's financial situation for the current fiscal year were welcome news.

Although general optimism prevailed, concern over the Club's financial future continued to be a major topic of discussion. In noting his personal satisfaction with the Club's accomplishments and progress in the last several years, Executive Director Harry Peet added some strong words of caution. Referring to the Club's financial success last year (a $6,000 surplus) and to the projected surplus for the current fiscal year, he attributed this success to careful budgeting by the Board, very tight control of expenses by the staff, and the decrease in staff (from three full-time positions in 1979 to two full-time and one part-time position at present). Regardless of how well we continue to control expenses, he added, they will only increase. And, with the staff fully involved in day-to-day operations, there is little opportunity to investigate potential new sources of income.

Section Assessment Increased

With only-to-be-expected reluctance, the Board decided to act on a future dues increase, an action which appeared imminent two years ago but which the Board was able to postpone until now.

Effective January 1, 1983, the Section assessment for adult members will be increased from $3.00 to $4.50. This assessment is the amount each Section contributes from its treasury toward Clubwide operations.

The Board rejected a proposal to also increase the assessment for junior Section members from $1.50 to $3.00. In addition, two aspects of the Section assessment were clarified. Firstly, Sections are NOT assessed for junior family members, only for individual juniors. Secondly, Sections are NOT assessed for life members or for individuals who have been members for thirty or more years.

Implicit in this action by the Board was an intent to also increase dues for At-Large members in 1983. Action was taken at this time on the Section assessment to allow the Club's Sections sufficient time to prepare for the increase; hence the effective date of January 1, 1983.

Other Board Actions

Merrill-Lynch Government Fund: In January of this year the Board adopted a much more aggressive stance than in past regarding the investment of the Club's operating funds to generate more income. In a further move in this direction, the Board authorized the Treasurer and Executive Director to invest operating funds in the Merrill-Lynch Government Fund. Cash flow has been a difficult problem; during the first few months of the fiscal year, which include the field season, expenses always exceed income. The Merrill-Lynch Fund will generate income AND insure that our funds are available as necessary.

Committees: Clubwide committees were approved (See page 11), and specific tasks were assigned to each committee by President James Wilkinson.

Haystack Mountain Spur Trail: Acting on a request by the Montreal Section, the Board officially recognized the short spur trail which leads from the Long Trail to the summit of Haystack Mountain as part of the Long Trail system.

1982 Long Trail Walk-Through: Encouraged by the success of this year's Long Trail Walk-Through, the Board authorized the Trails & Shelters Committee to begin planning a similar one for next year.

Trail Standards: A draft of updated trail standards prepared by the Trails & Shelters Committee was approved. These standards are general policy guidelines; the Committee will be preparing trailwork specifications and maintenance guidelines in the months ahead.

Sign Standards: A draft of proposed sign standards for the Long Trail system, once again prepared by the Trails & Shelters Committee, was distributed to Board members and Section Presidents (by mail) for review and comment. Action on this draft is expected at the January 16, 1982 Board Meeting.

Bylaw Amendment: The Board voted to delay final action on a Bylaw amendment until its January meeting. The proposed amendment is to delete the Caretaker and Ranger Programs Committee from the list of standing committees. Because the functions of the Caretaker and Ranger Programs are so closely related to the Club's overall trail maintenance and management effort, and necessarily must be coordinated with the efforts of the Long Trail Patrol, it is felt that "jurisdiction" of these programs should be among the duties of the Trails & Shelters Committee.

BAMFORTH RIDGE TRAIL:
HIKE AND MEMORIES

by David P. Morse, Sr.

For forty-five years I had worked five and a half days a week and had to hope that good weather would coincide with Sunday, the one day for a hike. But this summer, things have changed, and when a day starts out like today I go hiking. The temperature at 6 a.m. is in the low fifties. The air is clear and fresh and there is still mist in the valley.

For the past several weeks I have been on Camel's Hump and plan to cover each of the approach trails.

The first trip was the Forestry Trail, which was rebuilt several years ago to stand the ever-increasing day traffic, with water bars and steps to minimize the erosion. On that trip I came down the abandoned Callahan Trail, which had been blocked off due to heavy use and erosion. I hope that it may someday be put in condition to be reopened.

The second trip was via the Dean Trail to the Long Trail and the approach to Camel's Hump from the south. It provides an almost continuous view of the Hump as one climbs over successive rock ledges. This is probably the most exciting approach.

Today it will be the Bamforth Ridge Trail. This was originally the Long Trail, but due to problems of crossing the Winooski River, the Long Trail was rerouted through Honey Hollow, over Robbins Mountain to the bridge at Jonesville. This trail was renamed in memory of Eugene L. Bamforth at that time. Gene had spent many years in trail work and was especially fond of Camel's Hump. He often attended our Montpelier Section planning meetings with suggestions for hikes to be made and things that needed to be done, but he preferred to hike alone, and we would often meet him coming the other way on one of our trips. If we had an overnight at one of the shelters, we would find that he had been there before us and the shelter would be spotless. Especially in the Camel's Hump area he made sure there were trail signs at the intersections.

I remember one winter trip we found a sizeable igloo had been built against the side of the Lodge. This was a social and eating trip, and it was cold and very windy. We set up a charcoal grill in the entrance to the igloo and cooked a steak for this Montpelier Section's outing. We had our Christmas party there, but in recent years have changed the location to Duck Brook shelter.

I feel fortunate to have met Roy Buchanan for whom the lodge was named. He was the founder of the Long Trail Patrol and for 36 years its active leader. When our log cabin at Sterling Pond burned down in 1972, it was his plans that were used for the open shelter that the Montpelier Section built to replace it. Prof. Buchanan came up to give his help and advice, although he was then in his eighties.

Starting off at 7:30 on a relocation of the first mile of the trail (which I had not been on since its completion), I found the trail excellent, with substantial water bars and bridges over the streams. It joined the old trail just below the site of the former Buchanan Lodge at a lovely spot on Gleason Brook. The relocation had eliminated a dangerous crossing of the brook where it tumbled into a cascade.

Here I paused to reflect on memories of several trips to Buchanan Lodge. It burned down a few years ago. Nothing remains but the foundation stones, and it will probably not be rebuilt, since this is no longer the Long Trail.

The trail climbs steeply at points, but continues to come out on open rock outcroppings where Camel's Hump is seen, closer at each one. Several times it descends into ravines between cliffs and climbs to a new height on the further side. It must be remembered that although the difference in height from the Winooski River to the top of the Hump is about 3600 feet, the climb down into the ravines and up again must add another 500 feet to the vertical ascent, and that it will not be all down hill coming back.

The trail is nearly dry with just a few mucky spots. The blazes are not fresh, especially on the open rock, but can be followed.

Again a memory of a past trip. It was early spring. My son, David, and several friends had spent the night at Buchanan Lodge (it was then Wiley Lodge). I joined them to climb the Hump. Much of the trail was clear of snow, but there were pockets waist deep in each ravine. On the way back we were running and jumping as far across the snow pockets as we could. My knee hit a sharp rock under the snow, and when I climbed out the lower part of my pants leg was turning red. Fortunately I had not hit the kneecap square on, but had opened quite a gash. By the time I had walked the four miles back to the road, my leg and foot were starting to swell. I went straight to the hospital and had eight stitches.

Today, being alone, I am careful. It is apparent from the register that this trail is not getting much use. It could be a while before someone comes along. I do leave at home a plan of my intended trip and sign it at the registers, so that if I am overdue it should not be hard to find me.

I remember another trip of the Montpelier Section, an overnight to Gorham Lodge. It was August, very hot and humid, and there was no water on the Bamforth Ridge Trail. Some of our party turned back. There is little today from Gleason Brook to Gorham Lodge, but at Gorham the water is cold and "so good", as it was that day.

The trail crew has cut and skinned large trees to rebuild the base logs of the lodge which are giving way. It looks like quite a task and a delicate one, to jack up the log building on the ledge that it sits on and install new logs.

Another memory comes to mind: an overnight at Gorham. The rain began about half way up the Callahan Trail, and our party of about eight were pretty wet by the time we reached the lodge. It was already occupied by six or seven people. While we were getting supper, just about dark, another party of eight arrived, severely taxing the normal bunk space for 14. Wet clothes were hung everywhere. The table had to be moved out to make room for sleeping bags lined up like cord wood on the floor.

Leaving this spot, the trail climbs through open woods for .7 miles. In the lower elevations the vireos are abundant, shouting their demands. Occasionally an ovenbird or a thrush is heard. The warblers are silent for the most part, although I see a few. Higher up I hear the clear notes of the white-throated sparrow. I have them at home, but it seems that only at the high elevation are their notes so clear.

There are many mushrooms. Big yellow and orange ones. Someday I am going to learn to identify at least a few of the edible ones.

Here there is a look-out called Duxbury Window. It is closed in somewhat and should have a few trees trimmed out to keep the view of the Worcester Range and the valley between.

As I continue to climb, the next lookout is to the west, over the Winooski Valley and Lake Champlain. White Face Mountain, Giant, and the cone of Mt. Marcy can be seen in the Adirondacks across the lake.

The Long Trail Patrol "Celebrates" Its Fiftieth Birthday

Much has been made of the fact that 1981 marks the fiftieth anniversary of the completion of the Long Trail. Until now, however, little mention has been made of another fact: 1981 is also the Long Trail Patrol's fiftieth birthday!

For a combination of reasons the anniversary of the Long Trail completion passed by rather quietly. There were no parades (with the significant exception of End-to-Enders parading their way from Massachusetts to Canada!). And, unlike the celebration of 1931, no signal fires illuminated the higher peaks of the Green Mountains. In typical GMC fashion, however, thousands of hours of trail and shelter maintenance were performed by Club volunteers, making 1981 a particularly successful field season.

The Long Trail Patrol certainly contributed to that success, and, in typical LTP fashion, did celebrate its fiftieth birthday with a very special "party": a work party. In mid-August the Long Trail Patrol played an important role in completing a major renovation of Gorham Lodge.

Gorham Lodge: Some Background

The future of Gorham Lodge has been a topic of concern for several years. The need for and appropriateness of a high-elevation shelter on Camel's Hump has been discussed and debated both within the Club and with the Vermont Department of Forests, Parks and Recreation. The Club's position, as stated in its Long Trail System Management Plan for Camel's Hump State Park and Forest Reserve, is that a high-elevation shelter on Camel's Hump provides two vital functions: it allows for better management and protection of the mountain's summit area, and it enhances hiker safety. A final verdict on the long-term future of Gorham Lodge will be made when the State of Vermont completes its management planning process. In this process the Club's Management Plan will receive careful consideration.

Short-term considerations set the stage for the renovation. Deterioration of Gorham Lodge was quite apparent and required immediate attention. Rotted logs, especially the sills, had caused the entire structure to shift, and it had acquired a very noticeable lean. So noticeable in fact that hikers sitting at the window bench inside the lodge had a choice of views; they could look out at a marvelous panorama of the Worcester Mountains, or, by tilting their heads, could gaze apprehensively at the ground almost directly beneath them.

In early June of this year a final inspection of the lodge was carried out by the Trails & Shelters Committee. The decision was made, based upon that inspection, that Gorham could, and should, be repaired as soon as possible.

After completing another important project, the redecking of the suspension bridge over Clarendon Gorge, the Long Trail Patrol moved north to Camel's Hump. The replacement logs needed were cut, peeled, and transported to Gorham. Then, during the first week of August, lumber for the floor and lower bunk (which would be torn out when the rotted logs were replaced) was packed up the Hump. Off the mountain, other preparations proceeded. Members of the Trails & Shelters Committee, notably Herb Reed, procured the bolts, clamps, cable, and special tools which would be needed.

Work on the lodge itself commenced August 10th. Cables were attached to the upper part of the structure to "hold it together" while the rotted logs were replaced. Then, each of the old logs was pulled out and a new one installed. In all, eight logs, including the four sills, were replaced. BRATTLEBORO SECTION

Joe Cook, Dorothy MacDonald, John Elliot and I were determined to get George Roy up Mt. Isolation, his 46th and last White Mountain "4000-footer". Isolation is a mere bump on the Montalban Ridge running south from Mt. Washington, but at 4005 feet it qualifies for the AMC's sacred list. So it had to be ascended. Saturday, Aug. 1, we met George on Route 16, four miles south of Pinkham Notch Camp. Under sunny skies, we followed Rocky Branch and Isolation trails to the David Path at the site of a former shelter. We pitched our tents there before strolling up Mt. Isolation.

Isolation's major virtue is its bare summit. Against the clear evening sky, the cluster of buildings on Washington four miles away looked like a mosque. But our attention naturally focused on George. This was, after all, his final 4000-footer, demanding celebration. I deployed a bottle of champagne. So did Dorothy; so did Joe; so did John! Four bottles of bubbly, split five ways, made it hard to arrange a group picture, but the result justified the effort. (See cut.) We floated back to camp.

With good weather continuing Sunday, we ascended Washington via Davis Path. After lunch, I suggested descending by the Huntington Ravine Trail which I had walked up (but never down) some 45 years ago. "Steep and rocky; exciting, but not really dangerous," I assured the others. At the first pitch down the headwall I swallowed those words. It was dangerous, especially with backpacks and without rope. Fortunately John was an experienced rock climber. He talked us down. Time and again he went back for packs we had abandoned. Thanks to him, we did get down safely. But we strongly recommend putting a sign at the top of the ravine: "DO NOT DESCEND THIS TRAIL UNLESS YOU ARE A ROCK CLIMBER".

OTTAUQUECHEE SECTION

Restoration of Luce's Lookout Planned

For hikers plodding along the lesser-known Appalachian Trail section that heads eastward toward New Hampshire, having left the hustle and bustle of the Long Trail a good day's walk behind at Sherburne Pass, a small sign along the Trail pointing left and stating "lookout - 300 ft." may seem a welcome but probably minor diversion. But those that take the extra few minutes are in for a surprise: at a forest clearing reached in 200 feet is a welcome sign, a register box, and a 30-gallon sealed plastic water jug (with spigot and cup provided); and up a couple hundred foot scramble beyond is a 23-foot steel tower offering a 360 degree panorama of the Connecticut River valley sweeping from the Franconias in New Hampshire to the Green Mountain crest.

This is Luce's Lookout, and despite the small sign and out-of-the-way location, its an area of rich tradition. In 1894 Edwin Luce and his son Horatio, owners of a nearby farm, constructed the first tower here (it was 40 feet tall, all of wood, and quite a local sensation), and about 1930 GMC's tireless trailbuilder, Willis Ross, laid out the AT route to pass by this impressive landmark. After the old tower had collapsed the current steel structure was erected in 1956 but never finished, and interest waned as the area grew up in underbrush.

Had it not been for Dr. and Mrs. Frank Barnard, for the past 18 years owners of nearby Lookout Farm and the land upon which the Trail and tower stand, the warm welcome, the cold water, the register filled with Trail gossip, and perhaps even access to the tower itself would not have been there. Now that Dr. and Mrs. Barnard have sold Lookout Farm and retired to a smaller home in Woodstock village, the Ottauquechee Section is making plans to secure a licensing agreement with the new owner, restore the tower with a wooden platform and access ladder, and maintain the Barnard's hospitality to Trail users.

Its a big challenge, to be sure, but part of an 87-year tradition we think well worth maintaining.

PRESTON BRISTOW, Reporter

46 KV Power Line Proposed for Killington/Pico Area

— Position Statement —

46 KV POWER TRANSMISSION CIRCUIT PROPOSED FOR THE KILLINGTON/PICO AREA

The Green Mountain Club, a non-profit organization founded in 1910 to "make the mountains of Vermont play a larger role in the lives of the people" through creation of the Long Trail and other Vermont hiking trails, including 115 miles of the Appalachian Trail, view with concern any proposal which might impact the natural and scenic qualities of the Green Mountains. Recognized by the Vermont General Assembly as the "founder, sponsor, defender, and Protector" of Vermont's Long Trail System, acknowledged by Federal authorities as manager of this portion of the Appalachian National Scenic Trail, and with an annual membership of over 4000, the Green Mountain Club speaks for hikers and hiking trails in the State of Vermont.

BE IT RESOLVED by the Board of Directors of the Green Mountain Club:

(1) The Green Mountain Club supports the use of existing highway, railroad, transmission line, and other developed corridors to meet future energy transmission needs whenever technically feasible, rather than the creation of new energy transmission corridors, and urges use of the existing Route 4 corridor for the proposed Killington/Pico area 46 KV transmission circuit as a means of reducing the adverse impact on the Long and Appalachian Trails.

BACKPACKER'S PALSY

Modern Medicine magazine reports that backpackers who try to carry too much weight too frequently may develop a condition dubbed "backpacker's palsy." The nerves in backpackers' shoulders are compressed by the loads they carry to the point that they experience numbness, weakness and atrophy of the arms and shoulders. One patient who carried half his body weight for three years did not improve during the year he stopped backpacking and may suffer weakness for years to come.

Other backpackers with less severe cases may find improvement after several months of no backpacking, heavy lifting or strenuous exercise.

(Sent by Joe Sposta, USFS)
