BOB HUMES FINISHES THE TRAIL

When Bob Humes, now our vice president, took his first trip on the Long Trail in 1954, he was a new member of the Club, and accompanied members of the Worcester Section on a work party to Stratton Pond. Like others who first take a few short trips on the Trail, the completion of the entire Long Trail was only a remote possibility.

But in the following years, with the fine companionship of Wayne Baldwin, Bob Attenborough, Jim Green and others, Bob found himself doing a systematic coverage of long distances on the Trail. Now with the mileages fast accumulating, the End-to-End certificate became his goal.

A unique food supply plan used during these long trips enabled Bob and his companions to travel with lighter packs and without the necessity of leaving the Trail to buy food in nearby villages. In advance of the hikes, food supplies were buried at convenient locations in plastic-lined five-gallon cans.

Finally, in July of 1962, plans were made for the final trip needed for completion of the Trail. Recruiting Ray Secor, a new GMC'er from Schenectady, for a partner, Bob hiked up the logging road to Swezey Shelter for the night.

Heading northward, the next night was spent at Bromley Camp, and the next night at Griffith Shelter. On Friday the 13th, Bob's lucky day, they reached USFS Route 10 and Bob became our 117th End-to-Ender. That night was spent at Little Rock Pond Shelter.

Now let us quote from the final page of Bob's excellent report: "The next morning, Ray and I hiked down the Homer Stone Brook Trail, to be greeted just above South Wallingford by Ben and Dorothy Rolston. Ben then led us to a beautiful field overlooking the valley and Vermont countryside, and, after a pineapple juice toast, presented me with my End-to-End certificate. What a nice ending to the wonderful years of working toward completion of the Long Trail. There are parts of the Trail I want to do again, and camps I want to visit again. Time will never erase from my memory the call of the wilderness, and the beauty of Vermont on the Long Trail."

LONG TRAIL PATROL REPORT

The Patrol gang was planned to consist of three men for the whole season. However one man spent 16 days in the hospital and another man was out for 5 days because of a death in his immediate family so that there were but two men working for 21 days. There were three men working for the rest of the time. Much work was accomplished even though there was more rain than usual.

The Patrol, as usual, covered all of the trail from Chilcoat Pass to Canada. They made necessary repairs to all of the camps and cleared the side trails in this region. The trails were thoroughly "brushed" (mowed) from Ithiel Falls on the Lamoille River to Parker Camp while the other trails were "speed cleared." That is, the blow downs were cut out and confusing places were gone over to lessen the confusion (we hope).

The Appalachian Trail from Long Trail Lodge to the Barnard Gulf road was "speed cleared" to lessen the danger of losing the hordes (?) of hikers who were milling about the entrance of the A.T.

Cooper Lodge was badly damaged last winter with all of its glass (96 panes) smashed. The Patrol worked there on July 2 and 3. They took the windows out and put hen fence over these openings. ("If the public wants it to be an open front camp, let them have it so.") They moved the dump away from the spring. (We hope that the public likes pure water) and they built a new toilet. Later Ben Rolston put plastic screen over the camp's windows openings so that now the wind has no better entrance at Cooper than the porkies do.

Early in July all of the New York trails were "speed cleared" and its camps were repaired where they needed it so that this very popular region was in good shape before the less heavily traveled parts of the trail were cleared.

Wiley Lodge was the worst vandalized of the N.Y. camps this year. It had 11 panes of glass broken and its floor was badly chopped up. Both the glass and the floor were replaced.

BURLINGTON SECTION
Up The Trail Apiece

Since the last issue of the Long Trail News some of our Burlington Section members have given good accounts of themselves. During the 3rd Intersectional August Camp at Grout Pond two of our members completed the Green Mountain Club End-to-Ender qualifications. First Harris Abbott completed his at Little Rocky Pond and Elsie Washburn completed her qualification the following day on the Bromley trip out from Camp. Betty Myers completed the End-to-Ender project a couple of weeks before the Intersectional. Fred Smith qualified for the Adirondack 46er Club during the Burlington Section's Sawteeth Mt. trip in the Adirondack on Sept. 23rd.

The Intersectional Week-end, at Grout Pond, was attended by twenty-one from the local section. They reported a grand stay and all take their "hats off" to Don Kent for the job he did as this year's chairman of the event. Our group will be showing up in goodly numbers for the next years camp—you can count on us again. The foxes and Tommy Lane's pet coon gave the event an interesting addition that will long be remembered.

Some of the "high adventure" listings from our weekly outing schedule were: The Macintyre range trip in early July when two of Mountain Club of Maryland, Connie and Alice Roesslers, joined us. The Zwicks from Rochester were also there (Daan was former caretaker at Taft Lodge on Mt. Mansfield for a couple or more summers.)

Then there was the beach party at the Thomas's Camp followed by the White Mt. trip led by our section president, George Saunders.

On Sept. 9th, 42 of us gathered at the Underhill Half Way House State Park on the West side of Mt. Mansfield to help Andy Buchanan eat "roasted" sweet corn. A large number of the group had wound their way up to the now famous Cantilever Rock.

The section also went far afield to climb a new mountain, Cardigan Mt. in central New Hampshire. They found the smooth rocky dome of the 3121 ft. mountain has a view well worth any distance traveled ... try it sometime and see for yourself. This little mountain has for years been one of the interesting points for the Appalachian Mt. Club to climb.

Has anyone heard of the "Middlebury Puffers"? They are a group located around the Middlebury area that have developed a series of outings that are really "puffers." The Burlington Section is proud to present this group which we also sort of claim as near kins of us since Betty Myers and Elsie Washburn have been the sharpest prodders of the endeavor.

LARRY DEAN, Reporter

Taft Lodge, in the Burlington Section, was extensively repaired. The bottom front log was badly rotted so it was replaced across the door and a few feet each side of the door. Also the front half of the roof leaked a good deal so this was repaired with new roll roofing.

One day was spent at Sterling Pond Camp where last winter's vandals had used the table and both top bunks for firewood.

A week was spent in "brushing" the trail from Taylor Lodge to Puffer Camp. This difficult piece of trail maintenance had been needed for several years.

Battell's roofing was torn in many places and the front roof boards were rotted. New boards were brought in for the front part of the roof and new roll roofing was put on the whole roof.

Several days were spent at Glastenbury where the old GMC Camp was abandoned and the fire wardens hut was repaired so that we hope that it will serve for some years to come. No work was done on the Glastenbury tower.

The truck was used for various purposes around the area of the 1962 Intersectional at Grout Pond and then it went into its "winter quarters" at Bennington.

While other work was being done from Ithiel Falls on the Lamoille River to Parker Camp and from Hazens Notch to Jay-Montgomery Road the trail blazes were repainted for those distances.

ROY BUCHANAN

Annual Fall Get-Together:

October 4th was the first dinner meeting at which over eighty members and guests buzzed about their wonderful summer vacations. Following dinner a few members showed slides of their eventful trips.

Sam Wilkinson went to the AMC August Camp in the Tetons and through his pictures explained the tragic accident that occurred there last summer.

Harry Smith visited the AMC Cold River Camp in New Hampshire and showed the camp facilities as well as scenic trips in the area.

Marian West took us on a trip to Alaska where she visited her daughter, Bunny Beaman, and family at their summer fish camp. Bunny was an active member until she went to Alaska to teach and then chose the adventurous life of a 'Homesteader.'

Peggy Pons presented slides showing a quick 1300 mile circle tour of Ireland. Due to a ten day limit, a Volkswagon and hotels replaced the usual "foot-power" and "campsites" of the other travelers.

Bob Major showed excellent slides of his trip through Greece. With a pack on his back and a camera in his hand Bob not only took historically interesting pictures but the human interest shots were great. Such a person on this type of trip becomes an ambassador of good will for Uncle Sam.

The topic for the next dinner meeting in November will be "Hitch Hiking in Africa." A young man will tell of his experiences of hiking and hitching for many miles in Africa with a pack on his back.

BERNICE HUDSON

WORCESTER SECTION NEWS

As in the past, many of Worcester Section activities center around the Camp at Cedar Meadows, Leicester. Family picnics were held every Wednesday evening through the summer, as well as week-end campouts in July and August. Camping trips to Lake Sunapee, New Hampshire and the Intersectional in August, were highlights of the summer. Worcester Sect. had at least 29 registered at the Intersectional campout. Other day hikes to Harvard Forest, Petersham, Mass. and Mt. Wachusett were held. The best attended of our meetings was of course an "eating meeting," namely the Sugar-on-Snow party late in the Spring when over 100 attended.

The Worcester Section trail crew has been active, keeping trails and shelters around Stratton in shape for hikers, and 62 Intersectional. Work was done to preserve Webster shelter a few more years, by repairing the roof and replacing a bunk. Plans are in the works to relocate portion of the L. T. north of Arlington-West Wardsboro road to the west side of East Br. Deerfield R. This should avoid the muddy route over the lumbering road in toward Stratton Pond.

OUT ON THE TRAIL

In our meanderings on the Long Trail this summer, our attention has been drawn to the impressive amount of long-distance hiking that has been taking place. As an example, during a week end in the Clarendon-Cold River area in August, we met a total of eleven hikers.

But the unusual fact was that ALL of these eleven hikers were taking long trips, with the shortest trip being 48 miles. Three were going from Route 4 to Route 11. One hiker was going from Route 9 to Route 4. And the remaining seven hikers, in three separate groups, were covering the entire distance between Massachusetts and Sherburne Pass, either northbound or southbound. We were pleased to learn that three of these hikers were from Canada, and one from the West Indies.

Register books never cease to fascinate us. Stopped to look through our favorite register located in an isolated spot along the Cold River. Over 50 hikers signed the book during July, and over 80 during August. Once again, Massachusetts provided the greatest number of hikers. Also represented were Vermont, Connecticut, New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland, Virginia, Canada and the West Indies.

Met an interesting gal the other day at Deer View Shelter. A Florida resident, she left Georgia in June and has been heading north on the Appalachian Trail. She hoped to keep going until stopped by snow, probably in the White Mountains.

We surely enjoy the nice letters we get from hikers. Some offer constructive criticism and suggestions for improving the conditions of the Trail and the shelters. This information is very helpful. But many hikers just write to tell us of their pleasant experiences, and to express their appreciation of the Long Trail. One recent letter contained a check for a membership-at-large, noting, "I think it is only fair that I should make some contribution to the Green Mountain Club to further its excellent purposes."

One hiker, who was plagued with an excess of rain on his trip, wrote, "I learned why the Long Trail, in wet weather, is known to hikers as 'A Footbath in the Wilderness!'"

Another wrote, "We are glad we completed the entire Trail. In retrospect, the wild, mist-shrouded and foot-weary stretches, such as that over the two un-named peaks north of Jay, silent except for the call of a white-throat, served to draw us closer to what we choose to call the true heart of Vermont."

Why do people hike, and climb mountains? This question was answered so nicely by a Montreal hiker whom we met at Clement Shelter. "I love to get out on the Trail. It helps to iron out the wrinkles from my soul."

As we close these musings, there is a nip of frost in the air. Yes, the 1962 hiking season is drawing to a close. It has been a good season, and we have been blessed with many fine trips on the Trail. We are thankful. When our Trail gets its mantle of white, will you join us on snowshoes for a glimpse of winter's beauty?

BEN ROLSTON

A DO-IT-OURSELVES PROJECT

It is planned to place signs at both ends of each side trail and also on the Long Trail near each road crossing. The signs will be on heavy paper and will be used to give facts about our Club and the Trail to hikers. The drawing gives the approximate dimensions of boards to hold these signs and give them some protection from the weather. 200 or more of these boards will be needed, painted green. So here is a winter project that will use up some of those pieces of plank that some of our do-it-yourselfers have lying around in their workshops. We will need volunteers next spring, too, to put up these boards and signs. Please let us know how many of the boards you will provide.
