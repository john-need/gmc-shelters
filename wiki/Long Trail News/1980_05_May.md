New Executive Director
Is Harry Peet

Your Green Mountain Club has a new executive director, and he's a man familiar to many GMC'ers and already long-experienced with the Club, both as employee and volunteer.

That's Harry T. Peet, Jr., whose appointment as the Club's top administrator was confirmed by the Board of Directors March 15. He succeeds Stephen K. Rice, who had resigned effective Feb. 15 to take a new position with the Appalachian Mountain Club.

Peet, 34, had been serving as the Club's first field supervisor since Jan. 1, 1978. President Doug James named him acting executive director pending selection of Rice's successor. A Search Committee screened 115 applications for the job, interviewed eight finalists and re-interviewed three of these before selecting Peet for its recommendation. James made the initial appointment pending confirmation by the Board, which was heartily given.

Members of the Search Committee, who put in extensive effort on this matter, were Vice President James E. Wilkinson, Jr. and Directors Andrew Nuquist, Laurence Van Meter, Robert Grenier and Keith Edson.

Hailing from East Hartford, Conn., Peet graduated from Trinity College in Hartford in 1968 with a Bachelor of Science degree in physics. College physics became applied physics for the next two years as Peet worked as an experimental test engineer for Pratt & Whitney Aircraft. Concurrently he completed seven courses at Trinity College towards a Master of Science degree in physics. A variety of other jobs followed until, and overlapping with, his early employment with the GMC.

That began the summer of 1972 when Peet served as caretaker at Little Rock Pond Shelter. In 1973, 1976 and 1977 he was caretaker supervisor and in 1975 he worked as a ranger-naturalist on Camel's Hump and Mt. Mansfield. Since 1978, as noted, he has served in the then newly-created position of field supervisor.

On a volunteer basis he was a member of the Club's Board of Directors representing at-large members from 1975 to 1977, and directed the caretaker program from 1974 to 1977. A major volunteer effort was his service as editor of the 21st edition of the Guide Book of the Long Trail of 1977, a comprehensive revision.

Peet is single and has resided in Montpelier since the Club moved its offices there from Rutland in early 1978. He lists reading, music, sports, science, and military strategy and tactics as spare time interests.

PRESIDENT'S MESSAGE

As reported elsewhere in this issue, your Board of Directors voted unanimously to hire Harry Peet as Executive Director. I would like to add my personal endorsement to that action; I am looking forward to working with Harry in his new capacity and am confident that he will continue to represent the Club with the same dedication and diligence he has exhibited in past volunteer and staff roles.

While a technicality prevented the Board from acting on a proposed dues increase for 1980, the Club will likely be faced with that prospect for 1981. By skillful manipulation of expenditures, primarily in the filling of vacancies in the GMC office, a viable budget for this year without a dues increase was presented and approved. However, it is a bare minimum statement of support to the commitment the Club has made to its members, cooperating partners, and the public. In the short term, we will be monitoring income and expense even more closely than in the recent past, aware that adjustments may have to be made as the year progresses. In the longer term, we are evaluating our current field program activity with thoughts of making it more cost effective, while still maintaining the standard for which the GMC has become known. Whether revaluation of our field and other programs can result in reduced budgetary requirements in the face of rampantly-rising costs remains to be seen; hence the prospect for larger funding needs next year.

Reducing the cost of the Club's operations while maintaining the effectiveness of our programs will necessarily require more volunteer help, a fact that is recognized in our reassessment of the field programs. The Club has been fortunate to have a strong volunteer support for seventy years now, and it is the continuation of such support that will see the Club continue to prosper.

DOUG JAMES

Board Adopts Budget, AT Resolution

By Robert L. Hagerman, Editor

Despite inflationary pressures, the GMC Board of Directors has adopted what might be termed a "hold-the-line" budget for the coming year. It happened, however, more by necessity than by choice.

Approved by the Board at its meeting March 15 was a 1980-81 budget of $104,747 for both income and expenditures. That is about $3,000 less than the current fiscal year's budget of $107,600, and an even larger reduction from actual income and expenditures for the current year (which runs through April 30). That is now expected to end in a deficit of about $3,800.

The $104,747 figure didn't however, start out that way. The Club's Budget and Finance Committee had expected to submit a balanced budget proposal of $110,842. To produce the additional needed income, a key element of the Committee's recommendation was to have been that membership dues be increased for the 1980-81 year. Shortly before the Board meeting, however, the Budget and Finance Committee became aware that a requirement of the Club's bylaws relating to dues would prevent the proposed increase from taking effect, even if the full Board had approved it at this time. So with that avenue of additional revenue blocked, the Committee, with Executive Director Harry Peet's help, developed the reduced budget figure that was ultimately adopted.

One expense item eliminated was $1,500 for what was to have been a special membership solicitation mailing. And a particularly significant change related to the salaried staff. Following Peet's recommendation, a replacement of himself as field supervisor will not be hired until the second half of the fiscal year, that is, in November. Partially offsetting that major reduction in salary expense will be the hiring for the 1980 summer season of a supervisor of northern caretakers as well as one for the southern caretaker. Under the original budget proposal the northern supervisor was to have been dropped, leaving the field supervisor to handle that element of supervision directly (and see more details about this and other field programs in the article elsewhere by Peet).

While agreeing to the change, the Board expressed concern over the extra workload this may mean for Peet.

So while GMC'ers will not be faced with higher dues this coming year, dollar increases will occur in other areas, one of them for Club members and two of them for non-members. The retail price of both Guide Book of the Long Trail and Day Hiker's Guide to Vermont has been increased from $4.95 to $5.50. The special price to GMC'ers of $3.50 for each will hold, however - now a solid 34.53 discount. And fees at shelters manned by caretakers will increase from $1.00 to $1.50 per night for non-members, and from $.50 to $.75 for members. Some directors, feeling hikers themselves should bear a larger financial responsibility for their use of the Long Trail system, thought the increase should have been more, i.e. to $2.00 and $1.00 respectively. But a motion to that effect was defeated.*

Members of the Budget and Finance Committee presenting the budget recommendations were James E. Wilkinson, Jr., chairman, and Preston Bristow, Jr., Clifford Thatcher and Donald Wallace.

Appalachian Trail Funds

In other action, the Board approved without dissent a resolution to go to appropriate Congressional committees which urges that "substantially higher" appropriations be made for the Appalachian National Scenic Trail project. This is, of course, the project authorized by Congress in 1978 for acquisition of a trail corridor for the 2,000-mile-long Appalachian Trail. Preston Bristow, Jr., who sponsored the resolution, has been working as the coordinator of the project for the Vermont part of the AT, a part of which is superimposed on the southern third of the Long Trail. He explained that where $45 million had originally been proposed for this year's appropriation, that figure has now dropped to $9 million under President Carter's recent budget-cutting proposals.

As adopted, the resolution urges the Congressional committees "to seriously consider a maximum level for the Appalachian National Scenic Trail project." The resolution cites two grounds for this position. One is that the AT project represents a one-time capital investment, to be followed by basically volunteer management of the trail system, i.e. not a continuing federal bureaucracy and the expense thereof. The other is that much progress has not been made in Vermont in selecting a corridor and in working out arrangements with private landowners for purchase of land or conveyances or easements not executed because of lack of federal funds to pay for them. Such compensation, the resolution urges, should be done "in a timely manner."

AT Management Plan

Related to the above, the Board reviewed a draft of a management plan for that portion of the AT for which the GMC is responsible. That includes the 98 miles of LT/AT plus 18 miles of the AT alone, east of where it leaves the LT in Sherburne Pass.

The draft was developed by the Club's Long Range Planning Committee and basically written and presented to the Board by the Committee's chairman, Larry Van Meter. It sets out general management principles relating to both the physical trail - the trail proper, overnight facilities, signs, trailheads, water and sewage control - and what is termed "The Social Trail." The latter principals deal with GMC relationships with public agencies and private organizations on Trail matters, and with the Trail users themselves - their education, safety and protection. This part of the proposed plan also deals with other recreational uses of the AT, and with uses of land abutting the AT.

Based on comments made at the Board meeting and on others expected to be received in written form, the Long Range Planning Committee will revise its draft plan and present a proposed final version for Board action at its meeting in May. The GMC's plan for its part of the AT will then go to the Appalachian Trail Conference. The AT will incorporate it with plans from other ATC member clubs into a comprehensive management plan for the whole AT, this in cooperation with the National Park Service (NPS).

Shelter Resurvey Completed In Part of GMNF
By Ray T. Auger

Shortly after my term as Southern Caretaker Supervisor ended this past summer, Field Supervisor Harry Peet asked if I would like to work on a Shelter Survey Inspection Plan with Green Mountain National Forest representatives, as the GMC representative on the project. I enthusiastically replied "yes." The field work began shortly after I finished working as the Stratton Pond Fall Caretaker. But before I describe the project, let me tell you about two past surveys.

The first took place in 1972 when an inventory of all Long Trail shelters and sanitary facilities within the proclamation boundary of the Green Mountain National Forest was made. Its primary purpose was to identify potential and obvious pollution problems. The data were collected and put in book form by GMNF and Back Country Research Project personnel of the U.S. Forest Service (USFS).

In 1975 Steve Rice, later to be the GMC's executive director, undertook a similar survey of the entire Long Trail for the Back Country Research Project. In addition to updating the 1972 inventory, his 1975 survey included data on the impact of increased hiker use, and considered on-site management techniques such as stationing of caretakers, seeding, and fertilization. Steve took a "pan" photograph of each shelter site (several shots that together make up a panoramic view).

The survey I worked on took place in the Manchester Ranger District of the GMNF and included all shelters and primitive camping areas between the Massachusetts line and Vermont Rt. 140. As stated before, it was a joint USFS-GMC project, with Forest Technician Nort Philips and Soil Scientists Fred Putnam and Peg Harris acting as Forest Service representatives, and this writer as GMC representative. Patterned after Steve's survey, the 1979 project was expanded to include additional site photos, a more complete listing of the soils, hydrology, and vegetation of each area, and to critically review the number and locations of shelters. One long-term goal of the survey is to minimize the labor and dollar strains which the shelters are placing on the responsible maintaining organizations.

After some preliminaries, actual field work took place in late October - during which time it either snowed or we experienced record-breaking hot temperatures! Would you believe 87° on October 23? Each site was visited by Fred and Peg who collected data on the soils, hydrology, major and minor species of vegetation, percent of canopy cover, vegetation condition, and potential sites for new toilets. While this was being done, Nort and I took notes on the shelter and toilet conditions and took a "pan" photo (in color) of each site, repeating Steve's original camera positions in most cases. In addition, we photographed each toilet, some of the more popular campsites near the shelters, and, in some cases, water sources.

Processing and compilation of the data and photos then followed. Fred and Peg worked in Rutland, Nort in Manchester Center, and myself in Montpelier, with periodic get-togethers to discuss progress. My work included drawing transparent scale maps, each of which shows a shelter site and its structures, trails, landmarks, and future toilet locations. These maps can be used as overlays with previous maps to note changes or lack of change at each site. Then along with Steve Rice, Harry Peet, and Forest Service representative Bob Pramuk, the project team prepared short- and long-term recommendations for each site. The compiled data were collected in early December, typed, and put in book form by Christmas. For those who wish to see them, copies of the three shelter surveys described above are on file at the GMC office.

One encouraging observation noted in the field was that almost all the shelter sites showed a good vegetation regrowth since the 1975 survey. This is quite apparent when you compare a 1975 "pan" photo with a 1979 one.

In the next few months, survey recommendations will be presented to the appropriate GMC sections for discussion so that GMC-USFS annual work plans can be prepared. An expansion of the survey to include those shelters in the Middlebury and Rochester Ranger Districts is planned for the summer of 1980. Hopefully, the remainder of the Long Trail will be completed as a GMC Trails and Shelters Committee project.

Editor's Note: Since Ray prepared the above in January, the GMC Board of Directors started a review of the shelter recommendations at its March 15 meeting. But final action was tabled pending further input from others, notably the several GMC sections affected.

LETTER TO THE EDITOR

Sir:

Several members of the Bread Loaf Section have been concerned for some time about the position taken by the Club on our behalf regarding the protection of wilderness in Vermont. After discussions among themselves, these members requested a special section meeting to consider this matter. The meeting was held on March 22 and about 40 members attended. A resolution, the text of which follows, was put before the section and was adopted without dissent:

"The Bread Loaf Section of the Green Mountain Club believes that the designation of segments of the Green Mountain National Forest as legally-protected wilderness would be both consistent with and supportive of the Club's objectives of protecting the Long Trail as a "footpath in the wilderness" and preserving the primitive environment of the Long Trail system. Furthermore, we are satisfied that granting such status to land over which the Long Trail passes need not necessitate the removal of, or require adoption of a policy of non-replacement of, trail shelters. We therefore request the Board of Directors to modify its existing position so as to favor in principle the designation of additional wilderness on the Green Mountain National Forest, particularly for those identified roadless tracts through which the Long Trail passes. We also express the hope that the Club will cooperate with other interested organizations to ensure that any proposed legislation protects the Long Trail shelters and is otherwise in accordance with the Club's goals."

In discussion preceding the vote, it became clear that the overwhelming sentiment of the members present is that the Club should seize the opportunity which exists to assure the preservation of the natural character of segments of the Long Trail and the Green Mountains before pressures for incompatible development become even greater, as they certainly will. The membership also requested that the section's position be shared with other sections in the hope that they might act similarly, which is the reason for this letter. The section is also willing to share information it has gathered about the meaning of wilderness designation, including the erroneous idea that removal of shelters would be necessary. Interested persons can contact me.

EATING A "PEANUT BUTTER AND BLACK FLY SANDWICH" & OTHER GOINGS-ON AT KID GORE

By Dave Gonci, Connecticut Section

The peaceful solitude of backpacking seems to inspire the literary instincts of most hikers (although it doesn't do a thing for their writing). RELATING TO WILDERNESS

To review the GMC Board's action on what have been six proposed additional Wilderness Areas in Vermont, it adopted a basic position statement at its meeting Oct. 28, 1978. That was a limited position of opposition, "limited" in the sense that it opposed Wilderness designation only of the three proposed tracts over which the Long Trail passes (it took no position on the others). The Board's main concern was that under Wilderness designation, all trail shelters would be effectively prohibited. The Board also objected to permits which the U.S. Forest Service was then requiring Wilderness users to obtain.

Since then the USFS has dropped the permit requirement. The GMC has also been given reason to believe that the basic Wilderness law could be changed to allow "primitive shelters" in such areas. That put the Board in a position to be more supportive of additional Wilderness Areas - provided removal of its two objections holds firm.

The Board did not, as the Bread Loaf Section urges, "favor in principle the designation of additional wilderness in the Green Mountain National Forest." But it did indicate conditional support in its 1978 position statement for designation of one of the "Long Trail" tracts, the so-called Bread Loaf tract. Those conditions were resolution of the abovementioned general objections plus the specific one of revised boundaries.

Finally, at its meeting Oct. 13, 1979 the Board authorized then-Executive Director Steve Rice to participate in a mediation effort aimed at reaching some compromise between strong pro- and anti-Wilderness groups in Vermont. But that mediation effort failed and has been aborted. A final determination of additional Wilderness Areas in the U.S. will be made by Congress.

Field Program Plans Set; Volunteer Help Is Sought

By Harry T. Peet, Jr., Executive Director

Completion of our U.S. Forest Service Co-op work will be the number one priority for the Long Trail Patrol in the 1980 field season. The work which remains to be done is primarily between Lincoln Gap and the northernmost boundary of the Green Mountain National Forest on Mt. Ellen. During the winter, reimbursement rates for this work were renegotiated; the outlook is very promising.

To concentrate on this Co-op work we will operate only one LTP crew, that consisting of six persons and the crew boss. For the first time in four years, the 1980 Patrol will probably be composed of mostly experienced crew-members.

The Co-op work is expected to occupy the LTP the first ten weeks of the season. If, as expected, the GMC receives a third annual $10,000 State appropriation, the Patrol will then shift to rehabilitation work elsewhere on the Long Trail.

The number of Caretaker Program personnel will be the same as 1979: 14 caretakers plus two replacement caretakers and two supervisors. But the program will see two changes in shelter coverage. First, Cooper Lodge will not be manned. As was done so successfully last summer, we hope to recruit local youth camps to assist the Killington Section with particular projects. That is expected to offset, in large measure at least, the trail work which otherwise would have been done by that caretaker. On the other hand, a caretaker will be added at Glastenbury Mt. Shelter. One reason is to obtain accurate overnight use data to help in planning the size and character of an expected replacement for this shelter.

And a major change of another sort involves the use fee at caretaker locations. The Board of Directors at its meeting March 15 voted to increase this fee from $1.00 to $1.50 per night for non-GMC'ers and from $.50 to $.75 for GMC members.

For our ranger-naturalists, the coming field season will mean "business as usual." Increases in the program's budget will, however, allow more coverage on Camel's Hump and Mt. Mansfield in September and October. Fall hiking has become more and more popular, and for the last two years Camel's Hump in particular has been busier in September than in July and August.

Hiring of field personnel, incidentally, is underway at this writing; a roster will appear in the August LTN.

Also being pursued is the idea of local trails and shelters workshops with individual sections. These would serve as a successor to the Club-wide workshop held the last two years. While participation had been encouraging it was not as large as had been hoped, and there had been considerable administrative difficulties with scheduling, site selection and other arrangements. The "mini-workshops" could be scheduled to coincide with section work parties, and would therefore involve a minimum of expense and eliminate the arrangements necessary for the large trails and shelters workshop. Several sections have already expressed a strong interest. These will be held some time in late summer or early fall and by November we should be able to report the success of the first mini-workshops.

As always, the field season ahead promises to be a busy one. With an experienced core of ranger-naturalists, caretakers and Long Trail Patrol members to rely on, it should also be a very successful one!

The Field Programs Need You!

In recent months much thought has been given to ways of involving volunteers in field program activities and to ways of providing opportunities for all large members to participate directly in maintenance activities. Several ideas are being considered:

1. Volunteer Trail Crews - Club members could sign up to work on a trail crew for one or two weeks. These crews would operate just like the Long Trail Patrol, under the supervision of an experienced LTP member. Equipment, tools, transportation, and food would be provided. These crews would provide an opportunity for Club members to contribute to trail and shelter maintenance and obtain first-hand experience in different maintenance techniques.

2. Specific Work Projects - A schedule of work projects would be published in the Long Trail News, and members could sign up to participate in advance. Once again, tools, equipment, materials, food, and transportation would be provided. Each project would be supervised by an experienced Club member or LTP'er.

3. Volunteer Caretakers - Volunteers would be assigned to caretaker locations to assist the caretakers. Such assistance could be provided a week at a time or intermittently throughout the field season.

Whether or not these ideas become reality is up to you! Please let me know your reaction to these ideas, and, most importantly, whether you would be interested in participating. If enough interest is shown, these "projects" can begin sometime this summer or fall.

Winter Outing: Little Snow But Lots Of Cold

By Robert Schemer and Todd Jenner

EDITOR'S NOTE: The GMC's second annual Winter Outing was held March 1 and 2. Like the 1979 outing, this one served GMC members training and experience in winter camping and backcountry hiking. It was organized and led by Donald N. Wallace, whose assistant leaders were Pieter Crow and Manuel Peraza. Participants were Robert Schemer, Bryan Heatly and brothers Frank, Todd and Jeff Jenner. The basic account which follows is by Schemer. It is interspersed with extracts (in italics) from another account by Todd Jenner. We thank them both.

Winter Outing, 1980 was held under definite winter conditions. However, due to the "snow drought," snowshoes were not used but instead had a free ride on the back of our packs. Snow was in evidence, approximately 10 inches of powdered white stuff, with temperatures ranging from a comfortable 10° to an unbelievable -24°F.

The trip plan called for a meeting at Vermont Rt. 103 near the Long Trail crossing at 9:30 Saturday morning, March 1. After spotting a van at our intended point of exit (Upper Road), we gathered at the trailhead exchanging names, etc., and split up into two four-man teams (a non-chauvinistic remark since no "persons" were present). As such, we split up the tent, stove, fuel and cook kits between us for distribution of weight.

Don gave a brief talk concerning the intended route and off we went. We hiked north on the LT with our first rest stop at Clarendon Shelter. We were all visual examples of Don's first "on-the-Trail lecture" concerning the advisability of layering clothing to allow for proper ventilation. Trapped moisture can cause chilling, ergo a wet, uncomfortable hiker. Clothing being shed was indicative of just how much heat a body can generate.

Everyone was silent and the pace very slow. For a while it felt a little more like a funeral procession than a winter hike. But the purity of the air, the bright sun, the trees accented by the snow and the ever-appealing lure of the trail took their effect as we all loosened up and felt downright grateful to be able to share the splendor before us.

At this point, seeing overmitts casually laid aside while we rested and snacked, Don commented on the trail tip involving attachment of a rope or cord to your mittens. That allows them to hang free around your neck when not in use but remain easily accessible for immediate need. This also eliminates the ungainly "grab" as your overmitt sails off on a gust of wind when you were so sure they were entrapped firmly between your knees! At one trailside rest stop, Don reiterated the importance of drinking plenty of liquids during the course of a winter hike. Food is also an important factor, or rather the consumption of large amounts of calories for fuel and warmth.

Our pace was slow and frequent stops enabled Don to share his expertise in winter camping techniques. He expounded on the value of wool pants versus blue jeans and touched on the importance of windpants to keep snow from sticking and hence staying dry. We were instructed in the obvious perils of eating snow as a source of liquid and the fact that blowing on your hands for warmth actually has a detrimental affect.

Later in the day we were treated to a spectacular view of Killington and its neighboring peaks. The view provided a good reason to stop and another chance to learn "on-the-Trail". Dropping down and crossing Lower Cold River Road, our campsite was at hand.

By mid-afternoon we reached an opening in the woods overlooking the valley and affording our first clear view of the snow-capped peaks of Mendon, Killington, and Shrewsbury. Manuel swung his arm in an arc with an open hand and said simply, "Ahhh, such beauty." Cameras found their way out of cases and started clicking. We all did a little nibbling. It was a relaxed time, a scenic highlight of the day.

Donning our snowshoes for the first time this trip, we tramped out an area for the tents. Once the tents were up and ready for occupancy, another "gee, why didn't I think of that" tip was added to our growing list of learning experiences. A supply of snow was handily dumped beside the entrance to the tents for easy access. Who wants to leave the relative warmth of a shelter to search for and dip up a pot of snow to melt for that late night cup of cocoa!?

Once entrenched in our humble and warm abodes, the early winter darkness gave us added opportunities to swap hiking tips, equipment preferences and techniques. And finally, over the course of the evening, we were able to keep the "brothers three" straight. Isn't that right, Frank, I mean Jeff, I mean Todd?

Last instruction Don gave before turning in was, "Make sure everyone understands about putting their boots in their sleeping bags with them so they won't be frozen in the morning." ... Four of us in a small tent helped make it warm. So did the fact that I kept on my long underwear, wool pants and shirt, parka and wool hat, then wrapped myself up in my wool blanket inside my down bag on top of my sheepskin and ensolite pad. And I slept. At least I don't remember hearing my brother snore all night and I did feel refreshed at the first signs of daylight.

Sunday morning broke fair and bright with a temperature reading of -24° registered on our Outside thermometer. Our trip out was again leisurely paced and dotted with trail-side advice from the veterans. The notable quote of the day was "always use an ice axe to test the surface while crossing a river".

Again much activity and soon tents were away, packs were packed, and a motley group of eight overnight hikers started up the hill. We walked lightly, occasionally catching a snowshoe on a lower hanging branch or pausing to appreciate a pretty spot with no one in a hurry to get to our final destination. At one point, where the trail crossed Cold River, Don's foot got to feel it first hand. He then gave us all a good lecture on how not to cross a river.

The outing is highly recommended and is an excellent opportunity to share in a valuable educational experience provided by Don Wallace, his assistants, and the GMC.

GMC-DAY OUTING VERY NICE
(BUT FEW TURN OUT)

   Everything was just about right, except for one thing; very few GMC'ers participated. We speak of the GMC Day held March 16 at the Putney School in Putney. The private school, then closed for vacation, generously offered use of its upwards of 50 miles of cross-country ski trails. Arrangements for the event were kindly made by Brattleboro Sectioneer Joe Cook of Newfane.
   Vermont's winter was, of course, a very open one. But despite advice by some that Cook might just as well cancel the GMC Day, he remained confident that circumstances would change. Apparently he knows the right weatherman because on March 14 the area was hit by a good snowfall. Then March 16 itself was a sparkling day for the gathering of GMC cross-country enthusiasts.
   But where were they? Only five turned out: Joe Cook himself and his wife Debbie; Mark Brown of Putney, and also of the Brattleboro Section; and Doris Washburn and Dave Morse of the Montpelier Section (and in some mix-up of meeting place, even the latter two didn't actually meet up with the former three!). They report that though the hard-packed thin snowbase under the fresh snow made falling something to be avoided when possible, the weather, trails and skiing were very nice indeed.

Annual Meeting On Tap At Teago

Plans for the GMC's 1980 Annual Meeting, kindly developed by the host Ottauquechee Section, remain basically as announced in the February LTN: a pleasant meeting place and an attractive array of activities in addition to the main meeting itself. But there are a few important additional details including an extension of the reservation deadline for meals.

The gathering will be held Memorial Day weekend May 24-26 with Saturday, May 24, being the key day of the membership business meeting and other non-outing functions. These will be held at the Teago Grange hall in South Pomfret (see map); signs will be posted from the turnoff onto Vermont Rt. 12. Other particulars:

Registration and Business Meeting - Registration will begin at 8:30 a.m. with coffee, doughnuts and conversation to precede the business meeting beginning at 9:30. With a break for lunch, that is planned to continue into the afternoon. The Board of Directors is scheduled to have its organizational meeting at 3 p.m.

Camping - Two campgrounds are located about five miles away off Rt. 12 on Silver Lake at Barnard village. One is public, the other is private, and both charge fees for campsites (both tentsites and trailer hookups). They are Silver Lake State Park (802-234-9451) and Silver Lake Camping Area (802-234-9974). Call for details and reservations. Primitive (but free) camping is also available thanks to Fred Webster, who offers camping on his property about three miles away. Map and details available on request (see below).

Lodging - There are a number of inns, hotels, motels, cabins and tourist homes in the Woodstock area. A listing compiled by the Woodstock Chamber of Commerce is available on request (see below). Because of what may well be heavy Memorial Day weekend and tourist traffic, early reservations are recommended.

Meals - The Teago Volunteer Fire Department will be putting on two feeds. They are a buffet lunch at noon (adults, $2.50; children under 12, $1.50) and a ham, turkey and homemade pie dinner at 6 p.m. (adults, $5.50; children under 12, $4.00). Reservations are still required but the deadline has been extended to May 16 (see below for instructions).

Program - Two special programs are on tap for GMC'ers for late Saturday. At 3:30 p.m. is the planned panel discussion on the topic, "Wind Turbines on the Green Mountains: What Impact?" Members of the panel are expected to be representatives of the Vermont State Energy Office, the Green Mountain Power Corporation, and the University of Vermont. Then following the abovementioned dinner will be a slide show, definitely a single feature and possibly a double feature. Definite for 8 p.m. will be "An Invitation to Vermont Wilderness," a slide presentation by the Vermont Wilderness Association. Likely but not yet certain will be the premier of a slide show about New England's very special alpine areas including those on Mt. Mansfield and Camel's Hump. That is being produced for the GMC's Public Information Committee by Peter Zika. If completed in time, it will be shown at 7:30 p.m.

For the balance of the weekend, the big "O" Section is planning hikes Sunday and Monday on the Appalachian Trail (part of which it takes care of), Quechee Gorge, up Mt. Tom, and to the old Chateauguay gold mines. Trips to the Woodstock Historical Society are planned for Saturday and Sunday afternoons.

Public Transportation - For those desiring or requiring public transportation to get to the annual meeting, Amtrak has a station stop in White River Junction, and Vermont Transit Co. has bus service both to the Junction and to Woodstock (for schedules for the latter write them at 135 St. Paul St., Burlington, VT 05401, or telephone 802-864-6811). See below for how to arrange to be picked up.

THIRD MOSTEST & BESTEST

With an eye to both some fun and some recognition of significant section achievements, President Doug James has proclaimed the holding of the third annual awards contest for the 13 local and regional units. Awards of certificates of honor (no cash!) will be announced at the GMC's annual meeting for the winner in each of the following competition classes: Most Outstanding Service to the GMC; Most Outstanding Service to the Public; Best Trail Improvement; Best Shelter Improvement; Best Outhouse Improvement; Largest Work Outing Turnout; Largest Membership Increase; Most Unusual Outing; Best Outing Meal; Worst Outing Meal; and Funniest Outing Incident.

Winners will be determined by a committee of judges to be appointed by James. Written entries with appropriate numerical or descriptive information must reach him by May 17. His address is 936 Granville Rd., Westfield, MA 01085. Be sure to identify the competition class for which each entry is submitted, and write the word "Contest" on the outside of your mailing envelope. Keeping things informal, the judges may create one or more additional awards categories if circumstances require, and those submitting entries can even suggest their own award class, if their "mostest" or "bestest" doesn't fit any of the above.

Reservations and Information - Send meal reservations, with payment, to Tom Debevoise, Woodstock, VT 05091 (make checks payable to Ottauquechee Section, GMC). Remember the May 16 deadline! Ask him for any or all of the following: a larger version of the map printed here; the listing of lodgings; map and other information about Fred Webster's camping area; notice of need to be picked up at rail or bus station. For further information generally call Debevoise at 802-457-1186 (home; after 8 p.m. is best time) or Preston Bristow, Jr. at 457-3188 (office, weekdays) or 457-3797 (home; 6-7 p.m. is best time).

So come out, GMC'ers. It sounds like grand arrangements for a grand get-together.
