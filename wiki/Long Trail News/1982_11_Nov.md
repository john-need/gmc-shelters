Killington Ski Area Expansion Affects Long Trail
GMC Negotiates With Sherburne Corporation

by Harry Peet

A controversy over the construction of a new ski trail on Killington Peak this summer - a ski trail which closely parallels the Long Trail/Appalachian Trail, crosses it twice, and comes within twenty feet of Tamarack Shelter - threatened to become a major confrontation between the Vermont hiking community and Sherburne Corporation, operator of Killington Ski Area. The controversy became a public issue when the Vermont Appalachian Trail Committee, joined by the Vermont Agency of Environmental Conservation, filed an appeal of the Act 250 permit granted to Sherburne Corp. for construction of the ski trail.

Following the appeal, and a good deal of newspaper coverage of the event, efforts to mediate the dispute finally bore fruit when it was announced representatives of the ski area would meet with GMC President Jim Wilkinson and Executive Director Harry Peet to discuss a permanent, high-quality Trail route over ski area land. Presiding over this mediation attempt would be John Vihinen, executive director of the Rutland Regional Commission. As a condition for holding these discussions, the Vermont Appalachian Trail Committee and the State of Vermont agreed to drop the Act 250 permit appeal and acknowledged the dispute was caused by inadequate communication between trail supporters and ski area officials.

Two meetings between Sherburne Corp. and the GMC have taken place, and a third is scheduled for early December. Although details of these meetings are not being released, by mutual agreement of the parties involved, Jim Wilkinson and Harry Peet are satisfied with the progress that's been made and are hopeful of a result which will provide for the mutually satisfactory use of the mountain by both skiers and hikers.

The background of the controversy is very much involved, and begins more than three years ago. At that time the parcel of land in question, which lies between the Sherburne/Mendon town line (just south of Killington Peak) and another boundary south of Little Killington Peak, was owned by International Paper Company. As part of its efforts to permanently protect the Appalachian National Scenic Trail, the National Park Service began discussions with IP regarding acquisition of an easement for the Trail in that area. Concurrently, Sherburne Corporation expressed to IP its interest in that area for expanded ski area operations, particularly the area's suitability for a combined nordic/alpine skiing experience.

Lengthy negotiations between NPS and IP ensued, and involved, at different times and to varying degrees, the participation of Sherburne Corporation, the Appalachian Trail Conference, the Vermont Appalachian Trail Committee, and, last but not least, the GMC. Finally, an agreement which appeared to satisfy everyone was reached. It contained provisions for a protected trail corridor which would result in a new route for the LT/AT between Gov. Clement Shelter and Cooper Lodge - a route which would pass over the summit of Little Killington Peak - and also for ski area operations, including trails and a lift, in that corridor.

After the final language of the easement document was hammered out, all that remained to be done was for IP to sign the easement.

That event had been anticipated for several months when, unexpectantly, IP sold the land in question to Sherburne Corp. in June of this year. Soon thereafter, Sherburne Corp. submitted its Act 250 permit application for the new ski trail. Thus was the stage set for the dispute which followed.

GOOD LUCK, JACQUI!

by Harry Peet

Executive Assistant Jacqui Sulek will be leaving her GMC staff position in December. In submitting her resignation, Jacqui indicated it was time for her to seek new challenges and adventures. Unfortunately for the Club, Jacqui's current challenge is not to be found in the Green Mountains but on the high seas! The world of sailing beckons and, come springtime, we fully expect to hear she has dropped anchor in the Caribbean or South Seas.

Since 1978 Jacqui has served the Club well as a ranger-naturalist on Mt. Mansfield, as head ranger on that mountain (1979), as GMC Administrative Assistant (1980-81), and, most recently as Executive Assistant. In addition to her other talents, her artistic skills have added "a touch of class" to GMC exhibits, displays, and educational pamphlets, including the new "Tundra Trail Guide".

She will be sorely missed in the GMC office. While we're sorry to see her go, we wish her much success and many adventures with happy endings!

NEW EDITION OF LONG TRAIL GUIDE

A new edition of the "Guide Book of the Long Trail" is scheduled to be published in January, 1983. GMC members should look for details in the February Long Trail News.

While not as complete a rewrite as was done for the 21st edition, the new edition will feature completely updated trail descriptions, mileages, and maps. In particular, Divisions III, IX, and X have been substantially revised to include recent LT relocations north of Stratton Pond and south of Bolton Mt. For those hikers planning end-to-end trips next summer, please take note: the Long Trail is now 0.4 miles longer! 264.3 miles in all!

And everyone who has complained about the guide book mileage between Caughnawaqa Shelter and Story Spring Shelter can rest easier. You were right! The correct mileage is 11.7, not 3.5, miles.

The price of the new guidebook has not yet been determined, but rising printing costs may result in a substantial increase. As always, however, there will be a "healthy" discount for Club members.

LETTERS TO THE EDITOR

ON VANDALISM

I suppose that as a backpacker (that brings, but most often does not use a tent) and as an adopter (Greenwall Shelter) I have a vested interest in maintaining the shelter system.

I am not oblivious however, of the vandalism problem, having felt its sting more than once. I've cut my hands digging glass out of the firepit, I've slept a restless sleep on firecharred floors, I've creosoted over the needless graffiti on the shelter's walls, I have cleaned the spring of trash and have packed out countless pounds of purely thoughtless trash and I admit that I've felt this burden as much on my heart as on my back.

There is that other side of the coin though; the countless people I've met, many experiencing the LT and perhaps a touch of relatively undisturbed nature for the first time, or the entry in the log reading, "in from Peru Shelter in the rain, thank God for this clean dry shelter and whoever it was who left the firewood!", that makes it all worthwhile.

Would removing the shelter system also remove the chance for many to enjoy the LT? Would even one inexperienced hiker be subjected to the danger of hypothermia?

Would it add a burden on those of us who use the trail? Will removing the shelters stop vandalism at the expense of increased trash (plastic tarps and tents) or damage to trees (pine bough beds) or increased discomfort for those first discovering camping?

Perhaps we shall have to evolve from shelters to tent platforms to no trace camping. Logic says (to me at least) that creation of a stretch of platform-only camping on the LT is a real option. But as I think about this, I wonder if it would be anywhere near the same thing to return to a tent platform the way I can now return to Greenwall.

Perhaps, the logical solutions to real problems lie in the head and not in the heart. But it is my heart that brings me to the trail; my heart that says, "bear with grace this burden of thoughtlessness". And it is my heart that says, "with enough Herb Reed's and Fred Judd's and the unknown others doing the best we can, we can make the shelter system work, despite the worst efforts of vandals".

Respectfully,
Red Moselsky

Rutland, Vermont

INTERSECTIONAL IS COLD,
BUT FUN

By Frenchy Garfield

GOSHEN, Vt. - Over the years, GMC Intersectionals have been remembered for many things. Chances are that the 1982 edition will go down as the ice-age Intersectional, at least to those folks who spent the final weekend at Camp Thorpe when four inches of snow fell atop Killington.

The Intersectional Committee had done its job well. Thorpe, a camp for physically and mentally handicapped children and adults run by Joyce and Bill Heath, had all the facilities including cabins and an Olympic-sized swimming pool. There were days when the cabin fireplaces got a lot of use and the swimming pool hardly any!!

Despite the brisk weather, more than 100 members representing 11 sections, four members at large and the Intersectional staff provided by Explorer Post 2 of Sudbury, Massachusetts enjoyed 10 days of hiking, caving and evening activities in the spacious two-level lodge.

Activities included hikes on the Long Trail, which was only four miles away (parts of it were visible from camp if you wanted to use binoculars), spelunking in Nickawacket and Bristol caves and the famous Pittsford Ice Cave, and a Saturday night square dance.

Lincoln and Randy Mead of Burlington continued the family's long-standing tradition of hosting (or at least cooking) a Friday night supper which preceded the annual GMC-Explorer auction where your obedient servant nearly lost his voice.

The auction gets bigger and better as time goes on and this year raised more than $80.00 for the GMC treasury. Items put on the block included two fresh trout caught in the camp pond, a plastic soda pop bottle of suspicious origin (allegedly from Michigan) and assorted culinary gems from the kitchens of GMC cooks. Most of the latter were scoffed up by free-spending representatives of the People's Republic of Burlington.

We all missed our elder statesmen Clint Banks and Len Daley of Ohio who have driven to Intersectionals without fail for years, but were unable to do so this year because of illness. Everyone on hand signed a card for each telling how much they were missed by all.

GMC Executive Director, Harry Peet, visited the Intersectional Friday, bringing with him a large assortment of slides from the Mauri Wintturi collection including several that show the old summit house on Mt. Mansfield and other landmarks that have long since bowed to the pressures of time.

The final Saturday night campfire was dedicated to Mauri who was the driving force in bringing the Intersectional to Camp Thorpe. He scouted the camp himself last year and then badgered the Intersectional Committee into taking a look and booking it for 1982.

Connecticut section won the race for the largest delegation, by one, with 19 registrants, with Killington and Pioneer Valley tied for second with 18 apiece. The At-Large turnout, which normally tops the sections, was small this year with only four MALs on hand.

Intersectional chairman, Rob Carey, was ably aided by John Kirby of New York section who will take over the chairmanship next year along with Dot Knight of Pioneer Valley, Bob Poole of Connecticut and Frenchy Garfield.

By the time you read this the Committee will hopefully have decided upon the site for next year's Intersectional. The dates will be August 20th - 28th, inclusive, and the location will be southern Vermont.

BOLTON RELOCATION FINISHED!

On October 17, 1982 the Burlington Section officially opened the reroute of the Long Trail between a point north of Duck Brook Shelter and Bolton Mountain. About 25 Section members turned out for a series of hikes and a cookout, which were held despite the season's first snowfall.

The Section decided to reroute the Trail when advised by one of the landowners over which a greater part of the LT in that area passes that logging and other development in the Bolton Valley area might detract from the wilderness aspects of the stretch of trail affected. It was decided to move the trail higher up on the ridgeline to avoid the potential development and to take advantage of some fine views afforded by the higher elevations.

With several years notice of the potential changes, the GMC had plenty of time to seek a new route and accomplish the surveying, cutting and marking necessary to establish the new passage. Section work parties coiled steadily, and this summer the Long Trail Patrol worked on the reroute and helped bring it to completion.

With all the effort involved, a formal opening ceremony was deemed proper, and was scheduled for Sunday, October 17th. Unfortunately, the day started with pouring rain which turned to snow at the higher elevations of the new route. The rain/snow combined with the thick coating of fallen leaves on the ground made the hikers who turned out slip back two feet for every three walked. On top of that, the heavy clouds precluded any enjoyment of the new section's excellent lookouts.

Once gathered at noon at Duck Brook Shelter, the process of starting a fire to warm cold bodies and to heat up various lunches was the main order of business. With everything combustible being soaking wet, a lot of very close attention had to be paid to the tiny fire, but the persistence of Burlington Section members paid off and a respectable conflagration was ultimately achieved.

After lunch, a ribbon cutting ceremony was held just north of the Duck Brook Shelter. Burlington Section President Andy Squires read out a long list of all those who had worked so hard to complete the reroute. One of the finest lookouts on the new Trail was named after Burlington Section member Jack Harrington, in recognition of his special efforts on many a weekend. A wooden plaque signifying "Harrington's View" was unveiled, and will be put up at the site of the overlook.

The relocated LT, like the old route, is mostly over lands of the Bolton Valley Corporation, which operates the ski area of the same name. Ralph R. DesLauriers, President of Bolton Valley, was the first landowner in the state to sign a trail protection agreement when this program started back in 1973.

SEPTEMBER BOARD MEETING
COVERS MANY TOPICS

The Board of Directors meeting held in Rutland on September 25 covered a number of subjects aside from the Killington ski trail controversy. Chief among these was Executive Director Harry Peet's report on the summer field season.

The meeting was held in a function room at the Rutland Holiday Inn and was preceded by the customary coffee-and-danish preliminaries. At 9:30 AM President Jim Wilkinson gavelled for order, correctly predicting that the Board would need every available minute for the crowded agenda.

With the parliamentary preliminaries out of the way, Treasurer Joe Cook was called upon to give his report. Joe observed that expenses were running remarkably close to projections, and revenue a little ahead. Once again, an excellent response to the membership solicitation was largely responsible. Overall, it looks like we will end up the year in the black, thanks to excellent cost control and a continued fine record of membership support.

President Wilkinson had little to report and wished to abbreviate his remarks so as to allow the discussion to proceed quickly to the Killington problem. He did mention that he and Vice President Preston Bristow were involved in a serious auto accident in Bridgewater on the way to inspect the situation on Little Killington. Two cars were totally demolished in the mishap, but apparently no serious injuries were sustained.

Following this tale of travail, Executive Director Harry Peet proceeded to report on a variety of occurances and activities. The first of these related to the aftermath of the death of Mauri Wintturi.

Mauri's Legacy

Mauri left the Club around $14,000, which has been added to the Endowment. He also left all his books and photographs. A list of the books in the Mauri Wintturi collection was distributed at the meeting. It runs to eleven pages and includes several hundred books, paperbacks and magazines. Most of the titles relate directly to Vermont, though others are concerned with Maine, New Hampshire and the Adirondacks. It is a remarkable collection of outdoor literature. In view of the space problem at 43 State St., it will probably not be possible to keep the books at Club headquarters, and a suitable disposition for the works is being considered. Members suggestions are welcome.

The slides and photographs collected by Mauri over the years are a real bonanza of Trail and Club history - though it will be necessary to catalog and identify all the scenes. There is also a complete collection of issues of the Long Trail News and Long Trail Guide.

1982 Field Season

Harry proceeded to describe the "ad hoc" field season - the one which almost didn't happen! It started with the unexpected loss of half a dozen newly recruited caretaker personnel. In order to get a field season off the ground, Harry had to scramble to round up a few "warm bodies". Even so, two caretaker positions, Congdon Camp and Glastenbury Mountain Shelter, remained unmanned for the summer.

The Long Trail Patrol presented a different problem. An experienced crew boss was available, but all eight crewmembers were new and inexperienced. How would these nine strangers work out? Would anything get done? To say the least, the season was not off to an auspicious start.

Serendipity, or something, was on our side, because the nine strangers worked just fine together and turned out to be an enthusiastic and energetic group. A good deal of quality work was done.

One major project was the rock steps placed just outside the tourist center in Smuggler's Notch. This was the first project of the summer and it was quite an undertaking. Photos of the steps are elsewhere in this issue. Picture yourself hauling those rocks up the hillside in the 90-degree heat....

The crew did some other work in the Smuggler's Notch area. Most of this trail maintenance effort was south of Route 108, a very heavily used stretch of Trail leading to Mt. Mansfield.

The Long Trail Patrol was "volunteered" to help the Burlington Section with the reroute of the Trail in the Bolton area north of Jonesville. They did a major job here. The Long Trail was rerouted to avoid possible development and to move up higher on the ridgeline where some excellent lookouts were available.

Club Office Situation

The Club office setup still poses some problems, mainly in that the building may be on the verge of collapse! Engineers came in, opened up some sections of the wall in the GMC office, then departed hurriedly and haven't been back since! Not all that reassuring. The landlord, the Chittenden Trust Company, has also been making some noises about raising the rent, and also about moving to new quarters elsewhere. So, in general, the situation at Club headquarters is subject to some uncertainty.

On a more favorable note, the secretary, Deb Philip, is doing a fine job, and will be kept on as long as funding permits. The Club is thinking of purchasing a copier to eliminate the vast amount of staff time wasted running around town making copies. Harry is also taking a very tentative look at a computer, to replace the increasingly expensive service bureau now being used for the mailing list, etc.

Harry made note of the highly successful 50th anniversary party held for the Green Mountain National Forest at Hapgood Pond. Everyone had a great time, and the occasion was graced by the presence of Vermont's elder statesman, George Aiken.

The Shrewsbury Horror

From the sublime to the ridiculous, the next subject was the infamous Wade Property. It seems that no one can decide whether this battered structure is a dangerous eyesore fit only for demolition or a golden opportunity to introduce culture, commerce or convenience to the Long Trail down Shrewsbury way! On August 10, 1982, the Act 250 hearing regarding the ski trail was held before the District Environmental Commission. Representing VATC, Preston Bristow sought and received "party status" at the hearing. He testified that the proposed ski trail would adversely impact that LTIAT because there were no provisions at all for separation of the ski trail from the LTIAT and its shelters. More importantly, he argued the trail community could not adequately determine the potential impacts of the ski trail until an easement agreement had been worked out with the ski area.

Nonetheless, the District Environmental Commission granted the Act 250 permit, and Sherburne Corporation immediately began construction of the new trail. In turn, the VATC filed an appeal of the permit, and the Agency of Environmental Conservation quickly followed suit. During the next several weeks all attempts to negotiate with Sherburne Corp. were ignored.

On September 25th the GMC Board of Directors gathered at the Holiday Inn in Rutland for its regular autumn meeting. Although the GMC has a representative on the Vermont Appalachian Trail Committee, the Club, itself, had as yet taken no official action in the current dispute other than to request, should it be necessary, "party status" at the anticipated appeal hearing before the State Environmental Board. The situation on Killington Peak quickly became the prime topic of the meeting.

Preston Bristow reviewed in detail the situation described above, and then described his ill-fated attempt to inspect the new ski trail firsthand, which ended abruptly when he and Club President Jim Wilkinson were involved in an auto accident on the way to the mountain. Since several Killington Section members who had seen the new ski trail were in attendance, Preston asked for their impressions. Robert Badger passed around several photographs showing the new ski trail passing within twenty feet of Tamarack Shelter, and expressed his very strong concern that the shelter would be much abused this winter. The general consensus was that the new ski trail did, indeed, represent a serious encroachment of the LTIAT and would adversely affect the Trail.

Following this, a long period of discussion ensued, dealing mainly with clarifications of who the parties involved in the case were and what actions the Board could take. Bob Hagerman correctly pointed out that the immediate problem was the appeal of the Act 250 permit granted to Sherburne Corp., and that this involved repudiating the District Environmental Commission's ruling that the LTIAT would not be adversely affected. At-Large Director Neil Giroux suggested that the best way to protect the LTIAT in the affected area was to somehow reopen the easement negotiations with Sherburne Corporation, and he further suggested several ways this might be attempted.

At this point the Board broke for lunch. When the Board reconvened, Neil Giroux presented a resolution for consideration.

RESOLVED: That the Executive Advisory Council of the Green Mountain Club be and is hereby authorized and empowered to adopt a policy to preserve and protect the Green Mountain Club's interests in the area where the Long Trail System crosses land owned by the Sherburne Corporation, and take whatever action it deems necessary to execute that policy, and is hereby authorized to expend an amount not to exceed $10,000 for legal fees and expenses hereinafter as a result hereof incurred.

With all of the discussion that had preceded the presentation of the resolution, very little additional debate was needed. It was clearly the sense of the Board that it was necessary for the Club to take a position against the Act 250 permit approval in order to live up to its responsibility as "protector and defender" of the Long Trail System. And, although it was certainly hoped a negotiated solution could be reached, the resolution clearly indicated the Club's determination to take whatever steps might be necessary. The resolution was adopted unanimously when put to the vote.

Following the Board meeting, the Club learned Sherburne Corp. had requested that the appeal hearing before the State Environmental Board be bypassed and the matter go directly to Superior Court. Although the Club quickly responded to this new development by seeking additional legal assistance, it continued its attempts to reopen negotiations.

Those attempts were finally successful, and discussions with Sherburne Corporation are being conducted. It is hoped a final solution can be reported in the next issue of the Long Trail News.

1983 QUETICO PARK CANOE
EXCURSION

The Excursion Committee is excited about its plans for the fourth big GMC vacation. This will be ten days of canoeing in Quetico Provincial Park in Western Ontario.

Quetico Park is 1750 square miles of wilderness, accessible only to the canoeist. There are no roads, no buildings, no airplanes, and no outboard motors. Hundreds of interconnecting lakes, streams, and sandy beaches have remained in their natural state, to be explored at leisure. There are no poisonous snakes, insects, or plants to worry you. The waters are pure and clean, you can drink directly from any lake or stream; no treatment is needed. Fish such as Walleye and Great Northern Pike, Lake Trout and Black Bass are plentiful.

The two week trip includes 11 days on the water and two days travel each way. Travel will be by train from Essex Junction to Thunder Bay, Ontario and by bus the final 100 miles to Atikokan, Ont.; returning by the same route. There will be a layover in Montreal the first day, long enough for some sightseeing.

The outfitter, Quetico North of Atikokan, Ont., will provide complete outfitting. This includes all food while canoeing, tent, camp equipment, sleeping bag with flannel liner, duluth packs and, of course, canoes.

The canoe route will be approximately 60 miles, with 7 days paddling with full loads and 4 days in-camp, for side trips or relaxing. This will be entirely flatwater (i.e. no rapids, etc.). Participants need not be highly experienced canoeists. Even if you've never paddled before, there is time enough to learn the basics before August.

The cost of the trip will be $625.00, plus or minus $50.00, depending on the world monetary situation and other contingencies. This will include round trip rail and bus transportation from Essex Junction, Vt., accommodations and meals in Atikokan, and the services of the Quetico North Outfitters. The price does not include meals while traveling. The cost for children will be lower. Call for details (see below). Persons under the age of 18 must be accompanied by an adult responsible for that person's safety, welfare, and behavior. No pets are permitted.

Camping in Quetico is controlled by the issue of permits to groups of nine people per campsite. We will apply for three permits when they become available early in 1983. This will allow the GMC a limit of 27 participants, to be determined first-come-first-served on the basis of date of receipt of a $200.00 deposit (which is required). As we are not guaranteed the availability of three permits it is advised that you make your reservations early. Reservations should be in by Dec. 31, 1982. The balance will be due March 31, 1983. Important: If a participant wishes, he/she may make his/her own transportation arrangements (Please indicate on the reservation form) and deduct $300.00 from the total cost.

Participants do not need to have partners when making reservations. You may pick your own partners from the list at a later date.

The Excursion Committee, which is an ad-hoc part of the new Club Wide Activities Committee, is made up of Andy Squires, Chairman, Carlene Squires, Tom Brooks, and Dot Myer, all veterans of at least one Club excursion. GMC'ers wishing more information before deciding whether or not to participate may contact the Squires at RD #2, Box 188, Richmond, Vt. 05477. Tel. 802-434-2533. For those who do make reservations, more details will be sent as they become available.
