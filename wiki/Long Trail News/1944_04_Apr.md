               THE CHIEF'S MESSAGE
                               By LARRY D EAN
                           Pres., G. M. C. , Inc.
 As the Green Mountain Club enters on its 34th year of existence
.l\. It faces one of its most trying periods with World conditions
as they are . It is the second war experience in the Club's life time
and such periods mean curtailment of activities , in trail maintenance
and social functions to some degree , more in our weaker sections
but lesser in some of our stronger. The longer the War lasts the
more "repair work" we as a Club will have to experience until the
return of the so called good old days . Neglected trails and lodges
must be repaired, as well as our membership lists .
   W e all, at one time or another , hear from some of our members
now in the armed service and in every case they urge us to keep the
spirit of the Club alive and plead with us to preserve the Long
Trail for their return. I am sure you will all agree that we cannot
remain indifferent to their wishes.

                                                                               3

<!-- page: 4 -->
Yes, it is easy to feel that since many of our younger trail workers
are now soldiering we cannot do much about trail work. It is easier
to feel that now cars don't roll up to our doors as in past outing
days we cannot engage in present day outings or get out on the
trail. Have you tried working in smaller groups on the trail or
have you tried riding to your outing place by bus? You'll find that
it can be done and that it is not too bad either ahd lots of fun.
   May I, as president, in behalf of the parent Green Mountain
Club, assure you that it is not the Club's slightest intention to do
anything to impede the War Effort when we urge you all to carry
on in your section activities where and when possible.
   We cannot foresee what effect the post-war era will have on the
Club and the Long Trail but we who have worked for them and
sung their praises look forward with hope. We don't want to go
back to the "good old days," to do so would mean only stagnation
and death for the Club. We should now begin plans for broader
activities that will attract increased membership when the Boys
return. Right now , every single clubster should think about and
offer helpful suggestions for the triple program that we as a Club
should and must work out . This program embraces:
   1. The preservation of our Trails for hiking and maintenance of
shelters.
   2. Broader winter sports accommodations along our trails and at
some of our shelters .
   3. Increasing our cooperation with State recreational activities
and with all forest and wild life conservation efforts.
   We should start our planning NOW in order that we don't
"miss the first bus as the following ones will be crowded."

                         EDITORIAL
     HE EDITOR has very little to say. First, of course, an apology is
T    due to all the members of the club for the delay in theappearanceof
the News. There is really no good excuse, but there are reasons. The
first one is that Mr~ . Walsh, who so ably edited our best issues,
left the Middlebury College Press nearly a year ago, thus forcing us

to fall back on amateur assistance, and the second is that most of
our amateurs-especially the one who, in a moment of weakness,
agreed to edit this issue- have been kept unusually busy by various
phases of wa:i; work . However, here is an issue, and, since it takes
the place of two or three regular numbers, we can afford to make it
a little more voluminous and include more illustrations without
exceeding the limited appropriation of the last annual meeting.
   It is very clear that interest in the woods, the mountains and the
trail have not by any means been extinguished by the war. Some of
the boys have even sent from the fighting fronts for guide books, so
that they can at least dream plans for hikes to be taken when they
come back. They say, "Be sure to keep up the Long Trail so we
will have something worth while to come back to when the war is
over." Here then is a challenge that we must not fail to meet. Not
only must we buy all the War Bonds we can, support the Red
Cross loyally, cultivate our Victory Gardens energetically and pay
our Income Taxes cheerfully, but we must keep up the Long Trail
and its shelters so that they will be ready for the boys who are
<dreaming about them now on the beaches of Italy or in the jungles
of the South Pacific.
   Most of this work will have to be done by volunteer labor,
directed by the local sections of the Club, and we therefore call
upon all the sections, especially those that seem to have fallen into
a state of semi-somnolence, to bestir themselves at once, to rekindle
interest in the Long Trail, to seek new members for the club who
will also be workers on the trail, so that the boys will "have some-
thing worth while to come back to when the war is over.'' To be
sure there may not be the same thrill in maintenance work on a
trail already built as in going out and cutting a new one, but it
certainly is a matter of great satisfaction to do a worthy job in
preparation for the peace we all hope is not too far away.

THE LONG TRAIL LODGE
    OUBTLESS many members of the club have wondered what effect ·
D    the war has had on the operations of the Long Trail Lodge.
The answer is in one word,-BAD. For more than a year the Lodge
has not been open, because war-time restrictions on driving have
made it impossible to operate without a loss . As the Lodge already
had a heavy burden of debt, partly accumulated during the early
years of club operation, and partly due to the construction of the
Annex, or Chalet, for winter use, and as the fixed charges of main-
taining the property when closed are considerable, the club found
itself faced by a difficult situation. As noted in the report of the
last annual meeting, a committee was appointed to consider what
could be done, and do it. They studied the probl~m thoroughly,
weighing every suggested solution, and finally agreed that the best
one available was to accept an offer from Mr. L. G . Treadway to
lease the property from the club for a period of years .
   Accordingly ·a lease was very carefully drawn to cover, as far
as could be foreseen, all contingencies, and protect the club to the
greatest possible extent. This lease is for a period of five years, at a
gradually increasing rental, with a provision for renewal for a
like term at the highest rate. A low rental for the first two years
was felt justified by the uncertainty as to when the lessee would be
able to open the Lodge, while the return at the end of the five year
period will be sufficient to permit some of the debt to be extiw
guished each year. As Mr. Treadway assumes all expenses for
maintenance, insurance , taxes and operation, the rent received by
the club will be all in the clear, and the officers of the club will be
relieved of a considerable burden of care and attention. In this con-
nection, the club is glad to acknowledge its debt of gratitude to
Earl Boyce for the great amount of time and labor he has given,
without any compensation other thm the satisfaction of accom-
plishment, to looking after the business of the Lodge over a period
of many years . While he will doubtless be glad to be relieved of
the responsibility, we feel quite sure that his affection for the Lodge
will not permit him to cease to keep an eye on it, and see that all
goes according to the agreement.
                                                                      9

<!-- page: 10 -->
Exactly when the Lodge will open, or what Mr. Treadway's
 plans are for the immediate future, we are unable to announce at
 this time, but it may be considered certain that as soon as travel
 conditions become such as to give promise that the Lodge can be
 operated with even a small profit, it will be open again. There is
 nowhere a place just like it, and there are few who have been there
 who do not look forward to another visit. We hope the fact that
 the management of the Lodge will be independent of the club will
 not lessen in the slightest degree the pride that club members feel
 in it, nor their willingness to patronize the Lodge or direct their
,friends to it. The club still owns. the property, and it is obvious
 that, lease or no lease, we w~ll receive no return from it unless it
 can be run profitably. This is one reason why we wish Mr. Tread-
 way all success in his efforts to operate the Lodge. There are other
 reasons too; the affection of the club for Mortimer Proctor, who,
 with his mother, gave the Lodge to the club more than twenty years
 ago, the pride the club feels in the Lodge as a unique and attractive
 woodland resort , and our friendship for Mr. Treadway himself,
 who has supervised the management of the Lodge for the past ten
 years,- all these incline us to wish him success, and to feel that
 the Lodge is in good hands.

 THE REALIZATION OF A DREAM
                        By J ESSIE PHILLIMORE
     OR MANY YEARS members of the Worcester Section have dreamed
F  of having a camp of our own. Now, when " Where to go" has
become a major problem for most outing clubs , Dr. Gardner N .
Cobb, a charter member of this section, has given our club five
years free use of a building on his large estate.
   Camp Cobb , as it is now called, was originally built as a hunt'
ing hut for a group of business men. Long since neglected, it was

George delight in getting surprises ready for the next scheduled
outing.

                WINTER WEEKEND
                           By MARY PERKINS
    was with some skepticism that the outing committee of the
I
    T
   Killington Section scheduled a winter weekend to be held at
the Perkins' camp in Mendon at the foot of Blue Ridge. The place
was ideal, the time, February i2th and i 3th, convenient, but all
other elements were of a discouraging nature. Before anything
further is said it might be well to add, our winter weekend was a
grand success and every one of the twenty members who took part
in it agreed that it was one of the best sectional hikes we have
ever had.
   The committee decided to have the group live as one big family
as far as meals were concerned. This required the making of menus
for five meals, marketing, keeping account of the cost per person
per meal and also the ration points . It also involved packing the
food into camp. This was not too great a hardship for camp is
only a half mile from the main road where the cars were parked.
   The meals were filling, satisfying, and surprisingly inexpensive.
Saturday's dinner: soup, macaroni and cheese, tomato sauce, bread,

and hot dogs, bread, devil dogs, coffee-cost $.20. Sunday's
breakfast: grapefruit juice, one egg, oatmeal, doughnut, coffee-
cost $.15. Sunday's dinner: Boiled rice, meat loaf with tomato
sauce, string beans, bread, pie, coffee-cost $.19. Sunday's sup~
per: leftovers cost $. 05.
   The food problem was not the only part of our program that
proved successful. The play, which was not pre~arranged, but a do
as you please type, consisted of skiing, snow shoeing, taboggan-
ing, and inside games by the roaring fire. Some of the youngsters
hiked the three miles from camp to Pico to ski, others skied up the
road to the Tucker trail and in a ways, another group, wanted to
tramp around in the woods near camp. Of course "Doc" was on
hand with his camera taking shots, posed, and unposed, flattering
and otherwise.
   When night came and the cots were made up, we were ready to
turn in, but sleep was a few hours off. There was so much to talk
about , and for those who didn't want to talk, the listening was good.
   The weatherman deserves a little thanks for another part of our
successful winter weekend. Saturday was just ordinary, a little
cloudy, a few snow flurries, and moderately cold. Sunday was
different! Sunday was a perfect day! It was one of those days that
makes you wish you could paint a picture, write a poem, or sing
a song. We all felt it and expressed it in various ways. The beau~
tiful, deep blue, clear sky made a gorgeous background for the
snow laden trees on the hills in back of camp, and the sun peering
over the shoulder of Pico, made a halo on the mountain, Smoke
curling out of the chimneys, and voices from inside camp singing
"Oh, what a beautiful morning, Oh, what a beautiful day,'' lured
us into camp for breakfast.
