The Long Trail Lodge

A year ago, the Trustees at their April meeting decided, after much careful thought and discussion, that it would be in the club's best interests to dispose of the Long Trail Lodge property. Not wishing to act without first advising club members, the Board brought the matter up for discussion at the Annual Meeting last year.

At that time, and after it appeared that some members felt strongly about keeping the property from a purely sentimental point of view, the Board was asked to reconsider its decision to sell.

This the Board was glad to do. Not only has it given serious and continued thought to this matter through the year, but it appointed a committee to consider the whole matter and to report to the Board and the membership this spring.

The members of this committee are:

Lillian P. Lodge, Chairman          Larry W. Dean
Herbert Wheaton Congdon             Roy O. Buchanan
Walter Collins O'Kane               T. N. Goddard
Earl A. Boyce                       Craigue Perkins
Louis N. Bigelow                    P. C. Voter

Seven members of this committee met in Rutland on April 10, 1953 and spent a full afternoon discussing the problem from every angle. Previous to this meeting, much had been gone over by correspondence between committee members.

The report of this committee includes these points:

1. The Long Trail Lodge was presented to the club thirty years ago by Mr. Mortimer R. Proctor and his mother. It was a generous and kind gift, intended as a home or club house for the club. We have conserved this gift and increased its value by putting back into it all income for the intervening years. The chalet has been built, kitchens added, etc.

2. Through inevitable evolution and growth, the buildings which we call the Long Trail Lodge have become a complex property which is unique and beautiful, but actually more elaborate and extensive than a reasonable home for the Green Mountain Club. The club should recognize the fact that it is not adapted to exercise wise responsibility for so elaborate a project.

3. We should give full thought to the idea of possible major loss by fire and the situation we would be in if this happened, as against this same situation if a responsible company owned it. It is practically impossible to insure the many buildings against fire loss to the extent of their replacement cost. An example of this is Tree Tops cabin which burned last summer and for which we received $1000 insurance. It is costing the club approximately $4000 to rebuild with a cabin somewhat larger. If one of the main buildings had gone by fire, we would have been unable to rebuild with the amount received from the insurance.

4. If the club decides to dispose of the property, any monies received from such a sale should be placed in a fund to be known as the Proctor Memorial Fund, the income from which would be available to the club for carrying out its proper objectives.

The club has held the property for thirty years with no return on the investment. With our present mortgage, it will be many years before we can be out of debt. If the property were sold and the proceeds invested wisely, the income would provide sufficient funds to take care of Vermont trails and shelters under our supervision, and thus give us benefit from the gift—benefit which the donors would surely be pleased for us to have.

The Board therefore recommends that the Long Trail Lodge property be sold.

All members are urged to attend the Annual Meeting when this important problem will be discussed and voted upon.

LONG TRAIL NEWS RELEASE
From WORCESTER SECTION

It has occurred to me many times during the last several months, that the success of the Worcester Section Green Mountain Club, Inc. is due to the fine spirit of cooperation shown by the members, and the remarkable leadership of President Alfred Williams. Having just passed my first anniversary mark, I must confess that I am taken aback at the speed with which things are accomplished.

In the few short months since the first of the year, the interior improvements in the Holden camp have been a joy to behold. In place of a rather dingy ill-equipped kitchen there is now a brightly lighted, freshly painted, convenient and efficient room. It is certainly a joy to the willing workers who for so long had to make the best of an inadequate set-up. The main room of the camp is now most attractive, and plans are always in the making for improvements here.

So that the shock of a first view of the camp at some time in the future will not be too great, let me acquaint my readers with some of the other outstanding improvements out of doors. Work is progressing rapidly on the Joseph Frappier Memorial fireplace, which promises to be one of the most elaborate hereabouts. Club members are donating their time and money to the building of this memorial to one of the founders of the Worcester Section. A new water supply is to be ready shortly, which should delight those members who have been toting the drinking water from home.

Lest the reader might have the impression that life is all work and no play, I hasten to give a brief resume of the good times the Worcester Section has enjoyed during the past several months.

On February 15 an outing, followed by a supper and entertainment, featuring an original skit written by Marion Elvidge, and performed by club members.

Over the February 28-March 1 weekend, fourteen members and guests had an opportunity to spend the weekend at The Ark in Jaffrey, N. H. The male members of the group hiked to the top of Mt. Monadnock, under adverse conditions, with the females stopping at various points along the icy trail. Square dancing and fireside singing made top entertainment for the energetic mountaineers.

On March 7, another outing at camp with supper and colored slides of the Pasadena Rose Festival shown by Doctor and Mrs. Steele, with commentary.

The Recreational Conference in Amherst, Mass. gave members an opportunity to see old friends attend a sugaring-off party, and learn more of the whys and wherefores of the art of recreating.

On April 4, about thirty members attended a business meeting at the camp, which followed a full day of activity in connection with the various work projects.

We now look forward to the New England Folk Festival in Worcester on April 24-25, where the Worcester Section will have charge of a refreshment booth, which will swell the club's coffers. All you readers who plan to attend the New England Folk Festival be sure to come with a good appetite, and patronize the food concession.

Where certainly is no opportunity for spring fever laziness to develop in the Worcester Section. There is much to do, and much to see.

PAUL F. MOSS

In addition to this interesting report of local activities of the Worcester Section we have first hand information that there are loyal Worcester Section members of long standing who get up on the mountains in Vermont and keep up their section of The Long Trail. This requires considerable effort due to the fact that the distance from home to the mountains is great. This is true of all out of state section members. However, it would make a better and stronger Green Mountain Club if more members could popularize the Green Mountains by using them.


NEWS FROM THE BURLINGTON SECTION
Mountaineers Enjoy Oyster Stew Supper
at Mountain Cabin

Twenty-three Green Mountain Club followers braved the elements and took the long uphill climb in to their Taylor Lodge Sunday, February first for their second Oyster Stew supper. An advanced party packed the makings of the supper in the three odd miles from Stevensville to the lodge in Nebraska Notch during the late forenoon. After arriving, the men of the group carried water and worked up the necessary firewood for the supper.

During the afternoon four snowshoed up from the Moscow side; three from the Barre Section of the Club. A second group from the Burlington Section followed the morning group in during the afternoon.

Although the weather was cold the snow made for good snowshoeing and the trek through the snow coated woodland was pleasant. One of the party showed his skill maneuvering through the bushy trail on his skis.

President Beverly Thorpe (UVM Home Ec. Senior) prepared the oyster stew supper for the group who proclaimed it excellent. Jack Harrington, Clifton Havens and Miss Thorpe donated pies for the feed in the cabin on The Long Trail.

The thermometer registered eight degrees below when they left the cabin for their return trip to the cars with their flashlights lighting the way down about eight o'clock.

LARRY DEAN

Dear Fellow Members:
   Summer is just around the corner. We feel--or
think we do--a vital commotion under the ground
where life is beginning to stir and heave, making
ready to joyously burst forth into a new world.
   Golden sun and silver rain! Fresh and soothing
winds! The sweet and tantalizing fragrance of
flowers! The waters of woodland streams cavorting
gleefully down hillside slopes, to come finally to
their lazy and blissful ease in valley lakes and ponds!
   What luck! What immeasurably great, good for-
tune is ours, that we live here among the glories of
Vermont's Green Mountains! Let us be thankful--
and may we fully know, appreciate and revel in our
blessings.
   Here in the Burlington Section we are as excited
as a pup with two tails. Our mountains are calling
to us to get ready; to get our packs repaired and
adjusted, our boots greased, our blankets and sleep-
ing bags rolled. And, friends, we are ready. We have
been ready for weeks, for months--yea, forever; be-
cause we learned way back in the days of our first
sunrise, that whatever the vicissitudes of life else-
where, here in our mountains we shall be at peace--
with ourselves and all the world.
   So, we wish to extend to you our sincere hope that
these same woods and mountains will bring to you
this coming summer a measure of joy and happiness
equal to our own. We hope, too, that the spirit of
comradeship amongst us will serve as an invitation
for you to join us whenever inclination and oppor-
tunity come your way. We have some mighty fine
forests and mountains up this way, and we'll be
proud to show them off to you anytime.

                                       E. R. BEAL

A.T.C. BOARD MEMBER BUCHANAN

   The name of Roy O. Buchanan is almost synono-
mous with trail maintenance for he has been for
over thirty years the "spark plug" of this activity in
Vermont.
   On his seventieth birthday, December 13, 1951,
members of the Green Mountain Club commemo-
rated the event by a "party" for him and the
presentation of tokens of appreciation.
   Since 1920 Prof. Buchanan has directed the con-
struction of 23 shelters on the Long Trail. He has
long headed the Trail Patrol. In 1951 he actively
supervised the construction of the new Taylor Lodge.
   Besides building shelters and working on the Long
Trail and the connecting link of The Appalachian
Trail between the Long Trail and the Dartmouth
Outing Club trails, he has done much hiking for fun.
He has climbed all major peaks in the East, includ-
ing Marcy, Mansfield, Washington, Katahdin, and
the 46 peaks of the Adirondacks over 4,000 feet high.
He also enjoys snowshoeing, skiing and skating.
   He is a past president of the Green Mountain
Club, as well as of the Burlington Section, and has
been a trustee of the Club for many years. In 1948
he was elected and at the Skyland Conference in
1952, he was re-elected a member of the Board of
Managers of the Appalachian Trail Conference. He
prepared the chapters on that section of the Long
Trail used for The Appalachian Trail which appear
in the new Guide To The Appalachian Trail in New
England.
   Although he has now retired from his position on
the faculty of the University of Vermont, Prof.
Buchanan is still living in Burlington and still carry-
ing on his many outdoor activities and his trail work.

WHAT KIND OF A TREE IS THAT?
By VERA PERKINS

Looking through a hand-book on Trees which we received for Christmas, we were impressed with how much we didn't know! Along with this we recalled many discussions we'd heard while on hikes which went something like this:
"Is that a spruce tree?"
"No, I think it's a balsam."
"I thought balsam needles were light underneath."
"Well, I don't know. Could it be a hemlock?"—and so on.

We put these two thoughts together and decided maybe a short article might serve to help us increase our knowledge and also inspire further study for everyone.

Trees are a big subject so we are limiting our study this time to a few of the conifers—that is trees with needle-like or scaly leaves. Winter is as good a time as any to start this study. Among the conifers in our locality are several varieties of spruce and pine, hemlock, balsam fir, and juniper.

One characteristic which is helpful in distinguishing trees is their general appearance. We can learn to recognize many from their form and habit of growth. For example, spruce grow straight and tall, tapering up to a point, and have drooping, horizontal branches. Balsam Fir is a cone-shaped tree whose branches tend to point upward rather than droop. Juniper is a short, clumpy, spreading tree.

Anyone who has climbed Killington or any other fair-sized mountain does not need to be reminded that environment often alters the form of trees so they are less easily recognized by the general appearance.

There are other more specific characteristics which are helpful in identifying trees. The leaves (needles to us) of the conifers have their own distinguishing characteristics.

Pitch Pine has three needles in a cluster which stand out at right angles to the twig. Red Pine needles are a dark, glossy green and come in pairs, while White Pine has bluish green needles in clusters of 5. It is interesting to know that the needles stay on these trees for periods of from 2 to 5 years.

Spruce needles are in close spirals all around the twig and each needle is four sided. Balsam Fir has white lines on the under side of the needles. Hemlock has short, flat needles on small stalks in two flattened rows—they are darker above and silver lined underneath. Tamaracks shed their leaves in winter.

Cones contain the seed of this type of tree and these too differ. Pitch and White Pine cones have a prickle on them, while Red Pine cones are unarmed. Spruce cones are always hanging down. Hemlock cones are smaller. Tamarack ones grow upright. Junipers have modified cones the scales of which join to form a bluish berry which contains the seeds.

Observation of differences in the bark of these trees is another help in knowing them but it is difficult to consider these differences without a sample or at least a photograph.

Conifers have characteristic blossoms too. Have you ever noticed them?

Why not choose a tree, visit it at least once a month and get to know it thoroughly, bud, leaves, flowers, fruit, seasonal color, twigs and bark. The uses of its wood for an interesting study too.

Trees are all around us in infinite variety. They will stand still and let you look at them and it requires no expensive equipment to study them. This could well be an interesting and worthwhile hobby.

The above brings to mind the story of Col. Robert H. Montgomery, retired lawyer, who has an estate at Coe Cob in the town of Greenwich, Connecticut. In 1928 Col. Montgomery decided that he would "find a hobby." He did just that, and the hobby was collecting trees. His first step in the development of his hobby was to make a card index of every tree listed in Liberty Hyde Bailey's book on evergreens. Then he went to work looking for them. He not only found the evergreens he wanted but has grown and arranged them so beautifully that his collection is rated as most outstanding.

Two hundred of his choicest and rarest specimens have been moved thirty miles to the New York Botanical Garden. The cost of moving alone was $30,000. Collecting trees is no doubt an expensive hobby. At one time Col. Montgomery wished to buy a beautiful weeping hemlock. The nurseryman asked $1,500 for it. That being more than the Col. wished to pay, he told the nurseryman that if he lowered his price he (the Col.) might be interested. Sometime later the nurseryman parted with the tree for $500.

If we can't collect trees, we can know them and enjoy them. Let's do that.

ADIRONDAK LOJ CLUB TO REOPEN

Adirondak Loj Club, headquarters of the Adirondak Loj Chapter of the Adirondack Mountain Club, will reopen for the summer season the week end of Memorial Day when the Adirondack Forty-Sixers assemble for their annual spring meeting at Heart Lake. Forced to close down during the winter because of insufficient skiing patronage, the Club will again welcome the hiker, and those who just like to sit and look at the mountains, when these hardy bushwhacking ridge runners gather to swap tales of past climbs and possibly to repeat a few.

GMC members numbered among their ranks include Prof. Roy Buchanan and the late Prof. Lou Puffer.

Situated about 9 miles from the town of Lake Placid the Club offers the shortest approach to Mt. Marcy and other high peaks of the Adirondacks. Starting this summer the courtesy of Adirondack Mountain Club member rates will be extended to members of the Green Mountain Club. Address: Adirondak Loj Club, Lake Placid, N. Y.
