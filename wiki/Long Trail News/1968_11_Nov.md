ARTICLES OF ASSOCIATION
of
THE GREEN MOUNTAIN CLUB, INC.

I. We, the subscribers, hereby associate ourselves together as a corporation under the law of the state of Vermont to be known by the name of "The Green Mountain Club, Inc."

II. The purpose of the corporation shall be to make trails and roads in the Vermont mountains, to erect camps and shelter houses therein, to publish maps and guide books thereof, and in other ways make the Vermont mountains play a larger part in the life of the people.

III. The principal office shall be located at Rutland, Vermont.

IV. The corporation shall have no capital stock.

V. Members of said The Green Mountain Club, Inc., while so remaining pursuant to these article of association and its by-laws shall be entitled to the benefits, privileges, and advantages thereof and their rights therein shall terminate when they cease to be such members. Membership shall be non-assessable.

VI. These articles may be amended at any regular or special meeting, duly called, by a two-thirds vote of members present, provided written notice of such proposed amendment is mailed to each member at his last known address at least three weeks in advance of such meeting.

VII. This corporation is organized for charitable and educational purposes and not for profit.

TRAIL BILL SIGNED

Success After Four Years: On Wednesday, October 2, President Johnson signed the bill "To Establish a Nationwide System of Trails." This bill includes the Appalachian Trail for immediate action and the Long Trail as one of the several trails to be studied for possible future action. Stan Murray, Appalachian Trail Conference Chairman, was one of the honored guests at the signing ceremony which also included acceptance of the Redwood National Park, one Cascade National Park and the National Wild and Scenic Rivers System.

The new Trail Bill is of direct concern to all GMC'ers insofar as the Appalachian Trail uses the Long Trail for 95.3 miles from the Massachusetts Border to a point one-half mile north of Sherburne Pass. The Long Trail Patrol maintains the 17.9 miles of the A.T. east of Vt. Route # 12 and the New York Section maintains 27.3 miles of the A.T. in New York State and New Jersey (west of the Hudson River).

In my letter to each of the hiking clubs in New England, including the Green Mountain Club, I have urged the trail chairman of each club to very carefully review the existing route of the A.T. to be certain that it is placed in the very best location. We will want to cooperate as fully as possible with the Department of the Interior by having this information available for the first meeting of the Advisory Council.

It is, of course, too early to predict how soon the Advisory Council, including private citizens from hiking clubs representing the six regions of the A.T., as well as State and Federal Forest Service representatives, will meet. Nevertheless, I believe, we need to begin at once to see how each of the 54 clubs who maintain the A.T. and their members can most effectively promote the purpose of the Bill, which is to perpetuate the Appalachian Trail and maintain its continuity against encroaching suburbia, leisure home developments, and land development corporations.

I am hopeful that I will have more specific details to report to you after Ben Rolston and I attend the November 2nd meeting of the Appalachian Trail Conference Board of Managers to be held in Washington.

ARTHUR R. KOERBER, Vice Chairman
Appalachian Trail Conference
(New England Region)

OUTLYING TRAILS BULLETINS

Hikers who wish to know more about outlying trails and mountains which receive little or no mention in the GUIDE BOOK OF THE LONG TRAIL will be pleased to learn that the first three issues of the Club's new series of OUTLYING TRAILS BULLETINS are now available. Additional bulletins will be forthcoming shortly after winter snows force the Guide Book Committee to forsake notebooks and measuring wheels for drawing boards and typewriters.

Under the general plan adopted, each bulletin will be printed or mimeographed on 8 1/2 x 11 paper and will describe one or more mountains or a closely associated group of trail in a given area of Vermont. A detailed map showing trails and local road approaches will be an important feature of each bulletin. The text will include directions for finding the trail, trail measurement and descriptions notes about views and points of interest, and other information of interest and value to the hiker. All data and descriptive material used in the bulletins will be field checked to insure that the information is accurate, complete and up-to-date.

The following bulletins are now available:

Bulletin No. 1 - A Hiker's Guide to the Lake Willoughby Area (Wheeler, Pisgah, Bald)
Bulletin No. 2 - Mt. Norris
Bulletin No. 3 - Mt. Scrag and Mt. Cushman

To obtain your bulletins, send ten cents for each copy plus a self-addressed stamped envelope to the Green Mountain Club, P.O. Box 94, Rutland, Vermont 05701. Be sure your envelope is large enough to hold a bulletin about the size of a folded copy of THE LONG TRAIL NEWS.

Those who wish UNFOLDED bulletins should send a 9 x 12 envelope.

Other bulletins will become available during the winter. A complete listing of available bulletins and announcement of work in preparation will appear in each issue of the LONG TRAIL NEWS.

At present the Guide Book Committee has a working list of approximately 70 trails to be checked and considered for possible inclusion in future bulletins. But we are sure that Green Mountain Club members know of many others which are deserving of publicity. If you have information about local trails in or adjacent to Vermont, we would appreciate receiving it. The more details you can supply, the better, of course; however, at the very least, trail reports should include information about how to find the trails. The trail reports, incidentally, need not be confined to rugged wilderness peaks. There is a steadily growing interest in trails suitable for short afternoon walks, family groups and local sightseeing, and certainly some of the bulletins should include information about trails of this sort.

Please send your information about outlying trails to George Pearlstein, Box 115, West Pawlet, Vermont 05775.

LONG TRAIL PATROL

Another season is over for the Long Trail Patrol. We had a very busy summer and the Trail is practically standardized from French Camp to the Border.

About June 17th the Patrol started working at French Camp, an area which was in sad shape from there to Whiteface Mountain. This was due to lumbering operations. Clearing and relocating the Trail continued through the first week in July.

Following this the Patrol moved to Barrows and started north to Jay Peak. Construction of many ski trails had caused this area to be badly cut up. The Trail had to have at least a half mile of relocation around the north side of Jay Peak and is still in need of improving on the summit and westerly sides. During the last week of July we hired two more helpers from the Johnson area. They concentrated on the north end of the Trail from highway #105 south and Hazens Notch area, while the three original boys started work on Taft Lodge.

All of the lodges from French Camp to Journey's End have been inspected and repairs made. Water buckets have been replaced and there are new brooms and log books. Logging is now in progress around Ritterbush Pond but the camp is in good shape. Extensive repairs were made on the outhouse at Jay Peak and it is now in first class shape.

The Trail from Rt. 105 south to Jay Camp has been reblazed and the shelters have been creosoted. This section seemed to have quite a number of visitors during the Intersectional.

The Patrol spent three weeks at Taft Lodge. This lodge has had four logs put in on the south end, a new roof, floor replaced from the ground up, bunks repaired and a new outhouse is almost completed. The mountain company cooperated by hauling our supplies up to the top of the new lift line and furnishing transportation.

The truck was used at the Intersectional. It has made it through another summer with no major breakdowns. After the Intersectional work was done in different areas in the north again until the 27th of September.

Black flies didn't seem to be as plentiful this year for some reason. Also vandalism was at a minimum.

The north end of the Trail should be in good enough repair so that next year less time will be needed on it and the Patrol can help out elsewhere.

During the repair work at Taft Lodge various articles were uncovered as we took up the floor. Among these items was an ancient pinch bar, still good, also parts of utensils, clothes, lipstick and one Primus stove which may be claimed if the owner can identify it.

We welcome any information you may have on Trails and shelters conditions. This will help the trail crews make plans for next year, which I am sure will be another busy one.

Our thanks to the many people who helped us out with the repairs on Taft Lodge by carrying supplies and doing the actual repair work.

JACK HARRINGTON, Chairman
The Long Trail Patrol

NEW SHELTER ON MT. ASCUTNEY

The Ascutney Trails Association reports that a shelter for hikers is now ready for use on the Windsor Trail on Mt. Ascutney. Located at the Upper Spring (above the famous Half Way Spring) and named The Log Shelter, it is a 12' x 14' open face type, with back wall and one end of heavy spruce logs cut on the site; a vertical rock slab serves as the other end. Against the slab is a fireplace and 12' chimney, built of stones stone-boated down from the bank above.

Cutting a few trees has opened up a pleasant view to the north-east, featuring the Trafton Hoisington farm. Construction, under the general direction of Herbert Ogden, was begun on Memorial Day and completed on Sept. 18th, save for the finishing touches.

The Log Shelter now becomes the property of the State of Vermont, since it is on State land. It will be maintained by the ATA, as are all the hiking trails on the Mountain. Besides completing the shelter, the ATA has also reopened the original route of the Windsor Trail from the Half Way Spring to the Upper Spring, and back onto the present Windsor Trail. In addition, a new side trail has been opened from Blood Rock back up on to the Windsor Trail above the switchbacks.

MONTPELIER SECTION

   This has been a busy summer for the Montpelier Section. We finished the trail work between Madonna and Whiteface, then later in August went blueberrying on Mt. Hunger. There was plenty of competition for berries and nobody got very many. In September six members got up early and drove to Gore Mt. in the Northeast Kingdom. The views were unlimited from the fire tower and the trail not too steep.
   Our next hike was on a back road and we had an unexpected bonus in being joined by an engineer from Australia who was passing through and asked at the information booth what was going on in the way of hikes. He had been traveling by bus and intended to explore a few miles of the Long Trail before returning home. He was a pleasant companion—sixtyish, I should say.
   September 22, eleven ambitious members went to the Adirondacks, climbed Dix and Hough and made it back the same day. Finally we finished up September with a hike to Cantilever Rock and/or up to the Chin. Many other hikers were on the Mountain that last Sunday. The Adirondack buffs have an overnight planned for October and the White Mt. addicts will climb Mt. Hale.

FRANCES HOLMES, Reporter

TRAIL SIGNS - PLAIN OR FANCY

Trail signs provide the necessary information to guide the hiker on the trail. Should they be plain or fancy? Herbert Ogden made the attractive signs in the picture below and he explains his theory about a "fancy" trail sign and how to make them.

I use low grade pine boards from the lumber yard, knots and all. The lettering is laid out with a Stencil or E-Z Letter Guide. The letters are routed out with a Black and Decker router. About my only headache is keeping the router bits sharp enough to avoid fuzzy edges on the letters.

After the routing the whole thing is painted with wood preservative—the kind with a brown stain in it. The letters are then "painted," using a squeeze bottle (the one I use was designed for putting dye on one's hair) with a fine spout. The paint must be strained well, to avoid clogging of the spout. I just about fill the routed out letter with the paint. When it dries it goes a little concave, of course.

I should say that I put a "moulding" on the edge of the board, also with the Black and Decker router. This is not necessary, of course, but I do it in order to dress up the board a little bit more. My theory is that folks who ordinarily destroy or steal an ordinary board are, somehow, reluctant to do the same to such a "fancy" board. They figure someone must have put a lot of time into making it and they just can't bring themselves to steal it. In any event, we have had pretty good luck in having these signs left alone. I think I must have made around 200 for Gene Bamforth during the past 2 years. I expect a "mortality" report from him one of these days. Then I made them for the entire Killington Section and for Mt. Ascutney. On Mt. Ascutney 3 in particular were taken. I made replacements, but by the time I took them up to put on the trees the originals were there at the base of the tree. It looked as though some kids had taken them, shown them to pop and been told to take them right back where they found them!

I use two or three lag bolts to secure the sign to the tree and use a rubber washer, about 1 1/2" thick, between the board and the head of the lag bolt (which has a small washer on it, incidentally). This is to allow the tree to grow for some time without pulling the head of the bolt through the board. The bolts are generally 1 4", although down on the main highway I have been using, lately, 5/16" bolts to discourage stealing. I make them all pretty big (bigger than absolutely necessary) on the theory that folks are not so likely to steal something that cannot be readily hidden. I stamp the year of manufacture on the back with metal stamping dies.

That's the story! Now if you have some spare time this winter, perhaps you'd like to make some "fancy" signs for your section of the trail.

SKI TOURING TRIPS - WORKSHOPS
