EDITORIAL

Over the years there have been many people in the Green Mountain Club who have done outstanding work for the Club. Perhaps they never received the recognition or thanks that they deserved. They did not look for it or expect it. Their work was done under the heading of "labor of love." All this is as true today as it was in the past. However, we think that the LONG TRAIL NEWS, as a medium of expression of Club thinking, should be used at this time to say, "Thank you for a job well done," to Eugene Bamforth, Chairman of the Guide Book Committee.

The 1956-Fifteenth Edition of the Guide Book of the Long Trail is a book the Green Mountain Club may well be proud of. To complete this work, countless hours of labor were spent in checking and re-checking facts and figures to provide maximum accuracy. The text was rewritten, and the maps revised. We know that Eugene Bamforth did a great part of this work, even to living on the Trail for days at a time to get first hand, accurate information. As all hikers know, "nothing changes as much as the immovable mountains," so many changes had to be made in our new Guide Book.

In behalf of the Club, thank you, Gene. We do appreciate the work you have done in getting out the 1956 Guide Book.

REPORT OF TRUSTEES MEETING

At a meeting held in Rutland, Vermont, April 14th, thirteen trustees of the Green Mountain Club spent a full day on many items of Club business. A tentative budget was set up, reports from committee chairmen were read, and a number of suggestions for the good of the Club were made.

As the upkeep of trails and shelters is the main purpose of the Club, much time was taken up in the discussion of this subject. The trustees offered the following suggestions for consideration to the Trails and Shelters Committee: the placing of uniform registers in all shelters; the placing of informative

There are many shelters on the Trail in need of repair, so the Laura Woodward Shelter, that was destroyed by fire last year, may not get re-built this year. However, it is the intention of the Club to rebuild it eventually.

It was suggested that a set of pictures of Green Mountain Club Camps be kept on file at our office. These pictures should be named and dated as to the time they were taken. They would be some help in camp repair work. If anyone has pictures they care to donate for this purpose, send them in to Miss Hinchey, 108 Merchants Row, Rutland, Vermont.

Another request for information for our Card System of Trails and Shelters was made. Anyone using these facilities can help out by sending in a report of conditions found.

Green Mountain Club publicity has gone along in leaps and bounds this past year due to the hard work of a very active committee. Short Trip folders with a new look will be out soon. The 1956 Guide Book out since March 15th of this year, has been publicized in many ways; newspapers, mountain club magazines; window displays. Several book stores have been supplied with them. Miss Hinchey reports that 111 books have been sold to date and that the call for them is daily and steady.

Forty-five new Members-at-large have been added to our ranks, and three life members this past year.

The slide collection has been in constant use. Prof. Roy Buchanan has been out showing his very good collection six times during the last few months.

Arthur Koerber represented the Green Mountain Club at the New England Trail Conference at Amherst, Massachusetts, March 17th. Due to the stormy weather, many Green Mountain Club members who had planned to attend were unable to do so. Mr. Koerber suggested that a Club as large as ours should be able to give a comprehensive report that might contain helpful items of information about the Long Trail and the condition of our Shelters. For example he stated, "It would be of some help to other Clubs to know that at present there is no bridge at Clarendon Gorge." Not only is this meeting instructive, it is also entertaining. The afternoon program usually consists of illustrated lectures. Next year the New England Trail Conference will be held in April. If we hold a Long Trail Conference in the fall, it might be a good idea to give a copy of the report of that meeting to our representative to the N.E. Trail Conference.

Donald Kent, Allen Gormley, and George Petrocelli were appointed to serve on the Nominating Committee.

   Mr. Laddey, Mr. Phillips and Mr. Palmer were all active members until age forced them to cut down on their activities.
   We shall miss them all. May pleasant trails and wider views open up before them.

WORCESTER SECTION REPORTING

Worcester Section has been busy in January, February and March with a schedule of 8 local outings, one week end trip, one business meeting and a Valentine party.

Two of these outings (a combination of hiking and supper at a member's home) proved very popular. The first of these was held at Gertrude Cook's in Shrewsbury where skating, sliding and a pleasant easy hike were enjoyed. A picnic supper followed with hot soup and coffee served by the hostess. The second was a trip to the Upton State Forest, where a hike through the forest was taken (mostly through the snow) returning to the home of President Jim Green and his sister Frances, for a picnic supper with hot soup and coffee served by our hosts.

One of the outings at Camp Clarke in Holden, in charge of Mr. and Mrs. Forrest Thomson, had to be cancelled on account of a blizzard that hit Worcester late in the week. The other one was held as scheduled with John Knowlton, leader. The "Short Story" hour following supper turned out to be very interesting tales of members' experiences on the trails.

The business meeting and Valentine party was held at "Daniels House" of the Natural History Museum.

The winter week end at the Windham County House in Newfane, Vermont was as popular as ever with plenty of winter sports. Mr. and Mrs. Whitney, our hosts of the County House served sugar-on-snow on Saturday evening. Was it good!

Worcester had another blizzard on March 16th, so the mountaineering conference at Amherst lacked some Worcesterites who were "digging out" at home after the storm.

In April we have two outings and our sugaring-off coming up. The sugaring-off will be held at Camp Clarke on April 7th. President, Jim Green and the entertainment committee will be in charge. Mr. and Mrs. Hadar Sternstrom will show Kodachrome slides in the evening. April 21st, a local hike combined with a business meeting will be held at Camp Clarke. Mr. and Mrs. H. Alfred Williams will be leaders. This will also be spring camp cleaning day. April 29th a joint outing with the Metawaumpe Club of Amherst will be in charge of Donald Lacroix of the Metawaumpe's and Alan Gormly of GMC. The hike will extend from Crag Mountain to Mt. Grace, with a picnic lunch to be carried by members to be eaten on the trail.

Coming Events—The Long Trail clearing party, a bird walk, canoe trip, annual meeting at Rutland, a trip to Nickerson State Park on Cape Cod and the strawberry festival.

GUIDE BOOK EXHIBIT AT NEW YORK OFFICE OF VERMONT DEVELOPMENT COMMISSION

From March 19th through April 2nd, the Green Mountain Club had a window reservation at the New York office of the Vermont Development Commission for the display of the new Guide Books just published. Thanks to the enthusiasm and ingenuity of the director, Mary Lou Burditt, a Rutlander, the books were set up against an interesting likeness of the Green Mountains. A grass like carpet, such as florists use, was placed over wads of newspaper. A white cord designating the Long Trail, a miniature trail marker, and a wooden figure representing a hiker all helped to complete the picture. Along with the Guide Books, some of which were opened, cook books, and copies of the LONG TRAIL NEWS were also shown. As a background, the attractive poster which a Killington Section member had made, rested on a large easel. To either side of this, on smaller easels, two pictures of the Long Trail completed the framework.
In spite of the weather which caused competition with skiing interests, many inquiries about the Trail were made, and about thirty Guide Books were bought.

WHY CLIMB MOUNTAINS?

I suppose everybody in the Club has asked or been asked this question at some time or other and has been at a loss for an answer. I know I have. To get to the top; to see the view; to add one more climb to the total; these are the easy answers but are they the real ones?
In reading an article in the current issue of the DARTMOUTH ALUMNI Magazine, I came across several paragraphs that might hold the real answer. At least it set me thinking a bit. "Popular art can be enjoyed passively, great art requires creative imagination and effort. You can loll back and enjoy racing cars and cocktail music. But great art is difficult. It must be worked for, and the rewards are worth the effort.
"The rewards of great art are joy, a sharpening of the sensory system, more discrimination, an aliveness and awareness of things, a more sympathetic appreciation of others and especially of one's self.
"—There are penalties attached to the appreciation of great art. Because of it educated men experience self doubt and discontent, the shock of ugliness, and awareness of human cruelty and injustice, and a passionate desire for self-improvement and social betterment." (Good Teaching—A Case Study by John Hurd)
Substitute the word mountain for art and it looks as if you have an answer that you can think about. It gives a clue to the terrific and often tragic effort men make to climb Everest and other great Mountains. It also may explain why so many college professors enjoy mountain climbing.
TED GODDARD

THE MIDDLEBURY MOUNTAIN CLUB
REPORT ON THE MIDDLEBURY SECTION

As of our club's last check this past fall, the Middlebury section of trail from Mt. Horrid to Breadloaf Mountain is in good shape. Considerable trail work was done in the spring of 1955 and the last remnants of the lumbering operations are pretty well eliminated. Some areas are still a little obscure as a result of the numerous lumbered over areas, but these places have been fairly well marked.

Side trails have also received much work, the Voter-White, Burnt Hill and Skylight Pond trails now being in excellent condition. The Breadloaf and Silent Cliff trails still need some work (which will be done this spring) while the Boyce trail has been abandoned. Work is being started this spring to run a trail up Ripton Gorge from East Middlebury (on college property) which we hope to eventually connect with the Goshen trails.

North of Breadloaf Mountain the Long Trail is still in pretty rough shape. It is our intention, after completing work south of Breadloaf, to clear and mark as far north as possible or as needs attention.

With the publication of the 1956 edition of the Long Trail Guide Book, the two cabins of the Middlebury College Mountain Club are now listed. Both of them, Skyline Lodge (which we maintain is the best on the Long Trail) and Voter-White Lodge are in excellent shape. As far as other shelters are concerned, Sucker Brook and Breadloaf shelters still need more repairs, while Boyce shelter has been completely rebuilt (by the GMC this summer, I believe).

Having just returned from a three-day stay at Skyline Lodge, I am also able to make a report on present conditions of trails in that area as of March 31st. For the most part they are fairly well obliterated by about 8 to 10 feet of snow. For anyone intending to hike this area in winter I would strongly suggest that they know the trail beforehand, as the numerous lumbering roads can be very misleading. From my own experience, however, I would say that snowshoeing to Skyline Lodge in the heart of winter, is one of the most rewarding experiences a person could ask for.

MALCOLM McCALLUM, President
Middlebury College Mountain Club

A LONG STORY
6/15/55

On Sunday June 12, 1955 we left Randolph, Vermont to do a little skiing at Tuckerman's Ravine on Mt. Washington. Arriving there in late afternoon in a blinding cloudburst, typical of that region, we learned that the rains had so undermined the snow fields that skiing was prohibited. So, with broken hearts we decided to pack up into the ravine to do a little hiking.

To sleep in a cold, wet lean-to we were charged 50c a night per person. We stayed two nights and Tuesday afternoon, being thoroughly sick of the continual rains, we decided to move out and return to the Green Mts.

Wednesday our appetite for climbing and hiking not appeased we decided to climb Camel's Hump. We left Bolton at 5:30 P.M. to start our climb along the Long Trail. After a few hours climb we began to ask ourselves why we had never before seen such beauty in our native Vermont—the setting sun over Lake Champlain—range upon range of the softly carpeted Green Mts.—all such magnificent beauty that caused our old Vermonter's heart to beat proudly.

We pulled in here at 8:40 and set up housekeeping come morning.

6/16/55

A porkie wandered onto our front doorstep this morning—wanted a handout, or a spot of tea possibly; but having short rations ourselves, we sent him on his way. When last seen he was lumbering slowly down the mountains, forging away among the logs and stones.

The "Hump" was assaulted this morning and conquered at 11:35 A.M. What a magnificent view! We are still trying to find another way of saying tremendous. Out of the peace of the quiet afternoon, the "battle of the flies" started and continued unabated far into the night.

6/17/55

The flies are still around, but we are quite a lot the worse for wear.

And so on this glorious morning of June 17th we shall strike our way northward to an uneventful summer of work and the "civilian life," however, still mindful of the wonders, beauties and good times had on the Long Trail in our own Green Mts.

To whom it may concern—Thank you so much!

JORN S. BLACKMER
