   Since the business session will be reported elsewhere, let it suffice to say that we talked and heard reports, we ate a delicious luncheon, we talked some more, and then we adjourned. Following the afternoon session, those interested were divided into small groups and with able leaders from the Burlington and Killington sections scattered in search of birds, ferns, wild flowers, and other interesting flora and fauna. Despite the black flies that pursued the hikers relentlessly, driving some out of the woods entirely and onto the roadway, each group returned with reports of interesting discoveries along the way, well-pleased with their ventures.
   Attendance was slightly higher than that of last year. We missed several old friends, but met many new ones. We were especially glad to find not only most of the sections represented, but also a goodly number of members-at-large among those attending both business sessions and social events. We were pleased, too, to have the opportunity to meet and congratulate our three most recent "End-to-Enders" to receive their awards for having hiked the entire Long Trail; Miss Peggy Jones of the Barre Section and Dr. and Mrs. L. L. Dudley of Killington Section.
   Many stayed for the bean supper and sugarin' off in the evening. Since the humidity prevented the syrup from reaching its normal waxing temperature until later than we expected, some of us were a mite hungry before we ate. When the rich golden sweetness was finally poured over our plates of snow, however, we decided it was worth waiting for. After supper we adjourned to the chalet, and there heard an interesting talk on the life of a fire-lookout by one well qualified to tell about it. Our old friend, Miss Caroline Parmentier, whom we enjoyed meeting atop Stratton last summer, this year is manning the tower on Pico. It was a special treat to hear her describe the duties and responsibilities of a lookout in the Vermont Forestry Service. Inasmuch as three Long Trail shelters have burned in recent years, fire prevention and control are subjects especially close to us all. The evening program closed with the showing of colored slides, "Springtime in Europe," by Frances Green of the Worcester Section.
   Many members spent the night at the chalet, and sixteen campers stayed at the Perkins Farm which Mary and Craigue had so kindly made available again this year. The campers are circulating a petition to demand that Roy Buchanan be tethered out of reach of his car at the next such gathering; he sounded 5 a.m. reveille in what has become a traditional manner, by sounding his car horn.
   Sunday morning found our groups both scattering and gathering. Some had to leave early to start their long drive back to New York and other distant points. Others, however, joined the Rutlanders downtown to proceed to Spring Lake for an outing and picnic there. Twenty-five arrived at this beautiful spot in time for lunch and some of Craigue Perkins' "slow-boiled-for-that-extra-flavor" coffee, which was gratefully received. A sociably pleasant time was enjoyed by all, despite the showers that precluded our planned walks. Only Larry and Alberta Dean and a few of their Burlington contingent walked anyhow, having left Clarendon Gorge ahead of the shower only to arrive at Spring Lake soaked through. Once the party had broken up and started home in mid-afternoon, the sun came out again for the rest of the day, with a sort of rainbow finish to our pleasant weekend.

WORCESTER SECTION NEWS

The Worcester Section feted their Past Presidents at the G.M.C. Camp one Sunday in April. Over seventy-five members, past and present, gathered to honor the leaders of the club. A social hour and hike preceded a covered-dish supper after which movies of club activities were shown. Each President who attended gave one or two highlights of his reign. Inside the camp there is a border of small wooden pine trees around the top of the walls. There is a tree for each club year with the name of the President for that year inscribed upon it. We expect to live for some time, as the trees on the border date up to 1972!
   The "Spring Clean-up" on The Long Trail for this section was May 16-17. The work crew did a grand job. They also cut much of the needed timber to rebuild the open front shelter that was burned last year. The girls in the gang stayed at the Green Mountain House at West Wardsboro, and a few of the group hiked in to Stratton Pond to encourage (or hinder?) the workers. As usual it rained on Sunday but the men stayed on the job until time to leave for home. Those staying at the Inn attended the morning service at the West Wardsboro Baptist Church. In spite of hiking clothes, the minister very cordially welcomed us, and we more than doubled his congregation which was small because of the rain.
   Memorial Day week-end found seventeen G.M.Cers heading for Deerfield, N. H. Here we were guests of Dr. and Mrs. Edwin Olson at their summer home. Some folks accepted the hospitality of the farm house, others camped out, while five of the group set cots up in the barn and enjoyed (?) the company of a very noisy porcupine! Meals were prepared over a variety of portable stoves in a "cook-tent." Saturday evening was spent sitting around a large camp fire toasting marshmallows and singing lustily. Two very interesting hikes were enjoyed on this trip.
   Our annual Strawberry Festival was held at the club camp. As the reservations came in slowly, no program was planned. However, thirty-seven members attended, and a hike was taken after supper.
   The club camp was not in the path of the Worcester tornado, which passed by a short distance away. However, several G.M.C. members suffered losses, some slight and others of a more serious nature. Those who were not involved have been helping relatives and friends who were. Because of this, our attendance has not been, and probably for some time will not be, up to par.

NOTES FROM THE NEW YORK SECTION
  The Annual Meeting at the Long Trail Lodge on June 6th was attended by thirteen members of the New York Section. As usual, we enjoyed the good fellowship, meeting members from other sections, the talks, slides and even the business part of the day! Would we could bring a corner of beautiful Vermont home in our pockets to look at when the city seems hot and tired and the mountains very far away.
  Our July to November bulletin lists sixteen new members and thirteen guest card holders—potential members.
  Camp Thendara, open through the week as well as week-ends during the summer, offers a cool retreat even in hottest weather.
  Instead of the strenuous hikes of the cooler months, we find listed such walks as "Deep Shade Ramble to Thendara"; "A Day of Swimming, Walking and Sunning at Montauk Point"; "Summer Stroll"; "Tuxedo Slow Circular for Recuperating Vacationers". So you see we are active during the summer in a nice easy way.
  Over the Labor Day week-end we have a Long Trail trip scheduled to Stratton Mountain and are hoping for a good turn-out.
  What shall we say of our vacationing members? This year we seem to have them scattered all over the globe! What tales they'll have to tell and doubtless what pictures to show! A few of the places visited included Africa, India, Guatemala, points in South America and Europe, Puget Sound, Cold River, N.H., and even Grand Canyon and Yellowstone!
  The first fall dinner is scheduled for Sept. 24th. We always hear about the summer's travels then and catch up on lapsed friendships.
  To those in all sections who have not been away yet, happy days and a safe return.

VERMONT STATE CAMP CLINIC
  Craigue Perkins spoke briefly at the Vermont State Recreation Board's sixth annual Camp Clinic held in Woodstock, Vermont on May 21st. He told the group about the facilities of The Long Trail and gave advice on the use of Green Mountain Club shelters by camp groups.
  It was an all day meeting conducted by Mrs. Theresa S. Brungardt, Vermont Director of Recreation. Representatives from private camps, short term, and day camps were present.
  The featured speaker was Dr. Mary Beauchamp, assistant director for the community and field services at New York University. She told the camp directors that the emphasis of a good camp program should be on a theme of out door knowledge and skills rather than on too rigid scheduling.
  The Long Trail is being used more and more by summer campers. The knowledge of how it came to be, the work needed for its upkeep, and the privilege of being able to use it, perhaps never has been revealed to many who derive pleasure from it.
  Along this same line we read in "New England Trails," published by New England Trail Conference the following:
  "The problem presented by the use of camps and shelters by troops of scouts, summer camp groups, and others who monopolize them, often for long periods, and often cause considerable damage, was presented by several of the attending organizations. It has been a serious problem in certain New England areas for some time, and is becoming more acute. Several suggestions for dealing with the problem were made, but no solution was agreed upon. The great need for education in matters of trail use and responsibility was generally acknowledged."

KILLINGTON SECTION REPORTS

Early in May a Pancake Breakfast was enjoyed by a group of Killington Section members at Clarendon Lodge. After a lapse of some years, this event was put on the schedule again and proved to be popular especially with the young crowd.

The trip into Little Rock Pond on May 31st was, as usual, enjoyed by the nine hikers who made it. Flowers were found in abundance and so were the little black flies. On the way out, a few of the party went by way of the old road to South Wallingford. This route follows a pretty brook through the woods.

The Spring Lake Hike held June 7th after the annual meeting was well attended by GMCers but only four Killington Section members were present. However, all twenty-five who did attend had a grand get-to-gether.

Mountain climbing in hot weather is no doubt not the most comfortable sport to take on. Swimming is perhaps the ideal sport when the thermometer goes above 90 degrees. That is how we accounted for only four hikers who wanted to climb Killington by way of the Juggernaut trail that hot 21st of June. However, the four who conquered the heat as well as the mountain, claimed to be as comfortable as if they had stayed on their own hot back porches. Of course the flies had to be battled with, but the compensations, aside from conquest, were these: there was nothing in the world so delightful as placing hot, tired feet in a cool mountain stream, and the satisfaction derived from a drink of water that tasted better than anything else we could think of at that time.

Our Fourth of July Camping and Hiking trip covered that part of The Long Trail from Hazen's Notch to Parker Camp. Nineteen and one half miles of Long Trail and two miles of approach trail were covered in two days of hiking. A letter from two non-member hikers from Connecticut is being used elsewhere in this issue. It tells about conditions of the trails and shelters found in this area, so we will not repeat that information here. We had seven hikers and seven campers on this trip. The events of the camping party are reprinted from "Smoke & Blazes."

A CAMPER'S VIEW
OF THE BIG WEEKEND
by
LUCY BISHOP

Of course the "campers but not hikers" didn't get quite as much exercise on the Fourth of July weekend as the hikers did, but we had a grand time. Eleanor Walker, Pearl Nichols and I drove up Saturday morning, arriving at about 10:00 A.M. at the camp site selected by the hikers, who had long since been on their way.

Vera had left a note for us with directions for finding a spot about ten minutes' walk from the camp, with everything conducive to relaxation—sun, flat rocks, a gorgeous view. Sounded good to us, relaxation being our aim, so after inspecting the camp and organizing our belongings, we decided to look the place over. Since we started off without consulting the note again, we were a little hazy about details, the one thing we were all sure of being that the walk took about ten minutes. We strolled along the trail through the woods, finding the climb easy and the woods delightfully cool. Taking into consideration the speed at which we were travelling and the number of times we stopped to investigate, we expected the tour would take us about fifteen or twenty minutes, but when we had walked for half an hour and still not found the sunny opening, we had a slight suspicion that we were heading in the wrong direction. Another ten minutes confirmed the suspicion, so we headed back down the trail, the camp coming into view at exactly the right moment—lunch hour!

We enjoyed a leisurely meal, congratulating ourselves all the while on having accomplished a hike on our own, however brief and unscheduled. Following lunch we found the place we had been looking for in the morning—it was ideal! A cool breeze offset the sun, and the flat rocks were meant for lazing around with a book, also for sandwiching a cat nap between chapters!

Despite the lazy afternoon, we had keen appetites for dinner. Just as we were finishing our dessert the Fields—Helen, Fred, Tommy and Peter drove in, swelling the number of campers to seven. Not too much later the hikers, also seven strong, arrived, none the worse for the miles they had tramped. After they had finished eating and cleaning up, we all sat around the campfire rubbing on insect dope and exchanging notes on the day's experiences—until the flies and mosquitoes decided it was time for us to retire and saw to it that we did.

Sunday morning, after seeing the hikers off on the trail, and the Fields on their homeward way, we ate breakfast and proceeded to continue our program of sunning, napping, and reading from the point where we had dropped it on Saturday. Again, surprisingly our appetites were definitely present at noon, and we stowed away a huge lunch.

When we had accomplished the necessary cleaning up and packing away we set out to spend the afternoon hiking through the countryside—via Pearl's car! It was an A-1 hike with perfect weather, good roads, breath-taking views and double-decker frozen custard cones!

Target—one perfect weekend. Mission accomplished.

WEBSTER DAY TO BE STRATTON ANNUAL FEATURE

GMCers will recall the spot of last year's Intersectional and the bronze marker in honor of Daniel Webster who spoke at that site in 1840 to an audience of 15,000 people. It has been decided by a group of Stratton residents to make Daniel Webster Day an annual event in Stratton, with emphasis on political speakers from the local, state, or national level.

The site is to be developed as a recreation spot. Two picnic tables are to be erected and the water supply is to be improved. The property is owned by the Windham County Historical Society.

BURLINGTON SECTION NEWS

The Burlington Section was well represented at the G.M.C. Annual meeting. Since then there have been three Trail clearing trips. North of Bolton Mt. there is still some work to be done.

On July 4th and 5th eighteen members camped in the yard and on the piazza at Alice Rogers' and Helen Drews' in North Troy. On the 5th ten climbed Owl's Head in Canada on the shore of Lake Memphremagog. There is a beautiful view of the lake here right at the eastern foot of the mountain.

Another delightful Lake trip was taken July 19th when section members went out to Haag's camp on Lake Iroquois (formerly Hinesburg Pond).

A boat trip on the Ticonderoga is scheduled for August 2nd. The boat leaves Burlington at 2 P.M. and arrives back at 7 P.M. Passengers may leave the boat at Essex, New York and be picked up there later, if they want to, or they may stay on the boat for the entire trip. Of course there is always the possibility of "missing the boat" so we are not surprised to hear that one Burlington Section member has said, "I don't advise getting off at Essex. I won't dare get off if the boat sinks, even if the Captain orders us to abandon ship."

At the site where the Puffer Lodge is to be built between Bolton Mt. and Mayo Mt., work is progressing slowly. Roy and Chester Buchanan expect to spend some time up there working the week of July 26th.

Larry Dean is in California with thirty of his scouts.

IT PAYS TO TALK TO HIKERS

Not long ago Craigue Perkins saw two young hikers with packs on their backs passing through Rutland. He stopped to talk with them and found out that they were hiking on The Long Trail. They were going to do a stretch of trail that the Killington Section expected to do later so he asked them to write to him about their trip. The following is their letter:

Baxter Drive,
So. Norwalk, Conn.
June 28, 1953

Dear Mr. Perkins:

Our luck was with us and we had good rides right up to the road where the Trail crosses about a quarter of a mile from Hazen's Notch Camp. We arrived at Hazen's Notch around 6:30 P.M. on the 15th of June. The camp was in very good condition with plenty of wood in the box. Found only one good sized tree over the Trail and no trouble to follow at all.

We headed south around 9:30 A.M. the 16th. Crossed the road and found fairly easy going. The markers are fading and about two hundred yards from the road near knocked down cabin it is a little confusing. We got off on a blind trail and lost about twenty minutes. We doubled back and from then on it was O.K.

There are several good sized trees across the Trail a short way from Tillotson Camp. We arrived in scheduled walking time and found the camp in excellent shape. Left Tillotson Camp on the 17th at 9:30 A.M. The Trail was well marked except for one spot about a quarter of a mile from Belvidere Lookout. This is the area where a few hundred trees are down due to a storm.

We arrived at Ritterbush around 4 P.M. The camp was in rotten shape thanks to the porkies. They had really taken over lock stock and barrel. They had chewed through the floor and went to town on the bunks, table etc. Took us an hour to clean up and set the place in livable condition. We nailed the wire around the base of the cabin and piled plenty of stones where we thought they would do the most good.

Didn't get much sleep thanks to the N.G. porkies. I beaned a few of them with boulders but they didn't seem to mind. Guess I was too tired to give them my best.

Left camp on the 18th—7:30 A.M. Found the Trail overgrown from Ritterbush to Butternut Mt. but fairly easy to follow. From Butternut Mt. to Parker Camp the Trail is excellently marked. Brother Anthony sprained his knee around Devil's Gulch and we lost about an hour getting to Parker. No need to mention we found Parker Camp in tip top shape. We planned on going on to Barrows Camp on Friday but Anthony's leg was still giving him trouble so we stayed over taking it easy. I walked out to the main road and hitched in to Johnson for a paper and a little chow. The side trail to the main road from Parker Camp is very well marked.

Well on Saturday the 20th we hiked out and thumbed down to Norwalk, Connecticut. The motorists were good to us and we were in Norwalk around 3 A.M. Sunday.

The next trip we plan on starting at Parker and walking south for two weeks—God willing.

Please accept our thanks for the use of your G.M.C. trail and shortly we'll be joining you as members.

EMMETT & ANTHONY ROGERS

MOUNTAIN NAMES

An interesting project and one that requires some research, is that of finding the source of the names given to our mountains. If any reader of the "News" knows the story of the naming of any Vermont mountains, especially those crossed by The Long Trail, we would be pleased to receive the information for publication.

The following story was taken from an editorial in the "Rutland Herald."

Bird Mountain

A dispatch to The Herald from E. Poultney described how a group of people made a recent ascent of what was erroneously called "Bird's Eye Mt."

The mountain in question, presumably, is the mountain in the southeast part of the town of Castleton, with silhouette made notable by the abrupt cliff forming its southern face. It was named for Col. Amos Bird, who was the principal proprietor of the town, and came there from Salisbury, Conn., in 1766, to make surveys. He is said to have climbed the mountain, and used it as a base point for surveying.

Col. Bird returned to the town as a settler, and built the first sawmill, at the outlet of Lake Bomoseen. He died in the midst of his exertions. It is said that the first boards sawed at the mill were used to make his coffin.

The name has sometimes been corrupted to Bird's Eye, probably because the users were unfamiliar with the historic account, and thought the name Bird was assigned because of the "bird's eye view" obtainable from the summit. Such views can be had from the summits of most Vermont mountains. But the name of this mountain comes very definitely from Col. Bird, whose activities as a pioneer settler merit the honor awarded by his fellow citizens nearly 200 years ago.

FROST MOUNTAIN?

While we are on the subject of the naming of mountains it might be well to mention the fact that at one time the Green Mountain Club considered doing something about naming a mountain after poet, Robert Frost.

What has become of that idea?

Robert Frost has a home in Ripton, Vermont, near Breadloaf. It would seem that The Green Mountain Club would be the logical group to sponsor such a movement.

If one of America's most distinguished men of letters, thinks enough of the mountains of Vermont to make his home close to them, representatives of that State should recognize its indebtedness to him.

A LOST IDEA

Another idea that has been lost somewhere in the shuffle of important events is the idea to make The Long Trail known better by way of conducting a contest in the schools of New England and New York. The Green Mountain Club could afford to offer a prize or two, to the high school student or students who submit the best essay about The Long Trail, showing actual participation of a hike on some part of the Trail and a sound knowledge of the history and purpose of the Trail.

The above idea was brought to mind after a Killington Section member reported a conversation he had with two high school boys he met at Cooper Lodge on Killington. When asked why they never hiked with The Green Mountain Club, the boys replied, "Never heard of it." When questioned further about The Long Trail, it appeared that that was news to them also. They did know of the trail from Long Trail Lodge to Killington, and that was the extent of their Trail knowledge.

If there ever was a time in the history of mountain climbing when the stories of the mountains was interesting to the public, that time is being equaled today. With the story of the conquest of Everest uppermost in the minds of many, people are in a receptive mood to hear more about mountains. Why not tell them about the Green Mountains and The Long Trail?

FRANCES GREEN IN "VERMONT LIFE"

Another story by Frances Green, of the Worcester Section, was published in the summer issue "Vermont Life." This time Frances tells about a "Lake in the Woods—A trip to Little Rocky Pond in the Green Mountain National Forest." Both the words and the pictures do justice to that delightful spot. Her color picture of the Pond is strikingly beautiful.
