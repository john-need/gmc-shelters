NOTICE OF ANNUAL MEETING, by Herbert G. Ogden, President, Killington Section, Green Mountain Club
The Annual Meeting of the Killington Section of the Green Mountain Club will be held at 1 p.m. on Sunday 13 September.
Unless a member objects, it will be held entirely remotely, by Zoom. Zoom invitations will be sent to anyone who requests
them. Anyone who timely objects to meeting by Zoom may meet in person at the pavilion area of Rotary Park off North
Street in Rutland City. If there is an in-person meeting, members will still have the choice of participating remotely by
Zoom, and the Zoom host will broadcast the meeting from the above location. This would be technologically challenging,
but the Green Mt. Club has succeeded in holding hybrid Zoom and in person meetings before.

Any members meeting in person must be at least six feet apart, wearing cloth masks. They should bring their own chairs
and (if desired) refreshments. No one may meet in person if sick or symptomatic with COVID-19 (with fever, cough,
shortness of breath or difficulty breathing, chills, repeated shaking with chills, muscle pain, headache, sore throat, or new
loss of taste or smell).

To request a Zoom invitation, please email Section President Herb Ogden, hogden@vermontel.net.

To request an in-person meeting, please email Herb or US-mail him at 214 South End Road, Mt. Tabor VT 05253. So
that the Zoom host can know whether to go to the above location, anyone wishing an in-person meeting must notify Herb
by 4 September. If there are no requests, the meeting will occur entirely by Zoom.

The purpose of the Annual Meeting is to nominate and to elect Directors, hear reports of the Officers and the G.M.C.
Director, plan for the coming year, and transact any other appropriate business. Regardless of how you attend, please
participate!

►   COVID-19 UPDATE, by Sue Thomas, Outings Chairman
    Sadly, with the future uncertain and the anticipated second wave and flu season looming, this does not seem the
    time to resume group hikes. Most hike leaders I spoke with agreed that they would not like to be enforcers for
    the long list of regulations imposed by the main club at this time, and most just plain felt it was too soon. While
    Vermont's numbers remain low, we are still under a state of emergency, and all need to do our part to control
    community spread.

    That said, I urge everyone to get outside! When I hike in the woods and mountains, hear the birds sing and see
    wildflowers and beautiful views, it's hard to believe there is anything wrong in the world. I feel so fortunate,
    to live in such a beautiful place, with so many opportunities to be outside. Also, the main club is posting weekly
    suggestions to help replace group outings: https://www.greenmountainclub.org/hiking/hikevt/. When the
    Killington Section does resume outings, they will appear on the Section’s website as well as in the newsletter.

►   Fern Picking in Vermont, by Amy Peacock
            We’ve all seen them, whether near the trail or in floral bouquets: Nephridium Spinulosum Intermedium,
    the “Fancy Fern”. This fern is common in Vermont forests where it grows vigorously in the moisture and shade
    found there.
            According to the Mt. Tabor-Danby Historical Society website, the fern picking industry in Vermont was
    one of the few businesses that prospered during the Great Depression. The M.L. Ackert Fern Company, based in
    Danby and founded in 1917, was a leading supplier of ferns to florists, hotels and fish markets around the United
    States for many years.
            Chick Ackert, grandson to company founder Peter I. Ackert, explains in his story, The Beginning of the
    Ackert Fern Industry in Danby, VT, on the historical society’s website, that each fern picker built one or two pack
    boards, 2-feet wide and 3-feet long with shoulder straps to carry the picked ferns out of the woods. A load at the
    end of each day, he writes, was typically around 5,000 ferns; the price paid for ferns in the early season started at
    40 cents per 1,000. Mr. Ackert states: “No fern picker ever got rich, but I never knew one to starve, either.”
            To read more information and to see some wonderful photos of fern picking in Vermont, please go to the
    Mt. Tabor-Danby Historical Society website (mtdhistoricalsociety.org) and click on Learn More.

Outing Reports
        Since we don’t have any official outings to report due to the pandemic, the following are a few articles and
pictures of what some of our members have been doing for fun this summer.

My Adventure at Bald Mountain, by Isaac Jones
         On a very sunny Friday, my mom, sister and I went to the Bald Mountain hiking trail in Mendon. It’s a medium skill
hike that takes about 3-4 hours and is approximately 4 miles long. We checked out two lookouts that have a beautiful
view of the other mountains in the distance. Throughout the hike, we discovered lots of animals, from birds to chipmunks,
to even various sizes of toads. When my sister, Claire, picked up a toad, it peed on her hands and, guess what, it
happened again! (I thought that was funny.) We also saw a Downy Woodpecker climbing up a skinny tree. Also, at the
parking lot, we met a plump white chicken (see below), who looked like he was running away from a butcher. Bald
Mountain has a few different trails to take to get to the end. Overall, I like this hike a lot and I recommend you to try. If
you prefer a shorter hike, you can do a 2.4-mile hike by turning around once you get to the first lookout.

Silver Lake, by Claire J. Jones
         My Mom, Isaac and I, along with our friends, Noah, Benjamin and their mom, Carrie, went on a hike to Silver
Lake. We caught polliwogs and Benjamin saw a huge Bullfrog! We also saw a crayfish and swam in the lake. Ben
caught two polliwogs and Noah caught one.
         Our moms ate lunch and watched us play. One of our moms saw a chipmunk. Now this isn’t any ordinary
chipmunk. It was a one-eyed chipmunk.
         On the way back, my Mom saw me step on my shoelace, but when I stepped away she noticed it was a snake!
After that, I named it the shoelace snake. (It was probably a harmless garden snake!)

Trash Pickup Time, by Claire J. Jones
        Sue Thomas shared a hike called Wheelerville Road. On our first hike, we saw a lot of cans, bottles and trash
scattered throughout the road. On our second hike, we brought plastic bags and a claw to round up the trash and
returnable items. We brought it to a bottle redemption center along with our bottles from home. My brother and I split
the money evenley.

History and Mystery on a Road Walk in the Hill Country of Bethel, by Ruth Hare
         With enough imagination, this outing could take the travel-starved hiker to the summit of Mount Olympus, home
of the Greek gods. In reality, Sue and I were happy to reach a more modest goal: Lympus, Vt., home of . . . well, we’ll
get to that.

This crossroads in the town of Bethel carries an air of mystery and lore. According to online research, it was
indeed once named Olympus, although I found no explanation for the disappearance of the “O.” Vestiges remain in the
way the local newspaper, the Herald of Randolph, sometimes apostrophizes the area’s historic name: ’Lympus. Both
Sue and I knew of Lympus, but had never visited. Looking for interesting road walks that would allow us to be social, but
distanced, we set off one June morning on a roughly 7.5-mile “lollipop loop” route.
          We started from an informal parking spot along River Road in Stockbridge, just east of the bottom of Lilliesville
Road and not far from Gaysville. Heading up Lilliesville Road, we walked alongside a brook that clearly had been
reshaped by Tropical Storm Irene. After staying to the right at the intersection with Whittier Road, we continued a long,
but fairly gentle, climb to Lilliesville, a leafy settlement with an air of peace that belies its history as a busy industrial
center. A flagpole proudly marks the crossroads at its heart. According to the Valley News, in the 1850s the village had a
tannery, a “wool-pulling shop,” a slaughterhouse, three sawmills, a lodging house and at least one barrel-making
operation. But the area fell into decline once the railroad came to Bethel village, siphoning off business activity. Today,
this cluster of lovely old homes seems like the perfect place to climb onto a stranger’s porch, pour a glass of lemonade
and escape the modern world.
          Pressing on another mile or so, we reached a four-corners surrounded by high rolling fields, where we spent
several minutes attempting to answer the question: Is this it? The answer came as a sign from God. Specifically, a sign
reading “Bethel Lympus Church. Built 1841” (picture below) was attached to a simple, beautifully-restored, white church,
which still hosts community events, but online fundraising is underway for a new roof.
          Our walk continued southwest on Gay Hill Road in the shadow of Mount Lympus (close enough!), passing the
church and soon a wetland with a family of resident ducks (picture below).
          At the next corner, we met a number of other Lympus residents, resting quietly in the local cemetery. They
include 11 Civil War veterans, according to vermontcivilwar.org. Local families show a flair for alliteration, with stones for
Birt Buttles, Coralina Castle and Minnie Magivney. Other eye-catching names (thanks to findagrave.com) include
Manasseh, Roe Ann, Milon, Zilpha and Jeduthan (born 1753, died 1836). But we can only guess what it was like to be
married to Experience Pedee Partridge French.
          A left turn at the cemetery took us onto narrow Whittier Road, a pleasant tunnel through the trees that leads
downhill back to Lilliesville Road, closing the loop. Retracing our steps back to River Road ended our own experience,
one we’d like to repeat and would recommend for others. Link to the route on Google Maps: https://rb.gy/1x3eux.

Vermont Bike Tour, July 20-24, by Larry Walter
        This is something Dave Coppock and I hatched up a couple months ago that sounded like a good way to give our
spouses a break from us and do some touring. Vermont, having relatively low Covid numbers, was a good state to
remain in. Our group was comprised of 5 middle aged men, two mechanical engineers, a child psychologist, a retired
carpenter and a worn out surveyor. I was the oldest and had the heaviest bike. Dave designed the route, so everyone
knew it would be, at times, insanely difficult, but also very pretty. No surprises there. We rode on gravel and back
roads as much as possible through Calvin Coolidge State Park in Plymouth (Dave and I took the CCC road in
Shrewsbury), Allis State Park in Brookfield (went for a swim at the floating bridge), Sugar Ridge RV Campground in
Danville, Lake Champaign RV Campground in Randolph Center (just up the hill from VTC) and then home to Rutland via
Bethel and Killington. The floating bridge in Brookfield has a very interesting history . . . well worth a web search.
        We saw some beautiful country and only a couple depressed little towns. It rained from Joe’s Pond to a little golf
course at the height of land, where we made a white knuckled descent on soft, mushy gravel/mud. Our highest
temperature was around 91 degrees at Floyd’s Market in Randolph Center. Prettiest town: Peacham. Nicest
