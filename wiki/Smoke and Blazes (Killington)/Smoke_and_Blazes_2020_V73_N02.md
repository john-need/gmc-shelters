►   MESSAGE ON PANDEMIC, by Herb Ogden, Killington Section President.
    Like almost everything else, the Killington Section has been affected by the COVID-19 pandemic. For a while, all
    trail work was forbidden. The main reasons seem to have been (1) trail workers sometimes must use the
    Emergency Room, and we wanted to save all the ER capacity for folks with the virus and avoid contact with them
    in the ER, (2) we needed time to figure out who could work together relatively safely, and how, and (3) many
    people traveled over ten miles to do trail work, but the State guidelines limited non-essential travel to ten miles.
    By the time you read this, probably small groups will be allowed to do all sorts of trail work as long as they
    routinely stay six feet apart, don’t share tools, and don’t car pool with people from outside their own households.
    Probably small group hikes will be allowed if distance is maintained and car pools are restricted. I doubt if general
    use of shelters and outhouses will be allowed, because present research indicates the virus can linger even on
    porous surfaces long enough after being touched by a vector to infect the next person who touches that surface.
    But all this could change. Maybe Vermont will have minimized the spread of the virus so well that hiking and
    camping will be less restricted. But maybe we will let down our guard or invite people from highly infected areas
    to come to Vermont, to the point where the infection rate increases and restrictions will have to be re-imposed.
     Please keep up to date by visiting COVID-19 part of the website for the Green Mountain Club at
     https://www.greenmountainclub.org/covid-19-response/.

    On account of the pandemic, we postponed the Annual Meeting that was scheduled for April 19th. The Directors
    have not set a new date yet. If in-person meetings continue to be restricted to fewer people than may want to
    attend, or if the Directors decide that an in-person meeting is not safe regardless of what the State allows, we
    may have to conduct a meeting with a service like Zoom or vote for officers by an email ballot. The Montpelier
    Section has already done the latter. But we have email addresses for only about a quarter of our membership.
    Consequently, to allow electronic meeting or voting if necessary, could you please send your preferred
    email address to Section Secretary, Allison Henry, allisonnorton1@gmail.com? Of course, if you received
    this newsletter at your preferred email address, you need do nothing.

    Finally, an item that’s more fun: In 2019, Killington Section volunteers spent about 76.7 hours getting to work
    sites and about 287.9 hour working on the LT and side trails and preparing for work parties. This does not count
    a day of work by about 20 volunteers from Wayfair in Boston. There are always ways to improve our part of the
    Long Trail and our side trails. To help, please contact either of the Trails & Shelters Co-Chairs, Larry Walter
    at lwalter@sover.net, or Wayne Krevetski at wkrevetski@hotmail.com.

Now summer is upon us and trails are opening once again to hikers. However, many restrictions remain in place.
        After much consideration we have decided along with other GMC sections not to publish a hike schedule at this
        time. Until we are able once again to carpool, hike and gather less than 6 ft. apart and leave the face masks at
        home, it just doesn't make sense to plan group outings.

        As the summer progresses, if more restrictions are lifted and leaders once again feel comfortable with it, we may
        try a paddle or a bike ride, so watch our section's website or make sure you are on our email list. In the
        meantime, get outside, be safe,and look on the bright side -- wearing a face mask means less exposed skin for
        the black flies!

        For the latest information on trail closures, restrictions and COVID-19 hiking etiquette, go to
        www.greenmountainclub.org.



                                                 Outing Reports
Merck Forest & Farmland Center, Rupert, March 8, 2020, Vivian Bebee and Larry Walter, leaders
         Eight of us, plus Burdock, traveled to Merck Forest for what turned out to be a very pleasant hike. The weather
definitely did not disappoint either . . . bright blue sky, lots of sun and no wind to speak of. There was still snow on the
ground, but not of any depth, and some places along the way were completely bare.

From the Visitor Center, we hiked along Old Town Road past the Sugar House and barns, continuing up the steep section
to where Old Town Road meets Antone Road. There we left Old Town Road and took the Lodge Road over to Meyer
Road, which led us to the short, side road into Ridge Cabin. As we approached the Cabin from the back side to the front,
we had a “WOW” moment. It had been a several years since we had visited Ridge Cabin and I wasn’t expecting the view
to be so expansive. We stayed there a while having lunch, soaking up the sun and just enjoying our good fortune. I
would have loved to stay the night, but the Cabin was already rented. We were fortunate though in that whoever would
get to enjoy it next had not arrived yet.

On the way back, five of us, plus Burdock, returned to the Visitor Center the way we came and the other three took a
longer, more scenic route. While we waited for Larry, Mark and John to return, Sue and I had a nice visit with Ellie, the
resident cat. We sat in front of the fireplace at the Visitor Center and Ellie made good use of Sue’s lap. The picture below
was taken on this hike, as well as those on the front cover, Page 2 and Page 6.

Long Trail Lower Elevation Spring Cleaning, Shrewsbury/Wallingford, May 9, 2020, by Larry Walter
Crew One (Larry Walter and Bill Cooke). We cleared out all of the water bars going north from Route 103 to within a
couple hundred feet of the top of that rocky gap, where we cut out one blowdown. Then worked our way south from
Lottery Road and removed two pretty good-sized hang-ups to about five minutes south of Clarendon Shelter. There are
some loose floor boards that need to be screwed back down, right on the front of the main platform. Everything else
looked good. Our combi tool broke, but there were only about four water bars that still needed attention. We kicked the
leaves out of the others. It can wait till next year. Blazing looked good.

I recently handed over two sharpened crosscut saws to David Morrisey along with some handles on loan. The Bread Loaf
Section now has what they need for the wilderness sections.

General requests: If anyone gets back up to that overlook that's north of Route 140, we have a sign for that. I also have
a replacement for "Shewsbury" sign and several other LT N and LT S signs for use. We have a sign with mileage north
and south that needs to be placed at the little register, just north of Upper Cold River Rd.

Crew One Point Five (Herb Ogden). Herb cleaned waterbars from Lottery Road south to about 10 minutes south of
Beacon Hill where he met Bill and I. All four of us then spent about 45 minutes re-routing a chronic run-off problem just
south of Lottery Road, next to a farmer’s field. It’s much drier now. Herb then drove around to Sherburne Pass trail and
hung up a trail register he’d reconditioned on the same tree, just south of Route 4. He also replaced the LT North and
South signs at Route 103 a couple days later.

Crew Two (Dave Coppock and Wayne Krevetski). Dave and Wayne started up the Canty Trail to do several things.
This is what they got done:
         - Trimmed several smallish blowdowns out of the way.
         - Replaced a rotten 6' puncheon plank.
         - Set rock stepping stones in some perpetually boggy areas.
         - Cleaned water bars from the base of the steep climb to the Cascade.
         - Removed the old "Cascade" sign and loosened the lag screws on the other one.
         - Rebuilt the rock step around the enormous fallen tree just below the Cascade.
         - In this same area, there is an erosion problem. A chute of water coming out of the woods is gouging a trough
alongside the trail. Dave filled in the trench as best he could with rock. He kicked loose a one-ton rock perched
precariously just above the trail. It landed in the middle of the trail and he couldn't budge it from there. Two guys
with 5' bars could get it moved, though.
         - Blazing is good for the first mile of the trail, but after the main brook crossing, it is old and faded.
         - About 150 meters from the parking, there is a complicated blowdown tangle. It is possible to get under it easily
at this point, but will need attention in the next year or so. We saved the old “Cascades” sign for Herb, who had originally
placed it.


Long Trail Trivia Answers
       1. Mt. Abraham
       2. South side of Rutland-Woodstock Road in the Sherburne Pass area, near the Long Trail Inn, which was first
           operated as a Club House annex.
       3. Furthest North is Journey’s End Camp, built in 2003 to replace the original Journey’s End Camp that stood
           from 1931-2004. Visit the old camp at the Green Mountain Club headquarters on the “Short Trail.” Furthest
           South is Seth Warner Shelter.
       4. The name comes from the dissolved town of Mansfield, Vermont, in which the mountain was located. It was
           common for settlers to name Vermont towns for their previous homes; several of the original grantees were
           from Mansfield, Connecticut, which in turn is known to have been named for Moses Mansfield, one of the chief
           landowners there. The Town of Mansfield was platted before anyone involved had visited the site. The town
           later dissolved. Sadly, not named after it’s ridgeline that some say resembles the profile of a human’s face.
       5. Taft Lodge, built in 1920.
       6. Since Europeans first saw the mountain, probably Samuel de Champlain’s 1609 trip down Lake Champlain to
           the west, the mountain has had a number of names related to its distinctive shape. De Champlain named the
           mountain “Le Lion Couchant,” a heraldic image which roughly translated as “The Resting Lion.” Ira Allen later
           referred to the mountain as “Camel’s Rump” on a map from 1798. It was not until 1830 that the name
           “Camel’s Hump” came into usage.
       7. Bolton Lodge, Bryant Camp, Beaver Dam Camp and Hadsel-Mares Camp.
