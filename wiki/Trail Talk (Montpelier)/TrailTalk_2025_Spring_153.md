                                                                  Trilliums have a whorl of three heart-shaped leaves,
                                                             three sepals and a single flower with three petals. There
                                                             are a few dozen kinds of trillium and several in Vermont.
                                                             Painted trillium (trillium undulatum) has a white flower
                                                             with red veins, acting as an insect’s guide to nectar, and
                                                             grows in acidic, humus-rich soils underneath white pine,
                                                             red spruce, balsam fire, and red maple. Red trillium (tril-
                                                             lium erectum) is also known as wake-robin (because red-
                                                             breasted robins return in spring) or stinking Benjamin
                                                             (because of its odor, which attracts carrion flies). It grows
                                                             underneath beech, birch, and maple trees.
                                                                  Round-lobed hepatica (hepatica americana) has
                                                             leaves of 3 rounded lobes. Sharplobed hepatica (hepatica
                                                             acutiloba) has leaves of 3 pointed lobes. Both have hairy
                                                             stalks and flowers (6-10 petal-like sepals), which may be
                                                             white, pink, lavender, or blue. The flowers emerge amidst
                                                             last year’s withered leaves. They grow in leafy woods.
                                                                  Carolina spring beauty (claytonia caroliniana) has
                                                             5-petaled pink or white flowers veined with darker pink.
                                                             Like painted trillium, the veins act like a runway, guid-
                                                             ing insects to the flower’s nectar. The leaves of Carolina
                                                             spring beauty are broader than Virginia spring beauty
                                                             (claytonia virginica), which grows further south and at
                                                             lower elevations.

Zack Porter Talk
                                                                         Zack Porter, the Executive Director of Standing Trees,
                                                                    gave a talk at the Hayes Room of the Kellogg-Hubbard
                                                                    Library on January 7th. It was a cold and snowy night,
                                                                    but there were 23 people there to hear him speak. The talk
                                                                    was titled Future Wild: the critical importance of protect-
                                                                    ing Vermont and New England’s public forests.
                                                                         Zack’s preview description of his talk stated that
                                                                    “Vermont and New England as a whole have experienced
                                                                    profound changes over the last four hundred years: from
                                                                    thriving indigenous communities and high-functioning
                                                                    ecosystems; to native ecosystem and cultural devastation
                                                                    following colonization; to, most recently, a haphazard and
                                                                    as yet unfinished restoration of environmental health and
                                                                    function. In this presentation and discussion we explore
                                                                    the past, present, and future of land management in Ver-
                                                                    mont and the region, and shine a spotlight on the special
                                                                    role of public lands – like those of our beloved Worcester
                                                                    Range or the Green Mountain National Forest – in helping
                                                                    to mitigate climate change, support biodiversity, protect
                                                                    our communities from natural disasters, and strengthen
                                                                    human-land relationships.”
                                                                       Zack Porter is a Montpelier resident and Executive
                                                                    Director of Standing Trees. You can find out more about
                                                                    his organization at their website: www.standingtrees.org

     Although there are many phone apps to help identify
flowers, I recommend using iNaturalist (www.iNaturalist.
org). Once you download the app, you can explore what
others have observed in a particular area or make your
own observations by uploading a photograph of the plant.
If you think you know what the specimen is, you can
name it, or the app will give you suggestions, including
photographs and descriptions. At a later date, one or more
people may check your observations for accuracy. You
can also join other citizen scientists in a variety of iNatu-
ralist projects which monitor flora and/or fauna, such as
the Long Trail Naturalist Project, the Appalachian Trail
Corridor, and the Northeast Alpine Flower Watch.

Trail Tales                                         What To Do During Mud Season
                      by Morgan Irons                                                    by Steve Bailey

     I was several days into a southbound 100-Mile-Wil-
derness hike last August when I met “Augie” on the Jo-
Mary Road. He was one of many northbound Appalachian
Trail hikers within days of their final destination – Mt.
Katahdin. The Jo-Mary is a remote gravel road where AT
hikers often rendezvous with resupply packages driven in
by hostel or campground staff from Monson, Maine.
     “Peach”, a young, local teacher, was set up at this AT
road crossing with an open hatchback full of trail magic
– coffee, muffins, doughnuts, fresh raspberries and straw-
berries. She was giving back, she said, because of all the
magic she’d experienced during her own AT through hike
ten years earlier. The group around her car gradually grew
as people arrived in time for their scheduled food drop.
     Among them was Augie, a typically scruffy looking
man in his sixties who sat on a rock, head bowed, apart
from the group chatter. I was drawn to him as a fellow
senior hiker, and pressed him for his story.
     Augie had begun his section hike of the Appalachian
Trail when he was 22. Despite a nine-year gap early in his
marriage, he had kept in hiking shape, kept his AT dream
alive, and gradually covered miles of the trail during the
summers his family and work schedule permitted. He was
sometimes joined on the AT by his young son Silas.
     When I asked Augie how it felt to be reaching the
goal he had set for himself some forty years ago, tears
filled his eyes. “I’m just realizing that it’s so close,” he
said, shaking his head. He had reserved a cabin in Bax-
ter State Park, he told me, where his wife and son would
be waiting to celebrate with him. They would spend the
