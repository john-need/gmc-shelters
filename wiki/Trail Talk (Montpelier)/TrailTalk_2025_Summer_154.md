Naturalist’s Corner
                                      What’s the Difference Between a Bog and a Fen?
                                                        by Phyllis Rubenstein

       Bogs and fens are two delightful types of wetlands.
Their land forms were created over 10,000 years ago
when the glaciers receded and left behind bedrock basins,
which filled with water. They are both always wet and
contain plants that can survive in or near water.
       Although bogs and fens have similarities, they
receive water from different sources. Bogs receive little to
no groundwater, relying exclusively on precipitation from
rain or snow. Since bogs do not receive groundwater, they
are acidic and low in minerals and nutrients.
       Fens, which are classified as poor, intermediate, or
rich, receive rainwater runoff and groundwater from sur-
rounding woods. Since they receive water rich in minerals
and nutrients, they range from acidic to alkaline and host
different vegetation and animals than bogs.
       Dwarf shrub bogs contain plants such as sphagnum
mosses, sedges, cotton grass, pitcher plants and sundews;
heath shrubs such as Labrador tea, bog laurel, sheep lau-
rel, and leatherleaf; and stunted tamarack trees and black
spruce growing on moss hummocks.
       Fens have northern white cedar trees rather than
an abundance of black spruce, as well as tamarack. Poor
fens, which are acidic, contain vegetation similar to bogs,
as well as site specific plants such as bog bean.
       Intermediate fens are slightly acidic to neutral, may
be adjacent to open water, and contain shrubs such as
