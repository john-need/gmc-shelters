Mud season Reminder
The GMC main club would like to remind us to avoid the wet
and muddy trails that plague hikers, bikers and runners in
spring, in order to preserve the trails and prevent trail erosion.
These conditions can last from early April to early June
(https://www.greenmountainclub.org/hiking/mud-season/).

Some southern Vermont area hikes that are recommended for
mud season include:
Long Trail to Spruce Peak, Winhall: This 2.4-mile section of the LT gradually winds from Route 11/30 to the
summit of Spruce Peak where a view of the Taconic Range is found. Continue south on the LT for another 0.4
mile and have lunch at Spruce Peak Shelter. Return the way you came.
Homer Stone Brook Trail, South Wallingford: For those working on their side-to-side trails, the Homer Stone
Brook Trail is a good mud season choice. The 2.3-mile trail gradually approaches the scenic Little Rock Pond. From
here you can then circumnavigate the pond and even have lunch at the shelter. Please be aware the stream crossing
can be difficult during high-water events or conditions.
Prospect Rock, Manchester: The trail steadily ascends Old Rootville Road 1.8 miles to a rocky outlook. From
there, hikers are rewarded with views of Manchester and Mt. Equinox.
Black Mountain, Dummerston: Owned by The Nature Conservancy, this 1,280’ mountain is a granite pluton, or
in other words, a volcano that never erupted. Along the 1.8-mile trail, hikers can experience plant communities that
are rare to Vermont including mountain laurel that blooms each June.

Have a lovely spring. We look forward to seeing you on the trails!

Barb Raskin, Co-President, Bennington Section


Nature Notes: Community Science
By Terri Armata

Ah, spring and summer! Warm weather, birds, critters, flowers and bugs galore.
This is a wonderful time to become a Community Science volunteer. It’s easy and fun and contributes to
environmental databases. eBird, eButterfly and iNaturalist are just 3 websites where one can enter photos, sightings
and checklists of what is seen while walking in fields, forests, roads or simply enjoying your home yards.

An easy way to begin is to go to the Vermont Center For Ecostudies website: https://vtecostudies.org/, scroll
down to Get Involved, click on it and choose one or more of the projects shown. All have clear instructions about
signing in and reporting your findings. Don’t know the type of butterfly, bug, plant you’ve taken a photo of ? No
problem. iNaturalist and eButterfly have a metadata base that will help with identification and there are fellow users
who will identify your entry.

13 Rockwood Street Unearths a Garden
by Berta Winiker

Blood, sweat and tears
There was a positively virgin backyard awaiting root crops and displays of luscious vegetables. There was a
suspicion that a large machine would be needed so a rototiller and two strapping men were set to task. The men
were ultra protective of their tools and after a few token swipes, the job was declared “fini.” The short woman was
then left to her own devices which included a meager cache of tools, much determination, vision and time. The
street was not casually named “Rockwood Street.” It was deep into allergy season and hence, the tears. Repetitive
use of the tools produced the blood as well as the sweat.

Ripley’s believe it or not
A significant wall of rock edged the start of Rockwood Street. Tall tales were told of neighbors who pointed out
their own rock trophies. What had once been the site of Rockwood Mill, closed in the late 1930’s, had been cleared
to create this neighborhood. Vestiges of the industries that had earlier prospered were littered and scattered
remnants.

Fertility dances were held
The garden continued to yield variations on the boulder theme and the short woman felt much dismay. Was this the
meaning of a “sunken garden?” Numerous bags of topsoil, manure, limestone and compost restored the volume
and seeds were entrusted to the mix. The rains came, and CAME and CAME. The zucchini protested and bit the
dust and the tomatoes took center stage, glistening like rubies.

Grounding in words
The short woman, who was a librarian when she was not gardening, resorted to word games to make the rock
harvesting a more pleasant experience. She pictured herself opening up a Peterson Field Guide and exploring the
vocabulary of rocks. She particularly favored the longer, crunchier and more sophisticated words that she found
such as disilicate, olivine, epidote and molybdate. She thought that wolframite sounded very imposing and she
hoped that that wasn’t the boulder gracing the front lawn. How did one find a rock specialist in the community
with whom to consult in this pre-Front Porch Forum age of 2003? While the asparagus bed has come and gone
over these 22 years, wonder of wonders to report. The ground is still yielding boulders that seem to be in
competition for the grand prize.

Bioluminescence
By Ann Martin

It was an other-worldly experience, paddling before moonrise in a clear-bottom kayak during a warm, calm, inky-
black night on the brightest bioluminescent bay in the world. Welcome to Vieques, a small island off the coast of
Puerto Rico. Along with the bio-bay, Vieques boasts warm, sunny weather and gorgeous, uncrowded beaches.

We paid our fee and boarded the small, old school bus with
constellations and stars painted on the ceiling, and it proceeded
down to a rutted, potholed dirt road, the bus rocking and bouncing
as it crawled along. Arriving at the water’s edge each of the 18
adventurers were given a paddle and life vest, listened to safety
instructions, and then two by two, removed our shoes and got into
our tandem kayaks. Our leader had a soft orange light on the back
of his kayak and like little ducklings we followed him out into the
dark bay.

With amazement and wonder we watched as each time we dipped
our paddles into the water, there was a swirl of brilliant blue light. “Look at the bottom of the kayak” I called to
Billy, it was as though there were a stream of lights shining up through the kayak as we moved through the water.

Across the bay we gathered our kayaks around our guide as he taught us about bioluminescence. Bioluminescent
bays or "bio bays" are bodies of water where microscopic organisms called dinoflagellates grow in quantities large
enough to produce a "glow-in-the-dark" effect called bioluminescence. Dinoflagellates are small, single-celled
organisms that are half animal half plant, and it is believed by scientists that their glow is a defense against
predators. When you paddle or splash the water, these organisms shine in a neon blue-green color, making the
water look as if it was lit by billions of glowing stars.

The configuration of this bay which has a narrow mouth makes for a large concentration of the dinoflagellates.
The lack of light pollution surrounding the Natural Reserve of Mosquito Bay and the mangrove swamps that feed
the plankton make this natural spectacle a true wonder. The bay is protected so that you can only go out on the
water with a licensed guide, no swimming is allowed, the bay is only open certain hours, and is totally closed at least
3 nights a month.

Now a quick note about the name of the bay - Mosquito Bay. No, it doesn’t mean it’s infested with mosquitoes.
Mosquito Bay was named after Puerto Rican pirate Roberto Cofresi’s ship, El Mosquito as it was a small, fast ship.

                                                   “Dip your hands in the water” suggested our guide and we were
                                                   mesmerized by the swirls and sparkles that emanated from our
                                                   hands and from the water. What fun! The clouds broke apart
                                                   enough for the guide to shine his powerful laser up to the sky to
                                                   point out various constellations.

                                                   All too soon we paddled back across the bay still marveling at the
                                                   wondrous spectacle. Then we sadly relinquished our paddles, but
                                                   with smiles and exclamations of remembered excitement boarded
                                                   the bus for the bumpy ride back to town. If you get a chance -
                                                   go! You won’t regret it.

Volunteer with GMC!
There are many ways to volunteer with our GMC Section in Bennington, or with the main club.

The Bennington Section is always happy to welcome new volunteers to our trail work days which we hold in the
spring and the fall. The first work day of the year will be April 6 (find details in Meetup or elsewhere in the
newsletter). No prior experience is needed. We provide the tools, and will teach you the trail maintenance skills you
need to help out (including painting blazes, cutting back brush, removing blow downs and clearing water bars). You
provide sturdy work boots, a good pair of work gloves, water, lunch and a snack.

An opportunity that provides a more in-depth learning experience in trail maintenance is the Long Trail Volunteer
Patrol. This program is administered through the Green Mountain Club headquarters, and provides volunteers with
a weeklong opportunity in June and July to maintain and build trails along the LT. For more information, follow
this link to the main club website: https://www.greenmountainclub.org/volunteers/volunteer-long-trail-patrol/

workshops. Do you have a favorite place to hike, paddle or bike? Do you have an outdoor skill you’d like to share

leaders, we plan to hold a Trip Leader Training Workshop later this spring. We’ll share the actual date and

Long Trail Route History
by Martha Stitelman


                                          Although inspired by the mountains, the early LT in southern
                                          Vermont used existing lower roads and railroad routes, gradually
                                          becoming wilder as foot trails could be constructed.
                                          This map shows the southern section initially following logging
                                          routes along Woodford Hollow and Somerset (Blue).
                                          By the 1930’s the path led up to Glastenbury Mountain and along
                                          its ridge, but still required a valley walk on Harbour Road.(Red)
                                          It wasn’t until the 1970’s, with land acquired by the Green
                                          Mountain National Forest, that the Trail could take on its present
                                          form.
                                          The construction of the Harmon Hill steps and MacArthur
                                          Bridge now allow hikers to bypass settled areas of Bennington and
                                          Woodford. (Purple).
                                          What is now the West Ridge Trail was considered as an LT option,
                                          but was designated a side trail when today’s LT route was
                                          confirmed.

Look Out for Liverworts
by Nancy Felix

We all enjoy seeing vascular plants, which include trees, shrubs and herbs, on our hikes. However, liverworts are
another fascinating group of plants that few notice. These plants are small as they do not have a vascular system to
take up water and nutrients but must absorb them through their leaves. You can see clumps of them on rocks,
trees, or on the ground. To really see their structure it helps to have a hand lens or phone camera for magnification.




                                   Wort means small plant, and liver refers to its lobes which were thought to look
                                   like a liver. According to the Doctrine of Signatures, in ancient times these
                                   plants were thought to treat diseases of the liver since they resembled the liver.
                                   Liverworts were one of the earliest groups of plants to move out of water onto
                                   the land. They are most common in wet, tropical areas, but have adapted to live
                                   almost anywhere, even the desert.




Liverworts are often only one cell thick to allow them to absorb the water and nutrients that they need. They do
not have roots but instead have rhizoids which only serve to hold them to the surface. Liverworts are often the
first plants to colonize bare soil or rock. They stabilize the soil and hold in moisture which allows seeds of vascular
plants to germinate and grow. They also slowly break down rocks into soil that other plants can use.
 Liverworts reproduce by spores rather than seeds. The plant that we see is a gametophyte which has only 1 set of
chromosomes. This is different from vascular plants and animals which have 2 sets of chromosomes (or sometimes
more) in the cells of the individuals that we see. Liverworts have a second sporophyte phase which has 2 sets of
chromosomes after fusion of an egg and sperm. In this stage, capsules are formed which may be simple (as shown
here) or in complex receptacles (as shown for Marchantia). Capsules have 4 valves that open releasing spores and
elaters. Elaters are cells with spiral thickenings that respond to moisture and help disperse the spores. Cells in the
capsule divide to become spores with 1 set of chromosomes, and these spores form the gametophyte.

Liverworts can also reproduce asexually with gemmae. Gemmae are pieces of
the liverwort which can grow into a new plant. They can be found on the tips
of leaves or stems, or on the edges of the thallus. Gemmae in Marchantia are
found in splash cups which release the gemmae when rain splashes in.




There are 2 main types of liverworts: thalloid and leafy. The thalloid liverworts have a flattened ribbon-like
structure called a thallus. In contrast, leafy liverworts have leaves on 2 sides of a stem, and sometimes a third set
of smaller leaves on the underside. They differ from mosses which have leaves all around the stem.


                                        Conocephalum salebrosum, Snakewort: Locally, snakewort is our largest
                                        thalloid liverwort. It is known as snakewort since it has reticulations which
                                        resemble snake skin. Within each reticulated area is a pore which can be
                                        easily seen without a hand lens. You will find snakewort on streambanks,
                                        rock walls and in swamps. It has an odor of turpentine. The female
                                        sporophytes are cone-shaped and the males are bumps on the thallus.




Marchantia polymorpha, Common Liverwort: Marchantia, also known as
umbrella liverwort, is a thalloid liverwort with pores and reticulations less
obvious than snakewort. It is found on disturbed soil, and is frequently a
weed in greenhouses. Males and females are on separate plants, and both
have umbrella-shaped receptacles. The females look like ribs of the
umbrella (seen here), and the males look like full flattened umbrellas.
Marchantia also reproduces by gemmae cups as shown previously.

lobes and multiple cilia on each lobe giving
it a feathery appearance when you look
closely. It grows flat over the surface of old
logs, rocks and trees. The leaves range in
color from yellow-green to reddish.




                                                   Lophocolea heterophylla, Various-
                                                   leaved Crestwort: This leafy liverwort
                                                   has leaves that vary from rounded to
                                                   having 2 teeth on the end. The leaves
                                                   are translucent green and the plants are
                                                   found spreading across old logs. They
                                                   have a third row of smaller leaves on
                                                   the underside which have long narrow
                                                   teeth.

Keep a lookout for liverworts on your hikes this summer. You can even find them on your winter hikes as many
remain green when many of the other plants turn brown and die back. This is only a small sample of the common
liverworts in our area. You’ll be amazed at what you find if you look closely. If you are interested in naming the
various species, iNaturalist or Seek is a good place to start. Be sure to take close-up photos of the top and bottom
of the liverwort. Happy hunting!
