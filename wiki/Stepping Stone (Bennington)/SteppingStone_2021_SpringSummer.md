Due to the fantastic efforts of a number of people, but particularly Silvia Cassano and Jonah Spivak,
Bennington has received the designation of Appalachian Trail Community. There will be an official
virtual event celebrating this, most likely on June 3, in the early evening. Please take note of the date,

on the AT. It will encourage the thru hikers to visit in Bennington during their hikes.

Bennington is the third named AT Community along the AT in Vermont. The other two are
Norwich and Manchester. South of us, North Adams and Great Barrington are also so designated.
Of note, there is a group working in Bennington called the Bennington Outdoor Recreation
Collaborative. This group has many of the same members as the group that worked towards the AT
Community designation. Their goal is for improved signage in various forms around town to direct
people to the recreation options that are available. Naturally, the Green Mountain Club is one of
those.

We will have links on the Town of Bennington website that direct people to our section's website

soon. As the year progresses, with luck and vaccinations, the pandemic will recede enough for us to
resume offering outings. We hope to see many of you out on the trails with us.

                                          Nature Notes: The Shagbark Hickory Tree
                                          By Terri Armata
                                          One of the more distinctive and useful trees of our eastern
                                          forests is the Shagbark Hickory, (Carya Ovata). This is a
                                          slow growing, slender tree usually reaching 60-80 feet in
                                          height and living for about 200 years. It is easily identified,
                                          having long peeling strips of gray bark. A member of the
                                          walnut family, it produces nuts that animals and humans
                                          prize.

                                          The nuts can be eaten raw, (in small amounts), the flavor is
                                          buttery and sweet. Toasting them in an oven for 10 minutes
                                          at 200 F improves the taste.

                                          The wood is known for its strength and durability. The
                                          inner bark can be processed to make a yellow dye. The
                                          outer bark is used to make Shagbark Hickory Syrup. The
                                          recipe for doing this is available online. Briefly one picks up
                                          or breaks off bark, careful not to take firmly attached
pieces as that might damage the tree, washes it, (do not use soap), bakes the bark, boils and strains it,
then adds sugar to the resulting liquid and simmers it down. The taste: Complex, woodsy and smoky.
Use like Maple Syrup.

Cross Country Skiing
Susan Lambert proposed and facilitated many of the
cross-country ski trips of this winter and has written
the following report, with description of the route, of
one of the outstanding excursions:

Winter 20-21 may have been bad for many
things, but not for skiing! Some years lack of
snow has been a problem, but this year lack
of parking has been the main challenge. With
carpooling not being an option, ski trips have
often been planned around available places to
leave our cars. But, on February 22, it all
came together for a wonderful day of deep,
fluffy snow and plenty of sunshine as eight                                Rake Branch trip 2019
of us met on Route 9 where the Red Mill
Road begins. The group was made up of Reed, Kathy, Tim,
Lorna, Jill, Marty, Terri, and me.
                                                  Parking on the highway, we skied north on the snowmobile
                                                  road until we came to the intersection with Corridor 7,
                                                  then headed off into the woods for some bushwhacking in
                                                  quest of the Rake Branch River. What a fun time we had
                                                  going mostly downhill in a northeasterly direction for
                                                  about a mile! When we neared the river, we headed a little
                                                  south until we found easy access to a large beaver meadow
                                                  where we took a snack break and applied maxi-glide due to
                                                  suddenly sticky snow.
                                          Crossing the brook, we continued south to make our way
                                          to the top of the meadow. We found an old road running
                                          along the east side of the river and took it as far as the
                                          next big meadow. The brook crossing here was a little
                                          more challenging. Staying close to the edge, we headed
                                          upstream. Reed, Tim and Lorna decided to ski right up
                                          the river, but the rest of us ascended the bank into the
                                          woods on the west side. Reed left the river to climb up the
                                          east bank. We met up again at the next pond. Rounding
                                          the bend, we sighted the bridge on Corridor 7. We
climbed up to the snowmobile road and took it back to where we had left the cars. The snow
conditions made for a really fun ride down the big hill that is usually too scary to “let it rip.” The
total trip was 5.55 miles and took about 3.5 hours.

Peak Finder App
by Ann Martin

OK, you’re hiking with friends and get to an overlook and the conversation starts… “What is that
mountain over there and what about that one in the distance?” “Is that Mount Snow?” “No, Snow is
more that way, maybe it’s Haystack” “I think it must be…” “No, I think it’s…” And you all walk away not
really knowing what you were looking at. Does
this sound familiar?

Your queries are solved with a neat little app
named “Peak Finder”. Just hold your phone
up and point it in the direction of the
mountains you want to identify and you will see
a panoramic drawing with peaks labeled. How
cool is that? (View, at right side, is what can be
seen from the top of Mount Anthony.)




Now, you can click the ‘Camera Mode’ button
and overlay the image of your camera with the
panoramic drawing. (View, at the right side is
from Woodford area north of Route 9.)




Other cool features:
* Use the ‘Snapshot’ button to take a photo of the
  panoramic view,
* Edit the image
* Export the image for saving or sharing
* Touch the name of the mountain and see more
  information such as elevation, location, and more


You can also ‘telescope in’ to find less prominent peaks,
adjust the compass setting, see the visibility range, ‘fly up’ to the mountain and see the views from the
top, or see a list of all the mountains visible from your current location.

While out in the wilderness, if you are confused about where you are, by knowing the name of the
mountain seen you could orient yourself to the direction you want to walk.

WOW! All this for $4.99. It’s the best bargain of the day.

Forest Pests: Vermont Invasive Species and How You Can Help
by Ann Martin

While you’re out skiing, snowshoeing or hiking in the beautiful forests of Vermont, you might be
surprised to learn that some trees are threatened by invasive forest pests.

An invasive species is an introduced organism that negatively alters its new environment. These are
primarily spread by humans, either purposefully or by accident. Examples would be a plant that is
introduced for ornamental reasons then spreads and overtakes native plants, or plants/animals that
hitchhike aboard a ship, come ashore and quickly establish their new homes. With increased movement
of people and materials the rate of invasive species introduction has grown.

While there are quite a few invasive species in our forests, this article will focus on the emerald ash borer,
hemlock wooly adelgid, and Asian longhorned beetle. See the website vtinvasives.org for much more
information and photos about these and other pests.

Emerald Ash Borer

This beetle, native to Asia, attacks all species of ash trees. The adult lays eggs on the tree bark and, when
the eggs hatch, the larvae tunnel into the inner bark and outer wood. This then kills the tree by
interrupting the flow of food and water.

Look for bark splitting, S-shaped tunnels behind outer
bark, D-shaped exit holes, woodpecker flecking, and dead
top branches of ash trees.

Hemlock Woolly Aldegid

Hemlock trees provide food, shelter and shade to
wildlife across our region and keep our trout
streams cool. The Hemlock Woolly Aldegid is a
small and aphid-like insect. It has “wool” attached
to the twig, not attached to the needles. This
insect feeds on young twigs causing needles to dry
out and drop prematurely. Trees may die in 4 to 6
years.

Look for white, cottony balls at the base of the
tree needles.

Asian Longhorned Beetle

The larvae of this insect tunnel deep into the bark of many hardwood species including the maples for
which Vermont is famous. Although not yet present in Vermont, this beetle has the potential to destroy
our landscapes and maple sugar industry.

Look for perfectly round dime-sized exit holes at least a half inch deep. Egg-laying sites may ooze sap or
be healed over knot-like. There will be sawdust or frass (insect excrement) in branch crotches or at the
tree base.




               REPORT IF FOUND

               The sooner new infestations are found, the better the chances of slowing its spread. If
               you think you have found one of these insects or signs of infestation, take photos, then go
               to the Vermont Invasives website at https://vtinvasives.org/get-involved/report/
               reporting-an-invasive-insect and follow the directions for reporting.

The Old and the New
by Martha Stitelman

Martha has been archivist/custodian of past issues of The Stepping Stone, continuing the organizing of our history in
newsletters that Chris Berks initiated when he produced the Bennington GMC newsletter.

This home-bound year has been a good time to sort and neaten the Bennington Section archives: a box
of old meeting minutes, correspondence, membership lists, and a full collection of Stepping Stones from
1970 to present (beautifully corralled by Chris Berks). Some things seem remarkably similar through the
last 50 years - the trail work - the socials and potlucks - the building, repair and adjustments to shelters -
the search for new members - the interactions (and sometimes conflicts) with towns, neighbors, Forest
Service, VT state. Section members stay active for decades, gradually fade from the records, and are
replaced. Through-hiker numbers have been up and down.

The destinations of Bennington Section day hikes have been particularly consistent through the years:
mostly maintenance and visiting our local trails, with forays into Massachusetts and nearby Vermont.
 Sometimes it risks boredom to revisit the same places so often, but we also get the warmth and welcome
of seeing old friends. Sometimes it’s sad to see the old friends in trouble: the Dunville Hollow Road
washed out, bridges gone, the beeches disappearing, views fading. It’s nice to see most of the forest
regrowing, and new shelters and privies. One is never certain what to expect, and so we return with
                                                       curiosity.

                                                                 This year’s surprise for me was a tale of two
                                                                 sawdust piles from the old logging days. The near
                                                                 one is on Forest Road 273 between City Stream
                                                                 Rd and Sucker Pond. I have heard it was once
                                                                 house-high, but when I met it, it was shrunken
                                                                 and littered - used for target practice on old
                                                                 appliances, and eventually overgrown with weeds.
                                                                 Last year it became a large new parking lot: some
                                                                 of the adjacent old roads have been upgraded for
                                                                 ATV use, and a patch of woods cleared for
                                                                 successional habitat (for migratory birds)




 and a view. The other is at the old Grout Job lumber
mill site where the LT crosses the Stratton-Arlington
Road now. That sawmill closed 100 years ago; the pile
must have been huge and I wish I could find a

from the LT when leaves are down, just south of the

visitors at all, but it’s worth a visit if you’re in the area.

Trail Maintenance
Matt Vezina, our Trails and Shelters Co-ordinator, plans on speaking about trails and shelters at our
annual meeting. He continues to monitor the snow situation on the trails before scheduling specific dates
for spring trail work but is considering a mid-April start. He is looking at the five weekends from 4/17 to
5/15 with the goal of finishing maintenance on the LT just before the anticipated increase in trail users
for Memorial Day weekend. During that period, the GMC asks hikers to stay off the trail for mud season
except for trail work, and, by then, enough snow should be gone that we can maintain the water bars. He
plans to use the Meetup group for sharing the trail work days once official dates are scheduled and to give
at least two weeks notice prior to any scheduled events.

On March 21, Matt will also cover some of the ways our members can help keep the trail in good shape
throughout the year, beyond the scope of our scheduled maintenance trips. With the recent increase in
trail usage, he encourages everyone to be proactive with scattering new fire rings at the dispersed sites,
and maintaining the brush barrier at one of the undesirable sites north of Little Pond overlook so that it
can successfully revegetate.
