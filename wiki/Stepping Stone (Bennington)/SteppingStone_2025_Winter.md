Nature Notes: The Eastern Larch
by Terri Armata

The Eastern Larch (Larix laricina) is a conifer tree that
sheds all its needles every year in late fall. Before
falling, the needles turn a warm yellow. It is also
known as the Tamarack and Hackmatack. Found as far
north as Siberia, it is very cold resistant. It prefers
moist wet soils, so is often found around wetlands.
The wood is used for snowshoes, poles, posts and
pulpwood. The seeds are enjoyed by Red squirrels, the
inner bark by porcupines, seedlings by Snowshoe
hares. Some medicinal uses of the inner bark are as
poultices for cuts and infected wounds, for treating
frostbite, boils and hemorrhoids.

Safe and Fun GMC Trips
by Martha Stitelman

Don’t these people look happy, starting out on their hike! But are they ready?

Are they properly dressed? Some of them don’t have hats that cover their ears. Do they have any extra clothes?
Perhaps they think they’re warmly dressed, but what if they get soaked in sweat under those heavy layers? Are they
carrying any food and water? It’s a cloudy day - do they have a compass to navigate? Do they have sunscreen?
(can’t count on those clouds). There’s no obvious trail, do they have a map? Will they wish they had poles? There
are 18 of them - will they be able to go at the same pace and stay together? How will they contact help if there’s an
emergency?

 We hope all our trip participants return looking as happy as this group from a century ago! GMC trips are meant
to be both fun and safe. The Bennington Section plans a Trip Leaders’ Workshop later this winter to guide good
trip planning.

Praise Song
by Berta Winiker

Praise the vestiges of color as we approach stick season, what remains accentuated amidst subtlety

Praise the choice of chores and play, to rake, ready the snow tires, or just head out for a spin on the bike

Praise this confusion of seasons, a little Indian summer mixed with roaring winds and a side of snow glaze on the
road

Praise the swing in temperature, scraping the windshield one day and donning shorts the next

Praise the iris leaves transitioning to golden and holding clippers at bay for another day

Praise the hangers-on, the lingering leeks, tenacious sage, seed heads atop woody stems

Praise the bushel baskets, heaps of buttercup, delicata, turban, macoun, honeycrisp and jonagold

Praise the aroma of the kitchen, comfort food which sticks to the ribs, the simmer of slower foods

Praise the furnace, having had a long rest and kicking on without hesitation

Praise fleece and wool blankets, the pile of books, the projects to tackle

Praise the mountains of snow which will work muscles and the reward of hot cocoa, mulled cider

Praise that which provides sustenance - tradition, memory, goodwill and each and every season we are given

Invitation: As we watch the season turn from autumn into full winter, we might look around and see what simple
things we are still able to praise. What can you praise right now that seems plain or even depressing? As you write,
you might repeat the word “Praise” over and over working yourself into the space of gratitude again.

Trail Food: Black Bean Brownies
By Margie March

Black Bean Brownies are a favorite GMC snack for back country skiing, especially for birthday skis. They were first
shared on a birthday ski in the middle of a frozen swamp south of "Dewey Road" in the Aiken Wilderness in 2016.

BLACK BEAN BROWNIES - a winning recipe from Meg Boyles, 2016 Old Farmer's Almanac

2 cups black beans, drained and rinsed (1 can) but I use beans that I have cooked.
3 eggs
3/4 cup sugar
1/4 cup unsweetened cocoa powder
1/2 tsp. baking powder
a pinch of salt
3 Tbs. vegetable oil (OR 1 1/2 Tbs. of each - olive oil and butter)
1/2 cup dark chocolate chips
1/2 cup chopped walnuts

Preheat oven to 350 degrees and grease an 8"x8" baking pan.
Put the first 7 ingredients in a food processor and blend until smooth.
Transfer to a bowl and stir in the chocolate chips and walnuts.
Pour into prepared pan and bake for 35 minutes, or until a toothpick inserted into the center comes out clean.

Margaret Boyles lived in a wood-heated house in central New Hampshire, where she grew vegetables and kept
chickens.

THE SECRET TO ENJOYING YOURSELF OUTSIDE IN WINTER:
IT’S ALL ABOUT THE CLOTHES (and gear)!
by Barb Raskin

Having fun outside in the winter time can seem like an inherent contradiction. Luckily, we live in an age where
outdoor clothing and gear have evolved so rapidly, that it makes it much easier to have fun in winter weather
without succumbing to the cold and damp. Here are some of the most important things to think about when
you’re getting yourself out there:

LAYERS ARE THE KEY TO DRESSING

• We lose heat in a number of ways outdoors in the cold, and dressing for outdoor winter activities is meant to
  address that. Layering allows you to shed clothing when you’re too warm (to avoid sweating which cools you off)
  or putting clothes back on to avoid getting chilled. The layers also serve to trap warm air between them to help
  keep us comfortable.

• Start with wicking, quick drying base layers, both top and bottom (think “long johns”). No cotton allowed here!
  Pick base layers made with the newer, less itchy marino wool, or any synthetics that can move sweat away from
  your skin, which will keep you dry and warm. Base layers come in lightweight, midweight and heavyweight
  depending on your activity level, the
  temperature outside, and your cold
  tolerance (or for many of us, cold
  intolerance).

• Next, pick an insulating mid layer
  made of polyester fleece or wool
  (think “sweater”)

• Your insulating layer is next, and
  should be synthetic polyester or
  down (think “puffy”). It’s nice to
  have a hood on your puffy in case
  you need extra head warmth.

• Last is your hooded shell layer (think
  “rain jacket”) that will keep wind
  from blowing away all that heat
  you’re trying to hang onto, plus it          Photo from: https://www.paddypallin.com.au/blog/introduction-to-layering/
  will keep damp snow or sleet from
  getting your layers wet. Aim to pick a
  shell that’s both waterproof and breathable, which will help you avoid building up moisture under the shell. Pit
  zips are a useful feature for dumping excess heat while you’re trying to stay warm and dry.

• WAIT! Don’t forget the pants! And remember, NO JEANS. You can experiment with a base layer bottom under
  a separate water-resistant shell pant or hiking pant (usually nylon or polyester) for milder winter days; or a base
  layer bottom under insulated, waterproof/resistant snow pants on colder days. Some people like to wear a base
  layer bottom under fleece pants or tights, but you should then consider carrying waterproof/water resistant shell
  pants in your pack to layer on top of those if wind or snow are starting to come through your fleece bottoms.

Be sure to layer your head, hands and feet too!

• You can lose a significant amount of heat from an uncovered head. Wear a wool, acrylic or fleece hat or beanie.
  If you become chilled, the hood on your shell or insulating layer cinched down over your hat will help trap more
  heat.

• You can pick gloves or mittens that have a water resistant shell bonded to an insulating layer (think “ski mittens
  or gloves”), or pick an insulating liner glove or mitten to start with and slide a water resistant shell mitten over
  that. It all depends on what feels more comfortable to you. Keep in mind that wool or fleece gloves/mittens on
  their own will often not keep your hands warm if they become wet, the wind is blowing or it’s very cold. You can
                   add chemical hand warmers to mittens or gloves if needed.

                 •Your feet will thank you if you wear a substantial pair of wool socks. Some people also like to
                 wear thin liner socks under their wool socks for extra warmth. An ankle high, waterproof hiking
                 boot will keep your feet happier in the cold and snow than a trail shoe or runners. Many people
                 use their summer hiking boots year round as long as the waterproofing is functioning and they
                 can keep their feet warm with wool socks. In colder weather, you might prefer to choose an
                 insulated hiking boot specifically for winter time. Waterproof gaiters can also be important to
                 help keep snow out of your boots which will also help keep your feet dry.

SOME USEFUL GEAR IDEAS

• Be sure to bring a backpack on your winter outings to store your gear and extra layers, and give you some place
  to put the layers you will be taking off as you warm up. Aim for a 20-30 liter size pack on shorter outings, or
  30-40 liter size on all day, backcountry outings.

• Microspikes (check out Kahtoolas) are designed to fit over the bottom of
  your boots to help keep you from slipping on the ice. They’re fairly light
  weight and easy to carry in your pack to have when needed.

• Snowshoes are fun to try in deeper snow. You can actually borrow a pair or
  two from the Bennington Free Library if you have a card there, or rent
  them from the Mountain Goat in Manchester if you want to try them out.

• Hiking poles with baskets will help keep you stable, and the baskets will
  keep your poles from sinking down into the snow.

• Wrap a wide mouth water bottle in a wool sock or other insulated sleeve, and keep it inside the middle of your
  pack to keep your water from freezing. A water reservoir with a tube will often freeze in the cold and is not

• You can look for clothes and gear on line or in stores at places like REI and Eastern Mountain Sports. They

50 Years of GMC Winter Memories
By Harda Bradford

It was in 1975/76 when Stuart and I with our 2 small children
experienced our first winter season in Bennington. The old Norwegian
wooden skis with those flimsy low ankle ‘3 prong boots’ and bamboo
poles that we had purchased in Boston in the late 60’s, as well as a
‘Klepper’ folding boat, were a very important part of our meager
belongings. We both had always loved the outdoors and joining the
Green Mountain Club here in Bennington therefore became a perfect
fit for us at ages 41 and 34.

Now that 50 years have passed, there is indeed a joyous reason to
celebrate this particular milestone as we still continue to enjoy short
hikes, XC ski outings, and rail-trail outings with our small folding e-
                                                                                     Stuart and Harda 2000
bikes.

                                                       In 1975/76 Charlie Hines was our GMC president and Betsy
                                                       Paulson and Erika Olmsted were our public relations people.
                                                       They both made sure to call us when changes in the
                                                       scheduling of trips were made and they also made the
                                                       necessary preparations for our two yearly socials. At one of
                                                       our GMC spring social potlucks in April they made placemats
                                                       with photos of those tiny and large tracks in the snow which
                                                       we had encountered during our many back-country ski trips
                                                       and which we were eager to identify on the place mats. The
                                                       wooden skis still clutter our garage rafters to this day!

                                                      And, oh yes, I once had lost that small metal binding clip
                                                      while we were gathering on a cold winter morning in
                      1980                            Woodford, but Charlie never arrived unprepared and he
                                                      quickly was able to help out. If he asked me whether I had
brought a 2nd pair of socks in case of an accident, I would have lied out of fear even if I hadn’t brought them.
"Thank you, Charlie! You always were most concerned about our safety, especially in the wilderness!"

I also remember fondly our yearly, challenging, all day XC
Ski trip across the Aiken Wilderness with Charlie and his
wife Barbara and later with Reed Goossen. When we finally
rested for our lunch stop at one of the five beaver ponds I

On another challenging trip along the Deerfield River and Somerset Reservoir I still hear Tom Steffen's voice,
blaming his patient wife Nancy Jeanne for each of his falls, of course, never to be taken seriously, knowing Tom!

And, Ron Trout, known for his most stylish exterior clothing and ski equipment, once led us on a "Goose-Hunt" in
the Hogback Mountain. area. An unexpected slowly moving snowstorm started to obstruct our return trip. I
became particularly concerned as I was to leave for Europe the next morning. So, I returned immediately and
therefore, I left the group for which I understandably later got scolded.

The Adirondack Mountains in nearby New York State were less known to us Vermonters especially in the winter.
However, the idea to climb “White Face Mountain” on skis seemed to appeal to some of us with an overnight at
the old Saranac Hotel. Lorna Cheriton stands out regarding getting up a very icy last bare peak with a totally iced
over building and low level shrubs. When all of us knew to leave our skis behind, she was the only one to make it
to the top on skis! It was amazing, how agile and persistent she was.!

And last, but not least, every winter we spent one long weekend in a cabin at Merck Forest with 6-8 of us GMC
members. What fun to ponder life's deepest questions by candlelight way into the night, while keeping the stove
going. Even when there was no sound to be heard outside, our imagination pictured having an encounter with a
bear on the dreaded trip to the privy. Even virtually plugging one's ears when one of us was snoring too loudly was
all part of the fun. The same veggie stew kept us full and healthy year after year and, when the large cooking pot
once slipped off the sled, everybody screamed: "Oh, no there goes Stuart's Stew!” Martha Stitelman kept our
intellectual curiosity going with games while Jenny Meyers was the one to be asked for more water or wood as she
had always been our youngest participant.

Favorite Winter Trails

Barb Raskin: Woodford State Park in the winter is wonderful! It’s so quiet and peaceful, and the view over the
Adams Reservoir is breathtaking. You can ski or snowshoe all the main paved roads through the park when the
snow is deep enough. You can even ski across the Adams Reservoir after a period of prolonged and significant
freezing weather.

And then, there’s the Blue Trail. This is a 2.3
mile hiking trail that completely circles the
park. It’s formally called the Woodford Trail,
but nicknamed the “Blue Trail” after its blue
blazes. The Blue Trail covers lovely stretches
of flat trail through the woods, and stretches
where you’re hiking right next to the
reservoir, but you should also expect the
challenge of going up and down hills, and
crossing streams and sometimes boggy areas
by bridge, puncheon or stepping stone if
you’re going by snowshoe or XC ski. If
hiking in lighter snow cover, I highly
recommend bringing along hiking poles and some Kahtoola microspikes for your boots so you’ll be prepared if
you run into icy conditions.

Robin Tarnas: My favorite winter trail is still being built. It starts right in the heart of Bennington.

Over the last 30 years, Bennington has been making an effort to extend its town trail system. A narrow walkway
near Bennington Elementary School and the old Catamount School was built to allow pedestrian access for kids to
get safely to school. Part of this was upgraded to become our beautiful Riverwalk. A section of rail bed extending
between the old railroad station on Depot St, and Northside Drive was converted to a wider, more useful Rail Trail.

Going out towards Bennington College, a pathway that was carved through the brush along the flood plain by the
Walloomsac River became known as the Ninja Trail. Parts of this have been paved over the past few years. This
summer, a permanent bridge on the Ninja Trail over Furnace Brook was built, and the paving has been extended
even further. These pathways can be strung together to form the core of what we now call “ The Walloomsac
Valley Pathway."

Going south from Main Street, there are several properties
that are owned by the town and the state that can be
connected to extend this pathway more than two miles
further. The town is now looking into improving the trail
through the the park just south of Safford Street, through
the Historic Bennington Cemetery, along the river in the
Headwaters Park off Morgan Street, across the land once
acquired by the state for an abandoned road project, all the
way to the Greenberg Reserve park on Route 7 South and
beyond.

By connecting all of these sections, Bennington is creating
a linear park that is useful for everyone. It can be a
practical and safe way to commute around town. It can be a place to stroll, to bicycle, and to explore the area. It
can be an attractive tourist destination that brings people to southwestern Vermont where they can enjoy
everything that Bennington has to offer. It can be the connection between the town and all the surrounding natural
areas.

                                                       In the winter, it has some special attractions. It is one of the
                                                      few trails that be be bicycled in the winter. It can always be
                                                      walked. Some sections are great for cross country skiing with
                                                      very little elevation change. And on the rare occasions that
                                                      we get a huge snowfall in the valley, this trail can even be
                                                      traversed by snowshoe.

                                                      When thinking about beautiful trails in the area, we should
                                                      remember that Bennington’s own Walloomsac Valley Pathway

I resigned myself to lower altitudes on this summer's vacation, and chose to visit some archeological sites that I’d

Camping in Joshua Tree National Park
By Stuart Capewell




Joshua Tree National Park (JTNP) in Southern California is a wonderful place to visit, and camping there unlocks
so much more of the experience. If all you can manage is a day trip, JTNP is still a “must-see” destination, in my
opinion. From the eponymous Joshua Trees to the Cholla cacti, Ocotillo plants, and other desert greenery; the
round granite boulders weathered into so many shapes by millions of years of rainfall, and the occasional oases
teeming with animal and bird life - JTNP has so much to see, all within the relatively compact 1200 square miles of
the park.




Bennington Section GMC                                                                                           25

<!-- page: 26 -->
Winter 2025-26                                                                                           December

Another wonderful feature of the park is its nighttime skies. Parts of the park are officially designated astronomical
dark sky sites, and there are several parking locations within the park especially provided for late-night stays so you
can stargaze after dark with no problem. Of course having a tent pitched nearby, and knowing bed is just a few
steps away, rather than a long drive to get home, makes stargazing while camping an especially enjoyable way to
spend the evening.

JTNP is a great place to visit any time of year, but camping and comfort do change with the seasons.

Although the thought of hanging out in the desert in the cooler winter sounds great at first, the reality is a little
different. For a start, the desert nighttime temperature drops below freezing very often, especially in the high desert
(the Mojave, in the northern part of the park). Winter also marks the start of the rainy season in the park.
Although obviously as a desert JTNP does not get much rain, much of the meagre amount it gets falls between
December and February.

If you can avoid the rain, the lower daytime temperature does mean you avoid the unpleasant summertime heat, so
it is certainly worth spending time there if you are passing through in the winter, but I would prefer to stay in a
local motel rather than a tent at that time of year.

Summer, of course, is extremely hot. With dangerously high temperatures and very low humidity, it is far too easy
to get dehydrated when walking around JTNP in the summer. The long days and high midday sun also heat the
rocks and sand to painful temperatures, and they remain warm all night, so it is unlikely you will get any relief from
the heat overnight.

The only relief from the heat in the summer is the occasional monsoon, with bursts of heavy rain that cause flash
flooding. Which is an even worse situation to be in than the heat.

The other seasons are far better. Autumn marks the start of cooler nights, a welcome relief from the oppressive
summer - especially for those of us who lived locally to JTNP. The days can still be hot, but late afternoon often
brings a slight breeze, and the earlier sunset allows for stargazing at a more convenient time.

One of the most wonderful things to do on an autumn evening is to just lie in a reclining chair, or on a blanket,
and look at the sky. As the sun sets and the sky darkens, the stars begin to appear one at a time. Altair, Vega, and
Deneb (the bright summer triangle stars) appear first, then more of the fainter stars are uncovered as the sky
darkens. An hour or so after sunset, the darkening skies show so many stars it becomes difficult to pick out familiar
constellations. And as it gets darker still, the Milky Way itself becomes readily visible to the naked eye. For me, the
unfolding of the nighttime vista is something that never gets boring.

That leaves Spring. Spring has the same mild temperatures and reasonable sunset times as Autumn, so everything
I’ve already described applies here too. But spring brings another spectacular aspect to the desert – the spring
ephemeral blooms.

The timing and quality of the spring bloom is very dependent on the rainfall and temperatures experienced over
the winter, and so it is difficult to predict with certainty exactly when it will peak, and how showy that peak will be.
The JTNP website regularly updates predictions of blooming throughout the spring, with different plants coming
into flower at various times between early March and May. With the ideal weather conditions, a rare “superbloom”
can be triggered with a far higher density of flowers blooming simultaneously than an average year will provide.

A great advantage of camping during this time of year is that it’s easy to get up for a morning stroll, when the
flowers are still wet with dew, and the warm afternoon weather hasn’t caused the flowers to close up to conserve
moisture.

One aspect of camping I enjoy is experiencing how different the park looks with changing daylight. From both
photographic and visual aspects, the changing hues bought on by the rising and setting Sun can add even more
beauty to the landscape.

It is worth rising before dawn to see the park illuminated with a subtle blue light while the sun is still below the
horizon. The blue cast changes to color of the pinkish granite rocks, and is the perfect start to the day. The rising
Sun can also produce beautiful reds and yellows in the sky reflected off early-morning clouds which will burn off
as the day warms.

Sunset is just as rewarding, but I find it easier to see the color changes as the park goes from dark to light in the
morning than in the evening as the Sun sets.

After sunset, we get what is known as the Golden hour, where yellows, pinks, and reds seem to shift the color of
the park to a warm palette. The red coloration is enhanced after the sun has set, but is still illuminating the taller
rock formations and nearby mountains. This red glow that lights up the peaks is called Alpenglow, and it can on
occasion give a surreal fiery look to the mountains.

And then, of course, the sky darkens and it’s time for stargazing once again!

The freedom from worrying about the drive home is really what makes camping in Joshua Tree a most relaxing
experience.

JTNP has many campgrounds. Details can be found on their website: https://www.nps.gov/jotr/index.htm

Most of the campgrounds are in the north and northwest parts of the park, with Cottonwoods Spring
campground being the only one in the south. This is my personal favorite, but honestly there is not much
difference between them all. The campgrounds all require advance booking, which makes a spontaneous camping
trip less feasible, sadly.

The campgrounds have limited amenities, but are all accessed via road, so parking next to your tent is never an
issue – no need to hike in with all your gear, unless you really want to!

The towns of Joshua Tree and Twentynine Palms to the north of the park have all the expected shopping and
eating places, so it’s easy to pick up anything you need when you are there. Water is available at the west entrance,
the park HQ (near Oasis of Mara) and at black rock campground, so plan to stock up when you can, especially in
summer!

The towns also have motels and hotels available, should camping not be an option. This is a good idea during the
winter, as having a convenient place to get warm at the end of a chilly day in the park is most welcome.

I find staying in Joshua Tree National Park to be a superbly relaxing way to spend a few days. Another thing that
adds to the relaxation for me, is the almost complete lack of cellphone service within the park. (Being forced to
spend time away from electronics added to the feeling of calm.) I highly recommend it to everyone!
