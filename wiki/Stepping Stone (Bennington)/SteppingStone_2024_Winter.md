    The Lamoille Valley Rail Trail
    by Ann Martin

    My new favorite rail trail is the Lamoille Valley Rail Trail. https://railtrails.vermont.gov For those of you who are
    not familiar with the term “rail trail” these are abandoned railroad lines that have been converted to trails.
                                                                  Sometimes these trails are paved, some dirt, some hard
                                                                  pack stone, sometimes they are open for only bikes and
                                                                  walkers/joggers, some are also available for horseback
                                                                  riding or snow sports. The thing that all rail trails have in
                                                                  common is that they, at one time, followed railroad lines
                                                                  which means that they have no great elevation gains or
                                                                  losses, only a gentle grade.

                                                               The Lamoille Valley Rail Trail, in northern Vermont runs
                                                               93 miles from St. Johnsbury to Swanton and also
                                                               connects to other trail systems in the area. The trail
                                                               surface is made of compacted crushed stone which
                                                               makes for a great ride. It winds through forests, meanders
                                                               alongside the Lamoille River, crossing it multiple times,
                                                               and a rider will ride over many bridges, go through the
    Fisher Covered Bridge, go by historic railroad station buildings and old stone culverts.

    There are multiple parking areas along the trail, some allow
    overnight parking. You could do day trips or tackle the
    entire length. There is a shuttle service that will allow you to
    ride one way and get a ride back. This service also rents
    bikes, both regular and e-bikes. https://
    www.lamoillevalleybiketours.com/

    We only rode two portions of the trail, one day from
    Morrisville west to Cambridge and back, and another day
    from Morrisville east to Hardwick and back. We went in
    October and the weather was cool, the river gorgeous, and
    the trail was beautiful. We hope to do the whole trail next
    year.

    Dominica, The Nature Island
    By Nancy Felix

    Are you looking for a good hiking destination this winter? Let me
    recommend the Caribbean Island of Dominica. Known as “the Nature
    Island,” Dominica, is a small island nation in the Lesser Antilles, not to be
    confused with the much larger and more familiar Dominican Republic. We
    just returned (2024) from our second trip there. It is a magical place with
    mountains, rain forest, and of course the surrounding ocean. Hikes range
    from short trips to waterfalls to day-long expeditions. And for the really
    adventurous, there is the Waitukubuli National Trail which covers 115 miles
    in 14 segments winding over the island from south to north.

                                            On our first trip, we hiked the
                                            Middleham Falls trail in Morne Trois
                                            Piton National Park, which is 4 miles
                                            round trip to a waterfall. It was our
                                            first visit to a rainforest, and we
                                            immediately fell in love with it. We spent hours taking photos of the
                                            amazing tropical plants, and a few colorful crabs and snails. I must admit we
                                            never made it to the waterfall, a goal for another day.

                                            Our second hike was to Emerald Pool, a much shorter walk, and we did get
                                            to the waterfall. On the way we saw the most amazing ferns, mosses and
                                            liverworts. These plants thrive in the warm, damp climate and cover the sides
                                            of the trails. The pool at the base of the waterfall made an excellent
                                            swimming hole.

    On our recent trip, we hiked to several more waterfalls. The first, Spanny
    Falls, was down a trail with high rock walls covered with tropical plants. The
    second, Trafalgar Falls is a double waterfall with Father Falls, the tallest with
    hot water, and Mother Falls, shorter with cold water. We also hiked the Boeri
    Lake trail which had fascinating plants, and amazing views of the
    surrounding mountains and nearby Fresh Water Lake.

    Snorkeling was fun on both trips with lots of tropical fish to enjoy.
    Snorkeling sites are on the western or Caribbean side of the island (the
    Atlantic side is too rough). We snorkeled at Toucari Bay in the Northwest,
    Champagne Beach and Scotts Head in the Southwest, and in front of the
    Fort Young Hotel in Roseau on a day with no cruise ships. We hear that
    scuba diving off Scotts Head is even more spectacular with turtles and
    healthy coral reefs. We need to check it out on our next visit.

    The above describes only a sample of things to do in Dominica. There are hot springs in the southern part of the
    island, and a Boiling Lake hike. In the North, there is Cabrits National Park with historic Fort Shirley, and dry
    forest and scrub where anoles and the Dominican ground lizard can be found. Nearby there is a guided oar-boat

    Nature Notes: Red-Tail Hawks in Winter
    By Terri Armata
                                              Red-tail Hawks, (buteo jamaicensis), are seen in our area all year round,
                                              soaring in lazy circles in the sky, or perched in trees scanning for prey. Most
                                              are short distance migrants, leaving home territories when food becomes
                                              scarce due to colder temperatures or snow cover. Every August juvenile
                                              Red-tails begin to disperse from their nests and move south. Adults follow
                                              starting in late September with peak migration taking place in October and
                                              November. Some of these far northern birds will spend the winter here in
                                              Southern Vermont, hunting mice, voles, moles, rabbits, some birds and
                                              eating carrion. Depending on snowpack and prey, many of our residents
                                              will also remain here all winter. The Eastern Red-tail, subspecies borealis
                                              are most common, having a
    medium brown head, white throats, an obvious belly band and a red tail
    if adult. Juvenile tails are brown with narrow dark brown bands. A
    northern subspecies, buteo jamaicensis abieticola, which breeds in
    Quebec and Labrador has dark heads, rich coloration, and heavy
    streaking. A third subspecies, buteo jamaicensis calurus is mostly found
    in interior Alaska and the Western US. Some of these may find their
    way East. They have brown-red throats and wide dark belly bands. Be
    on the lookout for leucistic or partial albinos, as some have also been
    reported in Southern Vermont in past years.

         What if it Doesn’t Snow?
         By Margie March

             The fall question has always been, When will it snow? This fall was unusually dry and sunny and the leaves were
         in no hurry to come off the trees. We hiked and scuffed through the leaves and came home with unusually dry
         boots. At garden’s end, we had the rare experience of easily pulling up plants and shaking the dry soil off their
         roots, and having plenty of time to prepare the beds for winter. This made me wonder, will the winter also be
         unusual? Will we have a dry winter? There have been open winters in the past.

             That was a worrisome thought. Winters should have snow. But rather than fret, I suggest we get out our skis,
         snowshoes, and winter equipment, but keep our hiking sticks out and make a mental adjustment. I suggest that we
         embrace the possibility of extended exploration of our woods and hills. Crossing brooks, climbing over downed
         trees, and being able to see the trail would be easier on foot. We might find this a season to investigate our
         ecological communities. There are Northern Hardwood/Hemlock Forests, but also Northern Hardwood/Pine
         Forests, Red Pine Rocky Summits, Alpine Krummholz, Spruce and Fir Summits, and Balsam Flats. We could see
         how many ferns stay green in the winter, or hope to find evidence of animals in winter, such as spotting a
         porcupine.

           The Greylock Glen Outdoor Center, on the Adams side of Mount Greylock, will host a New Years Day one
         hour walk to look for signs of wildlife, watch and listen for birds, and see how plants survive the winter. Yes, an
         open winter is unusual, but could be interesting if we have changed our expectations. However, I am sure we could
         adjust to a big dump of snow.

    Biking the Erie Canal Part 2: Syracuse to Albany
    by Ann Martin

    Having ridden the Erie Canal Trail from Buffalo to Syracuse in August, we decided that September would be a
    good time to finish the journey from Syracuse to Albany. Preparation was easier this time as we had a better idea of
    what the trail was like and what to pack.

                                       We decided to take Amtrak with our bikes from Albany to Syracuse and leave our
                                       car at the rail station. I was nervous about taking our bikes on Amtrak - How
                                       would we know where to put them? Would we have enough time to get all the
                                       panniers off and get the bikes on? Would they be in different cars? So, as most
                                       things do, it all worked out OK. The Amtrak staff was very helpful, there was
                                       plenty of time to load things on board. There were only 2 bike cars with room
                                       for 1 bike in each car. We had to remove the front wheel from the bike and hang
                                       it up by the rear wheel. (Make sure you have reservations for your bikes as they
                                       have very limited space.)

                                        Finding the bike trail from the train station wasn’t
                                        too onerous, however the journey through
                                        Syracuse was not fun. There is a dedicated bike
                                     path along the side of the road but it crosses back
    and forth across the road many times, and watching for cars and hearing the traffic
    noise was not great. Once out of Syracuse the path becomes much nicer. We only
    rode about 15 miles and camped at Green Lakes State Park for our first night, where
    they are very accommodating to bicyclists. Any bicyclist can come in with no
    reservation, the cost is nominal, showers are free, and the park is lovely. We had
    forgotten our insect repellent, so were happy to see that it was for sale as we checked in - along with 2 salty bags of
    chips.

                                The next section of trail runs alongside the original Erie canal and is a straight, stone-
                                dust path right next to the canal. It runs through picturesque small towns and has many
                                interesting historical signs along the path which tell about life on the canal boats,
                                building of the canal, and much more. We weren’t looking to get as far as fast as we
                                could, so stopping and reading the signs was very enjoyable and enlightening for us. We
                                saw a beaver, some herons, and a crane. Our Dabrim hats continued to give us great
                                shade from the sun and glare.

                                We paused at Lock 21 to watch a boat go through the lock - it was going to the west so
                                the water had to be let out of the lock to lower the boat
                                to go on its way down the canal. So cool to watch!

    That night we stayed outside of Utica at Lock 20 which is a New York State Canal
    Corporation Cycler-Hiker-Boater campsite. They offer 18 of these types of sites
    along the Canalway Trail. These campsites are designated as “primitive” with a
    place for 4 tents, usually with picnic table, and a Porta-john. Campers have to bring
    their own potable water, cooking supplies, etc. There were day visitors there fishing
    and picnicking, but only one other bike camper. We woke up to deep fog with
    everything soggy and decided to ride awhile to get a good breakfast.

                                         We rolled into Little Falls about 1:00 pm and decided to take a half-day off and
                                         stay in a hotel. After a couple of days of dust, sweat, sunscreen, and insect

    some dust, some paved, some on road. We stopped briefly at Sprakers Church
    where they set out snacks and water for bikers. We paused at a beautiful lock

    stayed away.

    Our last day brought us through some nice sections of trail, however there are other sections that run very close to
    the freeway and there is a lot of road noise. This whole day was on good paved trails and that made the riding
    easier. Coming into Albany it was tricky to find the way over the busy bridge and into Rensselaer where our car was
    parked.

     It's always fun to finish a journey, look back and say “Wow, we really did that!” Then it was home to rest the legs

    Trail Maintenance Training Day and Workdays
    by Ann Martin

    Barb and Robin attended a workshop on trail maintenance while at the
    GMC Annual Meeting. They were so impressed that they were thrilled
    when the offer came for someone from headquarters to come down to
    Bennington to lead a day-long workshop outing along part of the LT
    from Route 9 to the north. On October 26, Lorne Currier from GMC
                              headquarters drove down to meet with about
                              a dozen local volunteers to learn how to
                              keep our trails in good order. His two main
                              guidelines were that we need to “Keep Water
                              Off the Trail” and “Keep People on the Trail.” Water can be very destructive to trails,
                              hastening erosion and creating boggy areas. So, we learned about water bars and how to
                              direct water off trails. Hikers sometimes take shortcuts or walk aside a main trail
                              widening it, again causing erosion and deterioration of trails. We learned how to create
                              brush-ins, which are piles of big logs and brush, on areas where we don’t want people to
                              go. We also discussed blazing, clipping, and clearing blowdowns.

                               Then, a few weeks later we put our learning into practice during two workdays.
    Volunteers created brush-ins, freshened blazes, and dug out water bars. While we are out working, hikers are always
    very appreciative of our efforts. We can always use more volunteers! mailto:benningtongmc@gmail.com

    St. Augustine Beach Expedition
    by David Pisaneschi

    SUNDAY NOVEMBER 17 TO SATURDAY NOVEMBER 23, 2024

    The setting for our trip was the beautiful 1,500-acre preserve situated in the northern part of Flagler County. It was
    purchased by Henry Cutting in 1886 and passed on to his widow Angela Mills Cutting Worden, who eventually
    married Boris Scherbatoff, an exiled Russian prince. Angela assumed the title of princess and it was then that the
    once named “Cherokee Grove” came to be known as Princess Place. We enjoyed a week of wonderful adventure
    close to St. Augustine Beach. Kayaking coastal estuaries, touring historical sites, and hiking through hammocks,
    scrub, and flatwoods. In the evening we relaxed at our private lodge on the banks of Pellicer Creek

    Day 1 Sunday Nov 17: After meeting up with our group and guide at the Orlando airport, we started the trip to
    our lodge. After lunch at a seaside cafe, Pier 220 in Titusville, we met the rest of our group at the lodge. After
    dinner, a black light hike revealed some interesting critters. Board games topped off the evening.

    Day 2 Monday Nov 18: We paddled Pellicer Creek from the Princess Place Preserve. We put in at the park
    launch site, took it out by our cabins. We paddled up to the I-95 overpass. Stopped at Faver Dykes State Park on
    our way out. Covered 8.85 miles in 4.25 hours. In the afternoon, we hiked the horse trail loop encountering several
    knee deep wet sections. Total hiking distance was 4.5 miles.

    Day 3 Tuesday Nov 19: Our put-in was at the Haw Creek Preserve. This Preserve is tucked away in western Flagler
    County. It’s a 1,000-acre tract of land bordering Haw Creek for about 2 miles on its southern boundary. While
    waiting for our shuttle drivers to return, we explored the boardwalk, being treated to views of the backwater
    swamp and Haw Creek. Upon our driver's return, we proceeded down Haw Creek. Some obstructions created
    excitement. We observed several King Fishers engaged in combat, blue herons, white ibises, a green heron, great
    egrets and lots of small gators. After finally reaching Dead Lake, we proceeded the last 1.5 miles across open water
    to our lunch stop at the Bull Creek launch site. After lunch, some of us explored Bull Creek, which turned out to
    be a short exploration, due to numerous obstructions. For the entire day, we paddled 8.42 miles in 4.25 hours.

    Day 4 Wednesday Nov 20: Paddled Deep Creek. Put it at the bridge off Highway 207. We started paddling
    upstream, but obstructions and blowdowns forced us to turn around. So, we backtracked and paddled back past
    our takeout and under the bridge, continuing downstream. Obstructions and blow downs created excitement. We
    turned around just before the confluence with the St Johns river. We observed turtles, along with a small and a
    large gator. We also saw piloted woodpeckers, wood ducks, sandhill cranes, anhingas, and black king fishers, We
    had lunch at the take-out. It was a short paddle. We paddled 6.61 miles in 3.00 hours. In the afternoon, some of us
    explored trails near the cabins at Princess Place covering 4.00 miles in 1.5 hours.

    Day 5 Thursday Nov 21: The weather turned chilly overnight with temperatures in the upper 40s. A stiff wind with
    temperatures in the 60s greeted us as we put in to paddle the Ocklawaha at the Kirkpatrick boat launch below the
    Rodman Dam. Our take-out was at the Johnson Field Boat Ramp on Route 19 close to Palatka. A wide channel,
    without obstructions and quick current allowed us to complete the 7.31 mile route in 2.5 hours. After lunch, we
    hiked in Ravine Gardens SP and visited the Azalea City Brewing Company in Palatka for some liquid refreshment.

    Day 5 Friday Nov 22: With morning temperatures in the 40s and 15 mph winds gusting to 30mph, hiking was
    more appealing than paddling. In the morning, we walked the trails and roads of the preserve, hiking 5 miles in 2
    hours. In the afternoon, we were treated to a guided tour of the historic lodge.
