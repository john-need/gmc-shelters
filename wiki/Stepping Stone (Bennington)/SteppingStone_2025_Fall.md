Nature Notes: Here Be Giants!
By Terri Armata

The week of August 5, I was walking a Forest Service Road in the Arlington/Stratton area, surveying for butterflies
on blooming Joe Pye Weed when a large black and lemon butterfly caught my eye. It was an Eastern Giant
Swallowtail, (Papilio cresphontes), nectaring furiously on the flowers. The Eastern Giant is a southern resident and
some disperse north in late summer. If the winter is mild their caterpillars may overwinter and Giants will appear in
the spring. There have been overwintering colonies around Lake Champlain. Easy to identify, it is large with a black
or dark brown dorsal side that is crossed with yellow bands and the ventral side is an eye popping lemon yellow.
The body is also lemon yellow. Its host plants are in the citrus family. Here in Vermont the shrub Prickly Ash (this
is in the citrus family) and the introduced garden plant Common Rue serve as host plants. The caterpillars look like
bird droppings and overwinter as pupae.




The Hills Are Alive: Hiking in Bavaria and Austria
by Kathy Kindness

                                                         “The hills are alive, with the sound of music….” Our
                                                         voices floated to the cerulean sky as we channeled our
                                                         inner Julie Andrews, singing words both trite and true. A
                                                         birdwatcher stood with her head tilted, listening to birds
                                                         she’d yet to identify. Cow, goat, and sheep bells softly
                                                         tinkled as we passed through turnstiles into verdant
                                                         pastures. Passing hikers greeted us with morgen or hallo.
                                                         At each bend in the path, we oohed and aahed at the
                                                         jagged peaks around us. We were in a hiker’s paradise, and
                                                         it was difficult not to break into song at each new
                                                         spectacular and jaw-dropping view.

                                                         In mid-June, our group of twelve, most from Western
                                                         MA and Vermont, day hiked in the Alps along the border
                                                         between Bavaria and Austria. Dee Dice, an Adventure
                                                         Travel leader with the Appalachian Mountain Club,
                                                         entrepreneur, and founder of Constant Growth
Adventures, led the trip titled Majestic Peaks and Lush Gorges. Dee had meticulously mapped out our itinerary, so
we had nothing to do but hike (up to 10 miles per day), eat (love that strudel), and ogle.

We began our trip in Garmisch-Partenkirchen,
Bavaria, formerly two separate towns on either
side of the Isar River. Hitler forced the centuries-
old towns to merge to host the 1936 Olympics.




                                                   This quintessential alpine community is filled with vibrant
                                                   buildings decorated with lüftlmalerei or mural art. These frescos
                                                   depict religious and folk themes, as well as architectural details.
                                                   Many homes are adorned by carved wooden balconies and loads
                                                   of colorful geraniums. Nature and town blend seamlessly as
                                                   Alpspitze and, farther away, Zugspitze (Germany’s highest peak)
                                                   tower over stucco buildings, some with tile roofs. One can hike
                                                   and explore by simply walking out of town.




Recovering from jet lag on our first day, we rode a cable car to the
summit of Zugspitze. Standing at a majestic 9,718 feet above sea
level, the summit typically offers breathtaking views of Germany,
Austria, Italy, and Switzerland. Our view was shrouded in mist,
however, so we focused on the ravages of climate change; two of
Germany’s last glaciers reside at the summit, and a third has been
downgraded as “dead ice.” The thought that future generations will
not experience those iconic snow-capped peaks, and that indeed the
whole nature of alpine life will alter with the melting snowpack,
tempered our giddiness at sledding in June.

The next days dawned bright and sunny, if unseasonably hot.
Periodically breaking into song, we summited Wank, standing at
5,840 above sea level. Nothing prepared us for eye-popping views
of the Alps as we traversed the well-kept trail to the summit Wank
Haus (where we enjoyed views of Zugspitze while eating torte and
apfel strudel). The next day, a short walk from town led us to the
Partnachklamm, featuring mounted walkways and tunnels. There,
the shimmering light-blue waters of the Partnach continue to
hollow and shape the limestone gorge. After, we hiked up and onward
to Wamberg, one of Germany’s highest “church villages.” Viewing the onion-domed church and stone-walled
cemetery from afar, we felt as if we’d stepped centuries back in time.

We were surprised at the Bavarians’ reverence for their Catholic
roots. Since 2018, public buildings are required to display crosses.
Small chapels dot the landscape next to grazing cattle. Many wayside
crucifixes and memorials pop up on hiking trails, mountain summits,
waterfall grottos, the tunnels leading to Zugspitze’ summit, and even
along gorges.

On day four we traveled a short distance to Mittenwald, its old town
center dominated by the Kirche of St. Peter and Paul. This
picturesque town, with its cobble-stoned streets along the Isar River,
was designated a market town in the 11th century. Evolving along an
important Roman trading route (Venice to Augsburg), the town has
also been a violin-making center for 300 years. The
Geigenbaumuseum (violin making museum) details Mittenwald’s
history, including how trees in the alpine climate have been
instrumental in the production of world-class violins.

From Mittenwald, we summited Hoher Kranzberg and were
rewarded with 360-degree views and respite from the heat. Later, we
took the Karwendelbahn up 7,362 feet on Germany’s second
highest cable car ride. The summit straddles both Austria and
Bavaria. We circumnavigated the steep cirque on summit trails
through the limestone scree dotted with tiny alpine flowers. In warm
weather, hikers can do the Mittenwalder Klettersteig (fixed rope route) over 8 peaks, and in winter, expert “off-
piste” skiers can ski Germany’s longest ungroomed skiing route. Sadly, in winter 2024-5 there wasn’t enough snow
to ski the seven-kilometer trail. We opted for beer from “Germany’s highest brewery” after our full day of
exploration.

Art in the Landscape
By Kate Follett

I’ve been painting seriously for about ten years, and the natural world is my biggest inspiration. As the landscape
has influenced my art, so too has art influenced how I see the landscape.

A surprising amount of artistic “talent” lies not in applying paint to a canvas, but in learning how to see the world
as it truly appears. Noticing and identifying shapes, colors, and light is the most challenging—and most rewarding
—part of visual art. With time and a few tips, you can also learn to enjoy the hidden visual world of the artist.

The Forest vs. the Trees

Art lovers are often impressed by the amount of detail in a picture. While detail can make a painting lifelike, it is
also the secret enemy of a landscape artist. The sheer amount of detail in a natural landscape can be overwhelming.

Next time you’re outdoors, try this exercise. Find a view a short distance away, such as across a pond or meadow. If
your vision is good, you can probably see very small details even at a distance—individual leaves, twigs, and rocks.
However, if you look at one leaf at a time, it’s impossible to view the entire scene. The same is true if you try to

paint one leaf at a time. The artist—and the viewer—gets overwhelmed by details without seeing a whole subject.
It also takes forever!

A large part of creating a successful painting is simplifying the major elements of a scene. Give your eyes a rest by
closing them or looking away for a moment. When you look back at your view, relax your eyes so they aren’t as
focused. Imagine trying to depict the scene not with paint, but with a collage of construction paper, cut out with
kindergarten scissors. What big, broad shapes would you make? Most landscape artists start their compositions
with these basic shapes.

When I prepare a painting, I look for two or three large, interesting elements that make up the view. Often, I am
drawn to shapes made by light as it hits the land or water. Maybe it’s a slash of bright sun across yellow grass, or
the blobby shapes of trees in fog. Only after getting a sense of the larger forms do I go back and add smaller
details. Sometimes, the big pieces are satisfying enough without getting too fussy.




    Marsh Grass, oil on paper. I was drawn to this scene by the bright gold band of sunlight on the reeds. The pine
         trees on the shore form a single, dark shape with just a few details to suggest trunks and branches.

Light vs. Local Color

One of the trickiest things an artist must see is the true color of objects. As children, we identify what artists call
“local color.” Grass is green. The sky is blue. Snow is white. As children (and as beginning artists), we paint by
reproducing local color—green trees with brown trunks next to a blue river under a turquoise sky—and the results
are predictably childish. This is because local color changes completely depending on an object’s location and the
light and air around it.

The color of an object in context is called “apparent color.” Ignoring what you know about local color and learning
to see apparent color is one of the most difficult—but most rewarding—skills an artist can learn.

An unexpected way to improve your color vision is doing jigsaw puzzles of nature scenes. Have you ever been
confused by the colors in a puzzle piece, only for them to make sense once the piece drops into place? Each puzzle
piece, separated from the larger object, shows the apparent colors, free from our preconceptions of local color.

Seeing apparent color is so challenging that even professional artists resort to tricks to identify it. (Or at least, I do!)
I usually make paintings from photographs, and I use technology to help me see apparent colors. Many photo
editors have an “eyedropper” tool that allows you to select a color from a specific point in a photograph, almost
like isolating the color in a puzzle piece. If you have a photo editor, try out the eyedropper on a nature photo you
enjoy. First, look at something and say what color you think it is—“green” grass or “blue” water. Then, use the
eyedropper to select what color it really is. The complex texture of grass is made up of yellows, browns, and blues.
Water is often the color of what it reflects—blue sky, dark hillsides, or gray overcast.




                                                                               In the outdoors, snow is wonderful for
                                                                               finding apparent colors. We “know” snow
                                                                               is white, but if you painted a snowy
                                                                               landscape with pure white paint, it would
                                                                               look glaring and flat. Because of its
                                                                               crystal structure, snow is particularly good
                                                                               at reflecting the different colors of light,
                                                                               shadows, and nearby objects. Winter
                                                                               sunlight, with its low angles, will make
                                                                               snow glow yellow or even pink, while
                                                                               shadows reflect intense sky blue. Snow
                                                                               that lies deep in shade, such as under
                                                                               evergreens, can be surprisingly dark. At
                                                                               sunset, a snowy landscape will reflect
                                                                               deep violet, blue, and even green.

Seeing Air

One color phenomenon is easy to see, but before I became an artist, I wasn’t consciously aware of it. Atmospheric
perspective is the idea that air and distance affect the color of objects in an outdoor scene. The more distant the
object, and the more air between you and it, the more the color changes.

If you’ve stood on top of one of Vermont’s mountains and taken in the view, you’ve seen atmospheric perspective.
As nearby hills recede into the distance, their rich green color becomes paler and bluer the further away they are.
The daytime sky also becomes paler towards the horizon. This is because air creates a faint but perceptible haze
with distance.

Atmospheric perspective changes depending on the weather. This has a great effect on the mood of a landscape,
and of a painting. The hot, humid air of summer makes colors even paler with distance, creating a hazy, lazy
feeling. A chilly fall or winter day will leave colors darker and bolder, creating a crisp, bright feeling.




   Wildfire Haze, oil on paper. The recent Canadian wildfires greatly increased atmospheric perspective, giving this
                                               view a ghostly quality.

Whenever I go outdoors, I keep my eyes open to shape, to color, to air, and to light, looking for scenes that could
make a painting. I’ve never lacked for subject matter—I have hundreds of landscape photographs waiting to
become art. But even if I don’t “see a painting” while on a hike, ski, or kayak, I find myself appreciating the visual
landscape in a much deeper way because of my artistic training. It’s a positive feedback loop where learning nature
improves my painting, and improving my painting helps me see more in nature. Even if you never pick up a brush
or pencil, try looking at the landscape with an artistic eye.

Autumn Poetry
by Lorna Cheriton

Autumn Walk

We are two trees
trunks separate
for the first five feet
then intertwined,

We are arms
reaching around the girth
of a giant tree,

We are moist moss and dry lichens -
symbiotic,

We are rough bark
and slippery needles,

We are dry leaves
whispering to each other,

We are October's blaze
and November's muted colors,

We are sweating uphill
and lying with sunlight
on translucent eyeballs
opening to see
distant purple mountains,

We are careening downhill,
slipping on loose rocks,
picking our way
across the river on logs,
we are an autumn couple

walking together.


If a life can be a year, twelve months,
I am in October,
when leaves turn brown and wither,
hair loses color, turns to grey;

strawberries are long gone
and peaches disappear,
but apples ripen,
maples blaze with flame,
the autumn sky is azure,

April’s angst long past.

by Ann Martin

horses, and you have a rail trail! These trails often go through lovely country and small towns. They can be surfaced
with asphalt, crushed granite, or hard-pack. They don’t have any large ups and downs, and there are no motorized
vehicles allowed. Perfect for our desires to go biking!

In deciding where to go, we looked at Pennsylvania and Ohio. These states had many railroads in the past, and both
places have done a great job of converting many of them to rail trails. Pennsylvania boasts over 2000 miles of rail

month! One nice thing is that you can ride as long or as short as

Teshekpuk Packraft 6/26-7/3/25
by David Pisaneschi

BACKGROUND

 I just returned from an area so remote, wild and untouched, that it's literally a blank spot on the map. No
guidebook describes it. The only person that I know who's been there is the well-known Alaska wild-land traveler
Roman Dial, when he traversed this area in his walk across Arctic Alaska in 2006. On this trip , I pack-rafted a river
draining into NPR-A’s Teshekpuk Lake that is so remote and little known that even Roman has never heard of it.

The largest single tract of wild public land in America, a landscape so vast and diverse it defies superlatives, is
known by a bland and somewhat misleading four-letter acronym: NPR-A.

While the NPR-A, or National Petroleum Reserve-
Alaska, does have oil beneath it, the 23-million-acre
expanse is also arguably the most important wetland
habitat complex in the Circumpolar Arctic for birds -
the breeding, nesting, molting, and pre-migratory
staging grounds for several million birds every year.
NPR-A was established in the western area of the
North Slope by an executive order from President
Warren G. Harding in 1923 to ensure energy reserves
for the U.S. Navy as it transitioned from coal to oil.

In 1976, Congress transferred management to the
Department of the Interior, continuing subsurface oil
and gas exploration, but also directing the Bureau of
Land Management to provide “maximum protection”
for surface areas with “significant subsistence,
recreational, fish and wildlife, historical or scenic
values.”

 U.S. Fish and Wildlife surveys in the reserve led to the
establishment of “Special Areas”—a land
management designation unique to Alaska that’s
placed on habitat of the most value for wildlife.

 Teshekpuk Lake, where I visited, is one of five
“Special Areas.” Teshekpuk Lake and the surrounding
tundra and wetlands host more breeding and molting
birds than any other part of Alaska’s Arctic. South of
Teshekpuk Lake is an area of sandy ridges, long river
bluffs, and flowered tundra benches winding between
and through a maze of ponds and lakes. This mosaic of water and land is thick with wildlife during the brief
summer. Birds from around the world migrate to Teshekpuk’s wetlands to breed. All four species of eider ducks, an
estimated 600,000 shorebirds and hundreds of thousands of geese including 1/3 of the world's brant, yellow-billed
loons, along with jaegers, snowy owls and a variety of passerines breed and molt in the area. This abundance of
bird life supports foxes, weasels, including wolverines, and a host of avian predators. The area is also home to the
Teshekpuk Caribou Herd, our only non-migratory caribou herd in Arctic Alaska. These 40,000-odd caribou spend
the entire year on the Arctic Slope and tend to gather south of the lake in late June to feed on the fresh green
tundra. We observed caribou on several occasions, including a large herd , which came close up on our last day,
while waiting for our float plane to pick us up at Teshekpuk Lake.

TRIP DESCRIPTION

June 25 : I met my guide, Alex, along with Matt and Scott at the Arctic Wild headquarters for a pre-trip meeting
where we checked gear, and discussed trip logistics.

June 26 : Alex picked me up at 7:15am for our flight with Wright Air Service from Fairbanks across the Yukon

Dunes. Scott, Matt and I flew to Dead Horse, where after a short layover, we boarded a bush plane to rendezvous
with Alex at the Pik Dunes. After setting up camp , we took a long walk where we observed caribou and numerous
birds.

June 27: We had a full day to explore the Pik Dunes and the surrounding wetlands. The walking was excellent in the
sand. It was a great day of hiking and exploring. We hiked to a nearby ridge for spectacular views. We then crossed
over to another ridge which provided numerous bird and caribou sightings. We then returned to camp for a short
nap. Took another short walk after dinner. Hiked 6 miles in 6 hours.

June 28: Broke camp and had a challenging 3 mile hike with 65-pound packs to our first lake. We broke out and
inflated our raft and paddled across the first lake to the second lake. We stopped along the way to view an
archaeological site. Continued on to camp #2. Covered 6 miles in 6 hours. 3 hours hiking; 3 hours of paddling.
