Nature Notes: Vermonters Invited to Search for Butter ies
By Terri Armata

Contact: Emily Anderson, eanderson@vtecostudies.org (802) 649-1431 x209

NORWICH, VT –
Vermonters now have another
excuse to get outside on sunny
days: to join a statewide survey
of the most angelic insects—
butterflies. The Vermont
Center for Ecostudies (VCE)
is recruiting volunteers to help
search fields and fens,
mountains and meadows, and
even their own backyards to
help document the status of
Vermont’s butterflies.

Butterflies were largely a mystery in Vermont before hundreds of volunteer community scientists helped complete
the first Vermont Butterfly Atlas (2002-2007), heralding a new era for their conservation. Twenty years later, the
second atlas is poised to detect changes in butterfly populations and provide essential information for
environmental management and policy.

“The 2nd Vermont Butterfly Atlas is a rare opportunity for us to understand long-term trends in butterfly
populations and prescribe conservation actions to both keep common species common and reverse the trends for
those in trouble,” said Kent McFarland, VCE biologist and director of the atlas.

The Vermont Butterfly Atlas is a five-year survey completed every 20 years to document the abundance,
distribution, and conservation status of butterflies across Vermont with the help of volunteer community
scientists. It is part of a network of atlases across the Northeast, spanning from Connecticut through the Canadian
Maritimes, and gives a landscape view of the butterfly fauna invaluable for conservation and a priceless snapshot
for future comparisons. Vermont is poised to be the first state or province in the region to repeat a butterfly atlas
20 years later.

Most anyone with a sharp eye can contribute. Everyone knows what a butterfly looks like, and many carry a digital
camera at all times—their smartphones. With a bit of training, it's easy to watch butterflies and report results to the
Vermont Butterfly Atlas at e-Butterfly.org, a worldwide butterfly reporting system built by VCE, the Montreal
Space for Life, and other partners. There is even a built-in computer vision system to help users identify the
butterflies in their images.

“We’re excited to offer training sessions for new participants at butterfly hotspots across the state as well as online
workshops on butterfly atlasing,” said Nathaniel Sharp, VCE biologist and atlas coordinator. “While there are tons
of great field guides and online resources listed on our website, getting outside searching for butterflies is the best
way to meet other butterfly lovers, learn how to identify local species, and get motivated to document the
butterflies in your neighborhood and beyond.” To learn more and join the survey, type in Vermont Butterfly Atlas
on your search engine.

Field trips in the Bennington area to be announced in Meetup.

Sleep Together, Paddle Separately?
By Lorna Cheriton

Asked to write something about the paddling Hamilton and I have done, I recall that, on almost our first date, we
canoed the Esopus River in New York State when it was in full spring flood. I was impressed that Ham stopped
the canoe on a small island where he made his way to the far end of the island to scout the river ahead. As I held
the canoe against the strong current, I saw a stick floating by that looked amazingly like one of our paddles. It
was! I grabbed it, getting myself thoroughly wet in the process. Later we learned someone had drowned in the
river that weekend when their canoe wrapped around a bridge bulwark.

We went on to paddle the North Saskatchewan River during visits to Alberta, the Missinabi River in Ontario as
far as James Bay, the Yukon River in northern Canada, and the Noatak River in Alaska.

In Bennington, our friends Tom and NancyJean Steffen introduced us to kayaking,
lending us their kayaks. We four took a beginning white water course at Zoar in
Massachusetts. We learned the kayakers’ motto, “Sleep together, paddle
separately.” With NancyJean and other friends, we’ve explored local rivers, hearing
frogs and watching tadpoles in the spring, and collecting fiddleheads at the
beginning of May. Kayaking the Walloomsac River from the Henry Bridge where
we put in at the Jeanne McWaters Park, we have the fun of navigating currents,
running a few rapids, surfing the occasional wave, and seeing wildlife – merganzers
who take flight as we approach but usually fly downstream where we inevitably                  Great Blue Heron
disturb them again, kingfishers flying across the river, Great Blue Herons, eagles,
and choruses of frogs in springtime.

A further drive away, the Battenkill is a beautiful river to paddle. Unless we take two vehicles, we usually place a
bicycle at the state park in New York state or at the Eagleville covered bridge, then return to put in at the golf
course on Route 313. At the take-out, one of us rides the bike back to get the truck. Further afield, we have
paddled the Deerfield River in Massachusetts, during water releases, either the upper section down to Zoar Gap,
or the lower Section below Zoar Gap. Paddling the fierce rapids of Zoar Gap itself has been an adrenalin-
producing experience. Both of us have capsized in those turbulent, class III-IV waters, with Hamilton once
breaking his glasses and bloodying his face after being scraped across boulders. With snow melt in spring or after a
strong rain, there is the rare opportunity to paddle the branch of the Deerfield River (usually too shallow and
rocky) from mid-way along the Somerset Road downstream to Searsburg reservoir close to Route 9.

Book Review: Ten Million Steps
By Hal March

Ten Million Steps by M. J. Eberhart (as trail name “Nimblewill Nomad”)

                                  Nimblewill Nomad writes about his ten month, 4400 mile hike up the East Coast, from
                                  the Florida Keys to Quebec on a so-called Eastern Continental Trail, which includes the
                                  FT (Florida National Scenic Trail), the AT (Appalachian Trail), the IAT (International
                                  Appalachian Trail) and several others. Thru hiking a route like this presents many
                                  challenges, and Nimblewill has, of course, a few problems. And like more than a few
                                  other book writing thru-hikers, he is carrying an extra burden in addition to the pack on
                                  his back: a long list of personal failures that he sums up as having “missed the mark on
                                  living.” This hike is his “Spiritual Journey,” a quest for inner peace, and this leads him to
                                  constantly find a bright side in whatever horrific jam he gets into. It turns out that none
                                  will be worse than his first day.

                           On January 1, 1998, apparently not discouraged by the reported “wettest season there
                           since 1940” or the news that the now flooded Florida National Scenic Trail is
“impassable” and “technically closed,” Nimblewell wades off into the Everglades. At a deep spot he goes down,
almost loses his pack, loses breakfast and at some point loses the minimally blazed trail. But he sloshes along until
dark, when he realizes that he doesn’t know which way to go, he’s getting cold, and he’s beginning to hear sounds
of the swamp at night. Clinging to a cypress tree, he tries to reassure himself, saying “calm down…if you must stay

Am-Trekking the USA
By Billy Martin

When is the last time you took an AMTRAK vacation? For business? Or perhaps you were yearning for a nostalgic
ride in a train coach enchanted with the scenery of America passing by, as you grab a bite to eat in the club car?

If you are going to hike the Cascades in Washington, walk Sabino Canyon in Tucson, pedal a rail trail near St.
Louis, or meander along the River Walk in San Antonio, you just might consider AMTRAK.

No Way! Never! Too Slow! Doesn’t leave or arrive when I want to! Are you kidding me?

Time for a whistle stop. Perhaps you have never heard of the 30 day, travel anywhere on the rail system between
any of their hundreds of destinations, no limit on distance, get on and off 10 times in that 30 day period AND for
a low fixed price. (Currently at this writing the Amtrak USA Rail Pass costs $499 per person) PS - They have sales
occasionally - we purchased the ticket at $299. https://www.amtrak.com/tickets/departure-rail-pass.html

                                                        Some Pros:
                                                        •Simply board the train, no hassles, no security checks,
                                                        just show something to prove you have a reservation
                                                        •Be amazed at the size and comfort of the large seat:
                                                        reclines, foot rest, adjustment under the thigh to relieve
                                                        pressure on your leg when awake or sleeping (longer
                                                        distance trains have more room)
                                                        •Only 2 seats on each side of center walkway
                                                        •2 carry-ons per person that store in a large area above
                                                        the seat
                                                        •2 larger bags that may be checked (free) or stored in a
                                                        separate section
                                                        •Bathrooms in each carriage
                   Observation Car                      •Club car for snacks or major meals
                                                       •Observation car with huge windows to sit and watch
                                                     America pass by (on some routes)
•   Use the AMTRAK app to schedule a departure or arrival destination, payment, find the train schedule
•   Get up and walk between cars when you desire
•   At some intermediate train stops you may depart the train and walk around outside
•   You will view some of America’s most iconic train terminals as you travel: Stunning period architecture, high
    vaulted ceilings, grandeur in all respects

Some Cons:
• A train leaves and departs on its schedule. You may have to depart/arrive at a time that is not convenient.

• You may need a warm covering or jacket while resting or asleep

of the experience. Our only wish was that we could have seen more of America while traveling during the night.
Not a big complaint since were sleeping quite soundly in our travel berths.
