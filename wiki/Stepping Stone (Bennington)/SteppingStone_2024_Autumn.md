Message from the Co-Presidents
By Barb Raskin and Tim Marr

Greetings from your Co-Presidents, Tim Marr and Barb Raskin:

For the first time that we’re aware of, the Bennington Section of the GMC has two co-presidents rather than a
president and vice-president. This arrangement grew from Tim’s idea that two co-leaders would bring more energy
and ideas to our leadership at a time when our membership is growing, and a new generation of leaders are
emerging. As Tim has served two terms as section president in the past, Barb has also appreciated the ready
availability of someone familiar with the policies and procedures of the section and GMC overall. We both find
that this new collaboration is exciting, fun and productive.

Although our membership voted to approve this new leadership arrangement last spring at our annual meeting, our
section bylaws still dictate that we have the more traditional stacked leadership arrangement of president and vice
president. In future executive meetings, we may consider changing our bylaws to allow for more flexible approaches
to section leadership, as well as updating the bylaws overall as they were last revised in the 1990’s. Any proposed
bylaws changes will be shared with membership, and will need to be voted on by all at our next spring annual
meeting. You can expect to see more on that in the coming months.

We want to give a shout out to Reed Goossen for serving as our section president from 2020-2024. Thanks, Reed!

We see the Bennington Section of the GMC as a vital, active, outdoor organization which shares the mission of
our parent GMC organization, to “make the Vermont mountains play a larger part in the life of the people”. Our
section maintains the portion of the Long Trail from Harmon Hill to Glastenbury Mountain (see below on an
upcoming trail maintenance training opportunity). We also maintain the Bald Mountain trail and the West Ridge
trail (two side trails accessed in Bennington, that connect with the Long Trail), and our section is now branching
out into trail advocacy and maintenance throughout the greater Bennington area (see Robin Tarnas’s below). As we
grow in membership and energy, we hope to continue to find ways to support our mission throughout our area.

We are looking forward to a busy fall schedule of outings through our local area. Particularly note these two dates
coming up which will have more details to follow on Meetup.

1) Saturday October 5: Trail Maintenance Workshop
We will be hosting one of the two volunteer coordinators from the main GMC office (Lorne Currier and Kate
Songer) for a hands-on workshop along the Bennington Long Trail. This will be both for newbies hoping to learn
the basics, as well as those of us who have been going out for years to maintain our water bars, clip back
vegetation, remove blowdowns, and create brush-ins to keep hikers on the trails.
Want to know the difference between a Hazel hoe and a Rogue hoe and how to use them? Want to know the best
way to clip a trail side beech tree or hobble bush? Want to know why trail maintenance is 50% physics and 50%
psychology? If so, this is the workshop for you.

2) Saturday November 16: Bennington GMC Annual Fall Potluck
We’ll be gathering again at the Second Congregational Church in Bennington to share a meal, followed by a slide
presentation on multiple ongoing and completed trail projects along the southern Long Trail.

Everyone have a wonderful fall season, and thanks for being a part of the Bennington section! - Barb and Tim

The New Trolley Path
By Robin Tarnas

 On July 20, an introductory walk on a new trail in
Bennington was hosted by the Green Mountain Club.
 This brought 29 people and one dog along this
rejuvenated path for the first time. The trail roughly
follows the route of the old Bennington Trolley.

  Back in the late 1800s the most efficient way to get
around Bennington was the electric trolley. People could
live outside of town but still come in every day to work
in the mills. People could shop downtown and still
return to their rural homes, even without using a horse.
This was what allowed Bennington to grow further than
an easy walk from its commercial center.

When cars came into common use and roads got better, ridership of the trolley system fell off. After the great
flood of 1927, the damaged rails were not rebuilt, and the infrastructure was torn up. Only the raised bed
remained.

Over the past year, the members of the Bennington County Regional Commission, along with the Town of
Bennington, have been working to re-create this trail. This Trolley Path extends through the Headwaters Park. It
connects with a stretch of land acquired by the Vermont Agency of Transportation along Morgan Street, across
Middle Pownal Road, all the way through the Greenberg Conservation Reserve located on Route 7 South. Putting
this together, a new trail, 2.4 miles long, is now open for hikers, cyclists, and dog walkers.

At the same time there have been similar projects in Pittsfield, Lanesboro, Adams, North Adams,
and Williamstown, Massachusetts, as well as in Pownal, Vermont. This extended trail would roughly follow the old
Bennington-Berkshire trolley system path. Eventually, the dream is to create a Western New England Greenway
from New York City all the way to Montreal.


Leading a Trail Crew on the Long Trail System
By Matt Vezina

Some readers know that my current summer job is working for GMC leading a professional trail crew on the Long
Trail system. I get lots of questions about the work we do and the how/why behind it, so this article
might provide some answers.

The work of trail crews generally focuses on the most damaged sections of trail, the spots that are already bad and
will continue to get worse without further intervention. The project site I chose for this article is one example, let's
take a closer look. In the "before" photo, I am standing on a course of existing stepping stones installed decades
ago by a previous trail crew. These stepping stones were aligned to the left of the photo and meant to send hikers
along that same path. Unfortunately a large spruce tree fell on the trail and was cut off slightly short of the
existing tread. An understandable decision given the location in a wilderness area where chainsaws are not allowed,
but one that had significant long term implications. For years hikers have been moving to the right of the "before"
photo to avoid the fallen tree. This resulted in the original tread becoming overgrown with sapling trees, and a

Nature Notes: Staghorn Sumac
By Terri Armata

 Native to New England, Staghorn Sumac is plentiful in
Bennington and the surrounding area. A member of the
Anacardiacae or Cashew family, this shrub or small tree
sometimes grows up to 40 feet in height and can form
thickets unless managed. It propagates both by seeds
spread by birds, mammals or wind and root sprouting.
The flowers are attractive to bees, wasps and beetles, the
foliage is used by some moths, butterflies and beetles as

Bromley Tower Opens for Hikers
by GMC

Once again, hikers can climb an observation tower on the summit of Bromley Mountain in Peru, VT. This 40-foot-
tall steel observation tower stands on Bromley’s summit (3,260 ft.), offering sweeping views of Killington, Okemo,
Stratton, and Glastenbury mountains, and the surrounding areas.

The original wood tower, first built in 1962, was
dismantled and removed from the mountain in the
summer of 2012 for safety reasons. Soon after, the
Manchester Section of the Green Mountain Club
began efforts to gather resources to rebuild the
beloved tower. Years of planning, permitting, and
fundraising were required to complete the project.
Bromley Tower was a joint project as it shares the
summit with Bromley Mountain Ski Resort, is owned
by the Vermont Department of Forests, Parks, and
Recreation, and is managed by the Green Mountain
Club.

The new Bromley Mountain Observation Tower is
now open for hikers to enjoy. Hikers can access the
tower by hiking the Long Trail/Appalachian Trail north from Route 11/30 (6.0 miles round trip) or by taking the
chair lift from the base of Bromley Mountain Resort. (For uphill winter travel, please follow the guidelines of
Bromley Mountain Resort.)

Mr. Fabulous Returns to the Long Trail
By Lorna Cheriton

Derek Lugo (trail name “Mr Fabulous) returned to Bennington August 24, 2024 for a second attempt to hike
Vermont’s Long Trail. Derek, whose trail name has been shortened to “Fab,” hiked the 2200-mile Appalachian
Trail in 2012 and wrote about his experiences in the book The Unlikely Thru-Hiker. An urban-dwelling Black
man, who has lived in Queens, Brooklyn and Harlem, New York, he had no experience hiking. On the AT, his
fastidious bathing each day even in cold streams and his careful maintenance of his beard and beehive of
Dreadnought braided hair… earned him his trail name “Mr Fabulous,” from other hikers. He now wears a custom-
designed ring with the fluctuating elevations of the AT in miniature.

The butterflies in his stomach before he makes his second start to hike the Long Trail could be from the memory
of his first attempt. He got severely sick from drinking beaver pond water and had to leave the trail. Ann and Billy
Martin brought him to their home where he stayed and recovered over several days and learned his water filter had
a leak due to a faulty O-ring. He is hiking the LT now with fellow long-distance hiker and writer, Christine Reed,
who he met on-line and who wrote Alone in Wonderland about her hike around Mt Shasta.

Trip Report: Paddling on Sherman Reservoir
by Lorna Cheriton

Back when we were planning the Stepping Stones
spring and summer schedule, I realized I would love to
paddle on my birthday. So I offered the canoe and
kayak trip on July 30. Although we didn't see the
resident eagle, we saw 2 lovely white egrets. One flew
across the inlet as we approached and settled in the
reeds. Within a few minutes, the second egret flew
across to join its mate. Coming back to the launch, we
7 paddlers had a picnic lunch at a table in the shade.

For two of the people who joined us this was their first
time on a Green Mountain Club trip. They had found
the trip on Meetup. The water was chilly from release
of water by the power company from the bottom of
the upriver reservoir: I was the only one who swam
(and only for a short time).

We got back to Bennington in good time for my slightly painful birthday present: the cortisone injection in my
shoulder that I had scheduled three months ago for the first day I was eligible, July 30. When the doctor cautioned
that I should avoid over-use of that shoulder and also avoid swimming for two days, I was glad I had scheduled the
injection for after my paddling and swimming, not at 8am before our trip.
