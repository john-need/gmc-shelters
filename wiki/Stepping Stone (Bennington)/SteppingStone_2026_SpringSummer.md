Nature Notes: Garlic Mustard
by Terri Armata

 Garlic Mustard, (Alliaria Petiolata), is an invasive biennial,
brought into the US in the 1800’s by European Settlers who used
it as an herb and as a ground cover. Other names for it: Jack-by-
the Hedge, Poor Man’s Mustard, Garlic Root and Hedge Garlic.
It spreads rapidly and sprouts early in the spring blocking
sunlight from reaching the later blooming spring forest flowers.
The roots also release a chemical that inhibits the growth of
underground fungi that are part of the forest ecosystem. The
leaves and chemical signature of garlic mustard fools the West
Virginia White Butterfly, (an early flying species of special
concern in Vermont), into laying its eggs on the plant where the
caterpillars will die. Unfortunately in this country there are no
animals or insects to keep its growth under control. The easiest
way to control it is to pull it up by hand.

It can be eaten in salad, or blanched, in sauces. Garlic Mustard
Pesto is reported to be quite tasty.

Garlic Mustard Pesto:

Ingredients

11 cups lightly packed garlic mustard leaves and tips, loosely chopped
1/4 cup pine nuts
1 garlic clove
1/3 cup grated parmesan cheese
1 cup extra virgin olive oil
1/2 teaspoon salt
1/2 teaspoon sugar
2 squeezes lemon juice

Directions

Step 1 In a blender, grind the garlic, pine nuts and parmesan.
Step 2 Add the garlic mustard.
Step 3 While blending, pour in a steady stream of the olive oil for 1
minutes, or until smooth.
Step 4 Add salt, sugar, lemon juice and pulse until mixed.

6. It’s MUD SEASON! Spring is here and the mountains and trails
   are transitioning to mud season as winter continues to melt away.
   As the trails thaw, they are extra vulnerable to erosion and
   exposed vegetation is fragile without the protection of snow and
   ice. To help protect the trails, the Vermont Department of
   Forests, Parks, and Recreation (VTFPR) has begun issuing trail
   closures on state land, updated on the app “TrailFinder.” Find
   updated trail closures: https://www.trailfinder.info/trails?
   status=Closed&view=map Find everything you need to know
   about Mud Season in Vermont: https://
   www.greenmountainclub.org/hiking/mud-season

   Please do your part to help us protect the trails in mud season.
   Follow any trail and road closures, and stay up to date with
   closures as they’re issued via TrailFinder. If you encounter mud
   on open trails, please turn around or continue straight through, not around, the mud.

The Hungry Lion Bike Tour - Beautiful Cycling for a Good Cause
by Kate Follett

Saturday, September 26th. In addition to being a part of the Green
Mountain Club, I’m also a member of the Deerfield Valley Lions Club,
which covers the nearby towns of Wilmington, Dover, Whitingham,
Jacksonville, and Halifax, Vermont. Last year, some of our GMC
members came to the Lions Club to speak about our work, which both
clubs enjoyed greatly. There is another event that I feel has great
crossover appeal between the Lions and the GMC: the Hungry Lion
Bike Tour (HLBT)
On the last Saturday of every September, the Lions Club hosts an
extensive bicycle tour around the hills, river valleys, fields, and farms of
southern Vermont and northwestern Massachusetts. This year, 2026, will
be the 15th anniversary of the tour, and we invite all GMC members to
help us make it a big success.
The Routes
The HLBT is actually several tours that happen simultaneously. Our routes help accommodate riders and walkers
of all levels.
     •    Stroll: This short (2-mile) road walk completes a loop around scenic Town Hill and Lake Sadagwa in
          Whitingham.
     •    Scenic Walk: A longer (5.5 mile) walk carries you up and down several hills in Whitingham, with beautiful
          hilltop views.
     •    Easy Out: This short ride (5.5 miles) is great for kids and families.
     •    Gravel Grinder: For mountain and gravel-bike enthusiasts, this route travels about 20 miles through
          Vermont’s back roads. Prepare for possible dust and mud!
     •    Rowe Zipster: This 35-mile trip is great for a full morning’s ride, dipping into the northernmost part of
          Massachusetts.
     •    Farm and River: This longer trip (50 miles) carries you along the winding Deerfield River and some of its
          reservoirs.
     •    Big Wheel Flyer: This is our longest route, up to 75 miles, for cyclists looking for a serious workout.
The HLBT works with the Ride with GPS app to guide you through each turn. Every year, the routes are planned,
mapped, and reviewed by experienced cyclists to make sure you get the best ride. There are rest stops along each
route with extra water, snacks, and helpful volunteers, and there are sag wagons to support each tour in case
anything goes wrong. Some of the routes with especially hard climbs even have an “elevator:” helpful Lions
members with pickup trucks that can give you a lift over the tough bits.
Tour Day
The HLBT routes are staggered so that most riders return to the staging area at Town Hill in Whitingham in time
for lunch. The Lions provide a meal that includes award-winning barbecue, salads and sides, dessert, and Vermont
craft beer donated by Ratu’s in Wilmington, Vermont. There is also live music throughout the afternoon.
Information: The website has not been updated yet for 2026, but check back closer to the event time. https://
www.hungrylionbiketour.com/

Winter in Alaska
by Robin Tarnas

One of the advantages of having a son who lives in Alaska is that we get to experience this rugged state in all
seasons of the year. We have been there in June when one can read in the backyard without a light at midnight. We
have been there in September when the moose and the caribou carry their magnificent racks of antlers and the
grizzlies jiggle with their layers of stored fat that will sustain them through their hibernation. We have been there in
the dead of winter when office workers leave their jobs between 11 and 2 so they can catch what little sun there is.
This year, we went in March. This gave us more sunshine, but still let us experience the full brunt of winter.

The day before we landed in Alaska, it was -15°F. That night it snowed 14 inches. I woke up to the task of
shoveling. Fortunately, the view from the driveway was magnificent. Across the valley was Mt Redoubt and the
Alaska range of mountains that continues all the way to Denali. To the northeast was the Chugach range, and to
the south was a view of the Cook Inlet which extends 100 miles to the Gulf of Alaska and the Pacific Ocean.

With such excellent conditions, we had to get out on our skis.
Anchorage is a superb place to do this. Because winter lasts so
long, the city is built around activities in the snow. Parks contain
miles of trails that roll through the foothills. Trails extend from
downtown out to the suburbs. Even housing developments build
ski trails between the backyards so people can ski right from their
doorsteps. Many of these trails are lit with electric lights to make
them more accessible, even through the long, dark winter nights.

During our visit we cross country skied almost every day. The
trails took us through forests that were very unfamiliar to our eye.
Instead of the mixed deciduous woods of Vermont with a wide
variety of trees, Alaska is covered by the taiga. This is a biome
dominated by one species of stunted spruce trees and carpeted
underneath with mosses and lichen that can survive being
completely covered for half the year. When we would ski to the
top of an overlook, we would see that the taiga would extend as
far as the eye can see without variation until it hit the mountains.
That far north, the tree line is only at 2000 feet, so that the
mountains rose barren above that altitude. This exposed all the
bare rocks and craggy outcroppings on the slopes and made the                Skiing in the taiga with our son, Andrew
mountains look that much more forbidding.

One of the advantages of exploring this area in the winter is that the grizzlies are hibernating. During the summer
one should never walk outside of the city without a can of bear spray. In the winter, only one of the dangerous
denizens of Alaska wandered around Anchorage. The moose are everywhere. We have seen moose grazing beside
the trail. There have been moose curled up in the snow, completely oblivious to our passing. On one morning, a
moose trotted across the front yard, having no difficulty managing the deep snow. These huge animals have to be
treated with respect as they can be cranky and impulsive. They are inescapable when they run in a straight line, but
fortunately they forget about you quickly if you hide behind a tree.

The other activity that captures the imagination this time of year is dog sledding. We saw the ceremonial beginning
of the Iditarod. This was the beginning of the 1100 mile race across the snow from Anchorage to Nome, Alaska.

Ancestral Homelands: Hopi, Navajo and Chaco Canyon
by Julie Haupt and Tim Marr

At the end of March, we took a Road Scholar trip
to the Southwest. We stayed 2 nights each in
Flagstaff, AZ, Chinle, AZ (in the middle of the
Navajo Reservation), and Gallup, NM. Our group
of 24, plus 3 guides traveled in three vans through
a variety of ancient pueblos, Navajo and Hopi
sites. Highlights included Walpi Village, Canyon de
Chelly, and Chaco Canyon.

Walpi Village is a Hopi village established around
900 AD and one of the oldest continuously
inhabited villages in the US. It is a traditional
Hopi stone pueblo built on the First Mesa on the
Hopi Reservation. It is only accessible with a Hopi
Guide and no photography is allowed.

A Thru-Hike on the Long Trail
by Andrew Hall

In June 2024 I set off for a thru-hike of the Long Trail - my third since first completing the trail in 2018. My goal -
first the entire trail in 10 days. I figured 10 days would be a reasonably quick pace while still being able to fully enjoy
the trail experience. Here’s a brief day-by-day log of the trip, as well as a few photos. I hope you enjoy!

Day 1 - Northern Terminus to Tillitson Camp.

Back on the Long Trail! A tough start for the day after a
friend of mine who intended to hike the first few days with
me bailed. Oh well, nothing to do but keep going! I set my
first day up as a medium milage day to settle into pace, get
used to the elevation gain, etc. 26ish miles today. Weather
was iffy with bits of rain here and there, but nothing crazy.
Arrived at Tillitson around 6pm to a fairly packed shelter,
but thankfully there was a bit of space left! Hitting Johnson
tomorrow for a stashed resupply, aiming for a little past
that to end the day.

Day 2 - Tillitson to Bear Hollow

Very tired this morning, sort of felt like I was drifting in
and out of consciousness as I descended Belvidere. Better
by the time I hit Devils Gulch though. Big rain today, very
thankful to have the umbrella. Miles started to feel easier as
the day went on, and I was cruising by the time I hit
Johnson. Resupply stash in a nearby abandoned building
was undisturbed, very good. The road walk at the end of
the day was a bit tough, as I got hit by a torrential
downpour/thunderstorm while walking - again very thankful
for the umbrella! Plenty of room at Bear Hollow shelter.

Day 3 - Bear Hollow to Buchanon

Another day of mixed weather, with plenty of rain as I went up and over Mansfield. Figured I’d get the bad
weather bypass on the south side a go, but I swear it’s tougher than the regular trail! (at least when descending).
Cascades of water down slick, slick rock. After Mansfield the rest of the day moved pretty quickly, though I did
have a nasty fall just before the shelter. Distracted by a hiker with a bluetooth speaker! Oh well, beautiful trail today
and resupply tomorrow morning.

Day 4 - Buchanan to Ski patrol shack near Starks Nest

Long descent into the valley with a resupply drop alongside the Winooski river. Breakfast sandwich and energy
drink were much needed. Overall feeling fine after the fall yesterday. Long climb up to Camels Hump honestly
didn’t feel bad, and was up and over before I knew it. Decided to push a bit further today just a ways past App
Gap. Definitely rougher terrain in this section - extremely steep, loose, and rocky. A bit of knee pain at the end of
the day, but beautiful views from the Ski Patrol shack!!

Day 5 - Ski Lodge to Boyce Shelter

Out of the thick of it! I feel like Breadloaf marks the clear divide of where the Long Trail gets extra tough when
heading North - glad I’m going south! Rainy walk over Ellen and Abraham in the morning, then an absolute cruise
through Breadloaf. These miles feel so easy! Very isolated though, only saw two people all day! Shelter all to myself

powerful, life-changing experience. Finished in Bennington for a night at home.

Southern Arizona’s Trails: A Winter Hiking Escape in the Sonoran
Desert
by Ann Martin

Last January and February, we slipped away to the southwest for six weeks
of sunshine and exploration. Coming from Vermont where snow and well
below freezing days are the norm that time of year, we were pleasantly
reminded by how perfectly the desert suited hiking. Daytime highs hovered
in the comfortable 60s and low 70s, the air was dry and crisp, and many of
the trails were blissfully uncrowded. Towering saguaros stood like sentinels
against bright blue skies, and every canyon we wandered into felt like a
private oasis.

Southern Arizona’s hiking scene is remarkably diverse for such a compact
region. Within an hour’s drive of Tucson you can trek through classic
Sonoran Desert with barrel and ocotillo cacti, scramble up rocky canyons
lined with cottonwoods, or climb into cooler pine forests on the “sky
islands” of the Catalinas. The variety can keep you coming back day after
day.

One of our first outings, an old favorite, was the Sabino Canyon Recreation
Area on the north side of Tucson. One can catch the electric shuttle and
then hike the gradual 4.2-mile (one-way) trail through a dramatic canyon, or

hike both ways, or shuttle both ways. The path crosses Sabino Creek several times—easy rock-hopping in winter
when water is low but still flowing enough to cool your feet. The payoff is a series of tiered waterfalls and deep
pools tucked into the rock. There are other trails in the surrounding hills and for this visit we chose a steeper trail

A few days later we headed to Saguaro National Park’s Tucson Mountain District (the east side) for a walk on the
Douglas Springs trail. The hike starts in a sandy wash and climbs steadily up along a beautiful trail with many stone
steps, overlooks to waterfalls, and gorgeous rock formations. The variety of cacti kept us stopping along the way to
take photos and identify them. It reminded us that sometimes the best hikes are the ones that invite you to slow
down and look closely.

For first time visitors a drive or walk through the Saguaro National Park East is a must. There are numerous trails
throughout the park, but do take in the Cactus Drive which is a paved 10 mile loop through the park with many
trailheads along the way. The saguaro cacti are like snowflakes - no two are ever the same! Combine the cacti with
the panoramic views of the surrounding mountains and you have a winner.

We then wandered down to small towns south of Tucson and hiked trails in those areas. There are way too many
trails to mention, just know that you could spend the entire winter hiking if you chose to. We traveled onward then
to Big Bend National Park in Texas, but that’s another story.

Remember that even in winter the sun is intense—hat, sunscreen, and plenty of water are non-negotiable. The
delightful temperatures and blue sky make this area desirable for many people, so expect crowds in town and at
popular trailheads, but there are so many vast outdoor opportunities that you can quickly escape to places that
capture the desert solitude.
